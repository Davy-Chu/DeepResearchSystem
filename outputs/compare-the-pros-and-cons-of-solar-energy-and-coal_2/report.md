# Research Report

## Research Question

Compare the pros and cons of solar energy and coal generated energy, and make a conclusion on which energy source should be prioritized for Canada

## Summary

The supplied evidence generally favours solar over new coal generation because solar has much lower lifecycle greenhouse-gas emissions, uses a renewable fuel, and is often less expensive for new generation. Solar's disadvantages are variability, lower capacity factors, space requirements, high upfront costs, and possible needs for storage, backup, transmission and other grid integration. Coal offers dispatchable and relatively continuous generation, but depends on finite mined fuel and has substantially higher lifecycle emissions. For Canada, the evidence supports prioritizing additional clean generation over new unabated coal, with solar developed where regionally suitable and combined with firming resources. However, the evidence does not establish that solar should be Canada's principal or universally lowest-cost new resource; a Canada-specific model instead emphasizes wind, transmission and hydropower balancing.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Solar has a major environmental advantage over coal because its lifecycle greenhouse-gas emissions are substantially lower, although solar equipment still creates environmental impacts during manufacturing, transport, installation, mining and disposal.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Multiple sources directly compare lifecycle and operational impacts and consistently support solar's lower emissions while acknowledging nonzero impacts.

**Evidence**

- The supplied comparisons report approximately 20–50 g CO₂/kWh for solar PV versus about 820 g CO₂/kWh for coal, and characterize solar as roughly 20 times more environmentally friendly per kWh. [S1] [S2]
- Solar produces little operational pollution, but panel production, transport, maintenance and disposal still create emissions and waste. [S4]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

Coal's principal operational advantage is dispatchability: it can provide relatively continuous electricity, while solar output varies with daylight, weather and seasons.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The evidence directly and consistently establishes the difference in dispatchability and variability. Storage and grid measures qualify the disadvantage but do not eliminate the underlying difference.

**Evidence**

- The evidence describes coal as dispatchable or baseload generation and solar as daylight-dependent and variable, with cited capacity factors of roughly 50–80% for coal and 20–32% for solar; another comparison cites 66% for coal and 23% for solar. [S1] [S3] [S4] [S5]
- Canadian system modelling treats solar as non-dispatchable and indicates that storage, hydropower, flexible resources, transmission, grid integration and demand shifting can help balance variable output. [S8] [S11]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

Solar generally has lower apparent costs and no fuel cost for new generation, but a full comparison with coal depends on financing, subsidies, carbon pricing, capacity factors, storage, transmission, backup and other system-level assumptions.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The supplied sources support an apparent new-generation cost advantage for solar in several comparisons, but they do not provide a direct, authoritative solar-versus-coal Canadian full-system cost comparison. The Canadian model's lowest-cost pathway also emphasizes wind and transmission rather than establishing solar as Canada's lowest-cost option.

**Evidence**

- Several sources report that solar can be cheaper than new coal and has no fuel cost, while also noting substantial upfront investment and the complication of subsidies in comparing projects. [S1] [S3] [S4] [S5]
- The evidence warns that simple levelized-cost comparisons may omit reliability, capacity, asset lifespan, replacement costs and other long-term system values. [S7]
- The Canada-specific model evaluates generation together with storage, transmission, retirements and hourly dispatch, demonstrating that whole-system costs extend beyond generation-level LCOE. [S11]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

Solar uses a renewable energy input and can be installed in varied locations, including rooftops, parking lots and remote or ground-mounted sites, but it requires suitable space and substantial initial investment.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The supplied sources directly and consistently support both the renewable-resource characteristic and the deployment trade-offs.

**Evidence**

- The sources describe sunlight as renewable and not depleted by generation, in contrast to coal's finite mined fuel. [S1] [S2] [S5]
- Solar's deployment flexibility and its space and upfront-capital requirements are directly identified as advantages and disadvantages. [S4] [S5]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

Coal is disadvantaged by its dependence on finite fuel and continual combustion, while solar avoids fuel depletion during operation; however, coal's dispatchability can provide reliability that solar alone cannot.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The resource and reliability characteristics are directly supported by multiple sources, with no material disagreement about the basic trade-off.

**Evidence**

- Coal is described as non-renewable and dependent on mining and continual combustion, whereas solar generates electricity from sunlight over the panel lifetime. [S1] [S2] [S5]
- The evidence identifies coal's reliability advantage and states that solar may require backup generation or other firming resources to guarantee supply. [S1] [S4] [S5]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

For Canada, the evidence supports prioritizing expansion of clean generation, including regionally suitable solar, over new unabated coal, while pairing variable resources with firming and grid resources.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The evidence strongly supports prioritizing clean electricity and moving away from new unabated coal. It supports solar as part of that expansion, but the Canada-specific optimization does not identify solar as the principal lowest-cost resource and does not establish a single national technology choice.

**Evidence**

- The supplied Canadian evidence describes solar as feasible and environmentally and economically attractive over the long term, and recommends phasing out unabated fossil generation while accelerating solar and wind alongside hydropower, storage, grid integration and demand shifting. [S4] [S8]
- A Canada-specific model finds that renewable expansion, new transmission and hydropower balancing can achieve decarbonization at lowest modeled cost, and that an $80/tonne CO₂e carbon price renders most Canadian coal plants uneconomic. [S11]
- The Government of Canada identifies wind and solar as Canada's fastest-growing electricity sources and describes coal-fired generation as being phased out in Ontario and soon in Alberta. [S14]
- Canada is expected to require substantially more electricity generation as demand grows by 26% to 84% from 2023 to 2050 across the cited scenarios, while more than 80% of current electricity production is already non-emitting. [S6] [S8]

### Finding 7

**Claim**

Solar should not automatically be treated as Canada's sole or dominant priority, because the supplied Canadian modelling identifies wind, transmission and hydropower balancing as the principal lowest-cost decarbonization pathway rather than solar specifically.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The model result is directly reported, although it is model-dependent and does not show that solar is unsuitable or that coal is preferable.

**Evidence**

- The Canada-specific optimization found that new interprovincial transmission and substantial wind expansion, supported by hydropower balancing, produced the lowest modeled cost for electricity-sector emissions reductions. [S11]
- The evidence explicitly notes that the model does not establish solar as the principal lowest-cost Canadian expansion resource and that the appropriate balance among solar, wind, hydro, nuclear, storage, transmission and demand response remains unresolved. [S11] [S7]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Solar is often reported as cheaper than new coal at the project or generation level, but system-level evidence warns that storage, reliability, transmission, capacity factors and other requirements can change the comparison. The Canadian model does not provide a direct solar-versus-coal full-system cost result and emphasizes wind and transmission instead. [S1] [S3] [S4] [S7] [S11]
- Coal has a reliability and dispatchability advantage over solar alone. Storage, hydropower, flexible resources, transmission and demand management can address solar variability, but the supplied evidence does not quantify the required resources for Canadian provinces and territories. [S1] [S4] [S5] [S8] [S11]
- The evidence supports a clean-electricity transition and expansion of renewables, but it does not determine the appropriate national or regional balance among solar, wind, hydropower, nuclear, storage, transmission, demand response and other firming resources. [S7] [S8] [S11]

## Remaining Gaps

- A Canada-specific authoritative comparison of coal and solar lifecycle emissions, levelized costs, capacity factors and full system costs—including storage, transmission and backup requirements—is not supplied.
- The evidence does not establish how solar performs across Canadian provinces and territories, particularly during winter, low-sun periods and differing regional grid conditions.
- The current and prospective role of coal in Canada, including plant retirements, replacement needs, employment and energy-security considerations, is not quantified.
- Solar is not compared comprehensively with all other firm low-emission options available to Canada, so the complete optimal national electricity mix cannot be determined.
- The evidence does not provide province-by-province modelling results showing where solar would be selected, curtailed, storage-backed or displaced by other resources.
- The supplied evidence does not determine the appropriate regional balance among solar, hydropower, nuclear, storage, transmission, demand response and other firming resources.

## Conclusion

Canada should prioritize clean electricity expansion over new unabated coal. Solar is preferable to coal on the supplied evidence for lifecycle emissions, renewable fuel availability and generally favourable apparent new-generation economics, but it should be deployed as one component of a regionally tailored system rather than treated as Canada's universally optimal resource. Solar investment should be accompanied by firming and integration measures such as storage, hydropower balancing, transmission, flexible generation and demand shifting. Because the Canadian model identifies wind, transmission and hydropower balancing—not solar alone—as the principal lowest-cost pathway, the strongest evidence-based conclusion is to prioritize a diversified clean-electricity transition and exclude new unabated coal, while expanding solar where Canadian regional conditions and full-system costs support it.
