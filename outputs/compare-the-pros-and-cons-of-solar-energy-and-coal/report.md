# Research Report

## Research Question

Compare the pros and cons of solar energy and coal generated energy, and make a conclusion on which energy source should be prioritized for Canada

## Summary

The accumulated evidence supports prioritizing solar over coal for new Canadian electricity investment, but not treating solar as a one-for-one replacement for coal. Solar offers major advantages in renewable supply, lifecycle emissions, and likely long-term cost competitiveness. Coal’s principal advantage is dispatchability and relatively consistent output. Because solar is variable and affected by short Canadian winter days, it should be developed alongside storage, transmission, demand management, and dependable low-emission resources such as hydroelectricity and nuclear power.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Solar has substantially lower greenhouse-gas emissions than coal and produces no direct operational emissions.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The environmental advantage is consistently reported, including by a Canadian government source. However, the numerical lifecycle comparison from S1 is not Canada-specific.

**Evidence**

- Natural Resources Canada states that solar PV produces no GHG emissions during operation, has very few lifecycle emissions, and reduces GHG emissions by 96% compared with coal-generated electricity. [S3]
- A comparative source reports lifecycle emissions of approximately 820 g CO₂/kWh for coal versus approximately 20–50 g CO₂/kWh for solar PV, although these figures are presented in an Australia-focused context. [S1]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

Solar uses a renewable and effectively inexhaustible energy resource, while coal depends on a finite fuel supply and creates environmental impacts through extraction and combustion.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Multiple sources consistently establish the distinction between renewable solar energy and finite, polluting coal fuel.

**Evidence**

- NRCan describes solar as one of the most abundant renewable energy sources and identifies it as a resource for reducing Canadian GHG emissions. [S3]
- EnergySage contrasts sunlight’s replenishing supply with fossil-fuel extraction, transportation, land degradation, water pollution, and greenhouse-gas emissions. [S2]
- The comparative analysis describes coal as finite and solar as effectively inexhaustible. [S1]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

Solar’s economic position is increasingly favorable, but its cost advantage over coal cannot be assumed uniformly across Canada without accounting for system requirements.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The evidence supports solar’s improving economic competitiveness, but the retrieved material does not provide a Canada-wide, technology-specific comparison including storage, transmission, backup, and winter reliability costs.

**Evidence**

- NRCan reports that solar-panel costs have fallen substantially and describes solar as one of the cheapest new energy sources in Canada, second only to wind according to a quoted CanmetENERGY specialist. [S3]
- The C.D. Howe Institute reports that Canadian electricity costs are shaped by provincial system structures, infrastructure cost recovery, fuel-price volatility, transmission constraints, and policy choices. [S12]
- EnergySage notes that subsidies and comparison methods complicate direct cost comparisons, while Energy Matters reports favorable cost comparisons for renewables backed by storage in Australia. [S1] [S2]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

Coal’s main advantage is dispatchability and relatively consistent availability; solar’s main disadvantage is variable, daylight-dependent production.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The reliability trade-off is directly supported by multiple sources, with NRCan providing Canada-specific evidence.

**Evidence**

- EnergySage states that coal and other fossil fuels have an advantage in reliable application because they can provide near-constant availability. [S2]
- Energy Matters describes coal as dispatchable baseload and solar as variable and daylight-dependent, while cautioning that their efficiency measures use different inputs. [S1]
- NRCan states that Canadian solar cannot be produced on demand like thermal generation and that Canada’s vast geography and short winter days make flexibility necessary. [S3]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

Solar should be prioritized over coal for new Canadian investment, but within a diversified low-emission electricity system rather than as a stand-alone replacement.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The recommendation follows consistent Canadian evidence on coal’s decline, solar’s environmental benefits, and the need for reliability-supporting infrastructure and complementary resources. It does not claim that solar can replace coal on a one-for-one capacity basis.

**Evidence**

- Canada’s Energy Regulator reports that coal supplied 5% of Canadian electricity generation in 2021, concentrated mainly in Alberta, Saskatchewan, Nova Scotia, and New Brunswick, and that coal generation had fallen 66% nationally since 2005. [S14]
- The CER reports that Canada has already been replacing coal through combinations of renewables, natural gas, nuclear refurbishments, imports, biomass, and demand-management measures rather than solar alone. [S14]
- NRCan identifies solar as low-emission and increasingly affordable while emphasizing the need for flexibility because it is non-dispatchable and affected by winter conditions. [S3]
- Federal clean-electricity strategy material emphasizes clean, affordable, and reliable electricity, including grid expansion and demand management. [S10]
- The CER projects 2024–2030 renewable capacity additions of 7,833 MW of wind, 3,019 MW of solar, and 1,227 MW of hydroelectricity, indicating a regionally differentiated and diversified expansion. [S7]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

Canada’s existing electricity system is already predominantly renewable or otherwise non-emitting, so the policy question is primarily how solar should complement existing resources and help replace remaining coal generation.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Canadian government sources directly support the conclusion that Canada has a predominantly clean electricity mix and that solar remains a small but rapidly growing component.

**Evidence**

- The CER reports that renewables supplied 66% of Canadian electricity in 2023, including 58% hydroelectricity, 6% wind, and 1% solar. [S7]
- NRCan states that more than 80% of Canada’s electricity comes from renewable and other non-emitting sources, with wind and solar among the fastest-growing sources. [S9]
- The CER reports that solar generation rose from 0.1 TWh in 2010 to 4.9 TWh in 2023, but still represented only about 1% of national generation in 2023. [S7]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- The sources report different shares of renewable electricity in Canada: the CER reports 66% in 2023, while a U.S. government profile reports approximately 81% in 2024. The figures may use different definitions, years, or data treatments and should not be combined as if directly comparable. [S7] [S13]
- Coal’s 5% share in the CER’s 2021 data is not equivalent to the 20.5% fossil-fuel share reported by the U.S. government profile for 2023, because the latter includes fuels beyond coal. [S13] [S14]
- Evidence that solar is cheaper than coal is conditional. The sources do not establish that solar is cheaper in every Canadian province after including storage, transmission, backup generation, and winter capacity requirements. [S1] [S2] [S3] [S12]
- Some quantitative comparisons are drawn from Australia or global forecasts rather than Canada. They provide context but should not be treated as Canada-specific evidence. [S1] [S6]
- The evidence supports a high-level Canadian recommendation but does not identify the optimal province-by-province mix of solar, wind, hydroelectricity, nuclear power, storage, transmission, and demand response. [S3] [S7] [S8] [S10] [S12]

## Remaining Gaps

- Canada- and province-specific full-system cost comparisons between solar and coal, including storage, transmission, backup, and winter reliability requirements.
- The amount of additional solar that each province can integrate economically and reliably.
- The optimal combination of firm and flexible resources to maintain reliability as coal is retired.
- A harmonized explanation of the different renewable-generation shares reported for Canada by S7 and S13.
- More detailed evidence on employment, local air pollution, mining, land use, and community effects of expanding solar and retiring coal.

## Conclusion

Canada should prioritize solar over coal for new electricity investment and should continue the transition away from unabated coal. Solar is renewable, has substantially lower lifecycle emissions, produces no operational GHG emissions, and is increasingly cost-competitive. Coal’s dispatchability is a meaningful advantage, but its finite fuel supply and environmental impacts make it a poor priority for new generation, particularly given Canada’s stated direction toward cleaner electricity. The practical policy should therefore be a portfolio approach: expand solar where regional conditions justify it; pair it with storage, transmission, demand management, and other dependable low-emission resources; and avoid presenting solar as a complete one-for-one replacement for coal. This conclusion is well supported at a national, high-level scale, while detailed provincial investment decisions require further system-cost and reliability analysis.
