## Comprehensiveness

### R1

- Coverage: 0.50
- Depth: 0.50
- Rationale: The report recognizes that exposure and engagement are not polarization and identifies several relevant outcomes, but it offers limited conceptual definition and taxonomy.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The report distinguishes exposure from underlying polarization: “these observations alone do not prove that users’ underlying polarization” changed.
  - It separately names issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, political attitudes, partisanship, engagement, and account following.
  - The conclusion distinguishes “what users see, what they interact with, what they believe, affective hostility, or concrete political behavior.”
- Missing:
  - It does not clearly define political polarization as a construct or explain how ideological extremity, partisan identity, affective hostility, and behavioral polarization differ.
  - Political knowledge, media diversity, ideological exposure, engagement, and general activity are mentioned but not systematically distinguished from polarization.
  - It does not establish a clear operational distinction between polarization and related outcomes such as ideological segregation or congenial exposure.

### R8

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: The report provides a strong, appropriately qualified synthesis and explains major sources of disagreement. It is not fully comprehensive because statistical precision, power, and several methodological reasons for divergent findings are underdeveloped.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The conclusion separately characterizes “substantial causal influence over exposure,” “occasional and context-dependent influence over opinions,” and “no established general effect on long-term polarization or concrete political behavior.”
  - It explains disagreement through different constructs, platforms, interventions, content environments, time horizons, populations, and outcomes.
  - It preserves the distinction between congenial exposure and algorithmic selection and avoids claiming that all ranking algorithms uniformly increase polarization.
  - It identifies unresolved issues involving effect persistence, offline behavior, generalizability, content supply, prior beliefs, networks, and user choice.

### Novel Value

- The report’s central synthesis is that recommendation algorithms have substantial causal effects on political visibility and platform activity, but less consistent and generally smaller or more context-dependent effects on beliefs and polarization.
- It highlights the important distinction between congenial content itself increasing polarization and algorithmic selection adding an incremental effect, which is often weak or inconsistent.
- It separates selective opinion shifts on X from the absence of detectable changes in affective polarization or partisan identity, avoiding the assumption that any attitude change equals greater polarization.

## Citations

### Support

#### F1: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED

- Claim: Recommendation algorithms robustly change what users see and how they behave on platforms.
- Sources: S9, S10, S4, S5, S11, S13
- Rationale: The sources support substantial effects on users’ content exposure and some on-platform behavior, across experiments on Meta platforms and X. However, “robustly” is too broad: effects are platform- and outcome-specific, and the Meta experiment found no significant changes in key political attitudes or several behavioral/attitudinal outcomes.
- Supporting text: S9 reports that moving users from algorithmic to chronological feeds substantially changed time spent, activity, and content exposure. S11 and S13 report that switching from a chronological to an algorithmic X feed increased engagement, shifted political opinions, and led users to follow conservative activist accounts. Conversely, S9 states that these substantial experience changes did not significantly alter polarization, political knowledge, or other key attitudes during the study.

#### F2: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Ideological segregation and congenial exposure are well documented, but these observations alone do not prove that ranking algorithms caused users’ underlying polarization.
- Sources: S10, S6, S3, S7, S9
- Rationale: The saved sources document substantial ideological segregation, political bubbles, and users’ tendency to seek congenial information. They also support the claim’s causal caution: the Facebook/Instagram randomized feed experiment found that changing ranking substantially altered exposure and on-platform experience but did not significantly change issue or affective polarization during the three-month study. S10 further notes that researchers had little direct insight into the algorithms’ inner workings. S3 and S7 make stronger causal claims, but the overall cited set still directly supports the qualified formulation that segregation and congenial exposure alone do not establish that ranking algorithms caused underlying polarization.
- Supporting text: S9 reports that moving users from algorithmic to chronological feeds substantially changed exposure and activity, yet “did not significantly alter levels of issue polarization [or] affective polarization.” S10 says liberals and conservatives occupied distinct political news bubbles and that segregation increased from algorithmic selection to user interaction.

#### F3: SUPPORTED

- Claim: For Facebook and Instagram, changing feed ranking substantially changed exposure and on-platform activity but did not produce detectable short-run changes in political beliefs or polarization.
- Sources: S9, S10, S4, S5
- Rationale: The saved sources directly support all material parts of the claim. S9 reports that replacing the default algorithmic feeds with chronological feeds substantially reduced time and activity, changed exposure to political and other content, and did not significantly alter issue polarization or other key attitudes during the three-month study. S10, S4, and S5 independently summarize that changing the algorithms substantially changed what users saw and how they behaved, while producing no detectable changes in beliefs or polarization over the study period. The claim appropriately limits the finding to short-run effects.
- Supporting text: S9: Moving users out of algorithmic feeds “substantially decreased the time they spent on the platforms and their activity” and changed content exposure, while the chronological feed “did not significantly alter levels of issue polarization” or other key attitudes during the “3-month study period.”

#### F4: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Algorithmic feeds can sometimes change political opinions, but the observed effect is selective and does not necessarily amount to increased polarization.
- Sources: S11, S13
- Rationale: S11 directly reports that switching to an algorithmic feed shifted political opinions, with effects concentrated in specified policy and political-attitude measures. It also states that neither switching the algorithm on nor off significantly affected affective polarization or self-reported partisanship. S13 independently supports the opinion-shift and asymmetry/selectivity, though it does not mention polarization explicitly.
- Supporting text: S11: Switching to an algorithmic feed “shifted political opinion towards more conservative positions,” while “neither switching the algorithm on nor switching it off significantly affected affective polarization or self-reported partisanship.” S13: effects occurred for particular political issues, and there were no symmetrical effects when switching to a chronological feed.

#### F5: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Like-minded political content can increase polarization, but algorithmic selection does not consistently add to the effect of receiving congenial content.
- Sources: S14
- Rationale: The source directly reports that exposure to like-minded arguments increased both attitude and affective polarization more than exposure to opposing arguments, while algorithmic selection did not amplify these effects. It also notes a small algorithmic-selection effect for attitude polarization on one topic, supporting the claim’s qualification that algorithmic selection does not consistently add to the effect.
- Supporting text: The abstract states: “exposure to like-minded arguments increased participants’ attitude polarization and affective polarization,” but “these effects were not amplified by algorithmic selection.” It adds that, for one topic, algorithmically selected arguments produced “slightly stronger attitude polarization” than randomly selected arguments.

#### F6: SUPPORTED

- Claim: The best-supported synthesis is that algorithms are substantial mediators of political visibility and information flow, but their incremental effect on polarization is heterogeneous rather than uniformly polarization-increasing.
- Sources: S9, S11, S14
- Rationale: The cited sources collectively support both parts of the synthesis. S9 shows that changing feed-ranking algorithms substantially changed users’ time, activity, and exposure to political, untrustworthy, uncivil, moderate, and ideologically mixed content, while producing no significant change in several polarization measures. S11 reports that switching to an algorithmic feed increased engagement, shifted some political opinions conservatively, and promoted conservative content, but did not significantly affect affective polarization or self-reported partisanship. S14 finds that like-minded exposure increased polarization, yet algorithmic curation generally did not amplify those effects, although it produced a small direct effect on attitude polarization for one topic. Thus, the evidence supports substantial mediation of visibility/information flow and heterogeneous, non-uniform polarization effects.
- Supporting text: S9: “The chronological feed also affected exposure to content” and, despite these changes, “did not significantly alter levels of issue polarization” or affective polarization. S11: the algorithmic feed “increased engagement” and “promotes conservative content,” but did not significantly affect affective polarization or partisanship. S14: algorithmic curation “did not amplify” the polarization effects of like-minded exposure, but had “small direct effects on attitude polarization.”

#### F7: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED

- Claim: The literature reaches different conclusions because studies examine different causal pathways, platforms, interventions, time periods, populations, and outcomes.
- Sources: S6, S9, S14, S11
- Rationale: The sources support that findings differ across studies and that study designs examine different causal pathways, platforms, interventions, periods, populations, and outcomes. However, they do not establish that these differences are the reason the literature reaches different conclusions as a general causal explanation. The claim is therefore broader than the saved evidence.
- Supporting text: S14 says empirical evidence is “inconsistent at best” and attributes this partly to studies failing to separate exposure to like-minded content from algorithmic effects. S9 compares Facebook and Instagram algorithmic versus chronological feeds during the 2020 U.S. election over three months, while S11 reports a seven-week 2023 experiment on X with different political-attitude effects. S6 likewise describes studies of Facebook and Instagram, including a three-month chronological-feed intervention and a 2020-election sample.
