# Research Report

## Research Question

To what extent do social-media recommendation algorithms contribute to political polarization? Separate evidence that algorithms change what users see from evidence that they actually change political beliefs or behavior, and explain why the literature reaches different conclusions.

## Summary

The accumulated evidence supports a qualified conclusion. Recommendation algorithms clearly and substantially shape users’ information environments: they alter content visibility, ideological composition, exposure to political and untrustworthy material, engagement, and the accounts users follow. However, changing what users see does not uniformly change political beliefs or polarization. Facebook and Instagram experiments found major exposure and activity changes but no detectable short-run changes in issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, or related attitudes. A randomized X experiment found shifts toward more conservative positions on selected issues, but not significant changes in affective polarization or self-reported partisanship. Separate panel experiments found that exposure to like-minded arguments increased polarization, while algorithmic selection generally did not amplify that effect. The literature therefore suggests that algorithms are important mediators and facilitators of political information flows, but their incremental causal effect on polarization is heterogeneous, platform-dependent, and generally less consistent than broad claims about “echo chambers” imply.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Recommendation algorithms robustly change what users see and how they behave on platforms.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The strongest evidence comes from randomized or large-scale platform studies, including the primary Facebook study and the primary X-study abstract. These directly measure changes in feeds, exposure, engagement, and following behavior.

**Evidence**

- Replacing Facebook and Instagram’s algorithmic feeds with reverse-chronological feeds changed time spent, activity, exposure to political and untrustworthy content, exposure to moderate and ideologically mixed sources, and some uncivil content. [S9]
- Large-scale Facebook evidence found substantial ideological segregation in political-news exposure, with segregation increasing from algorithmic selection to user exposure and interaction; liberals and conservatives consumed political news from largely different sources. [S10]
- The reported Facebook and Instagram studies found that removing algorithmic ranking reduced time spent on those platforms, while disabling resharing reduced exposure to political news and untrustworthy sources. [S4] [S5]
- On X, the algorithmic feed increased engagement, promoted conservative content, reduced the visibility of traditional-media posts, and encouraged users to follow conservative political activist accounts. [S11] [S13]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

Ideological segregation and congenial exposure are well documented, but these observations alone do not prove that ranking algorithms caused users’ underlying polarization.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The exposure patterns are supported by large-scale observational evidence and by the platform studies. The causal attribution to algorithms alone remains limited because users’ prior beliefs, networks, follows, and choices also shape the available content.

**Evidence**

- A study of 208 million U.S. Facebook adults found limited overlap in political-news consumption between liberals and conservatives, with ideological segregation increasing across stages from selection to exposure to interaction. [S10]
- The average Facebook user reportedly received about half of visible content from people, pages, or groups sharing the user’s beliefs. [S6]
- Review-style sources describe feedback loops in which users select congenial accounts and engage with congenial content, after which algorithms learn from those behaviors and rank similar material. [S3] [S7]
- The Facebook study notes that algorithmic ranking operates on content generated by users’ existing connections and that user choice and network composition may independently shape exposure. [S9]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

For Facebook and Instagram, changing feed ranking substantially changed exposure and on-platform activity but did not produce detectable short-run changes in political beliefs or polarization.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The primary study directly reports the randomized intervention and null political-attitude results, while multiple independent reports summarize the same distinction.

**Evidence**

- The randomized Facebook and Instagram study found that chronological feeds did not significantly alter issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, or other key attitudes during the three-month study. [S9]
- Reporting on the same research states that changing the ranking system altered what users saw and how they behaved on the platforms without affecting measured beliefs during the study period. [S10]
- Changing to chronological feeds, disabling resharing, and reducing content from ideologically aligned accounts reportedly produced no significant changes in polarization, political attitudes, susceptibility to misinformation, or extremist views, despite changing exposure. [S4] [S5]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

Algorithmic feeds can sometimes change political opinions, but the observed effect is selective and does not necessarily amount to increased polarization.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The X result is reported in the primary study abstract and independently summarized by the researchers’ institution. It is randomized evidence of opinion change, but it concerns a specific platform, user population, period, and set of outcomes.

**Evidence**

- A randomized seven-week X field experiment found that switching from a chronological to an algorithmic feed increased engagement and shifted opinions toward more conservative positions, especially on policy priorities, views of investigations into Donald Trump, and the war in Ukraine. [S11] [S13]
- The X experiment found no significant effect on affective polarization or self-reported partisanship, despite the shift in selected political opinions. [S11]
- The reported mechanism involved greater exposure to conservative content, subsequent following of conservative activist accounts, and persistence of those follows after the algorithmic feed was removed. [S11] [S13]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

Like-minded political content can increase polarization, but algorithmic selection does not consistently add to the effect of receiving congenial content.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The study directly separates content congruence from algorithmic versus random selection, providing evidence about the incremental role of curation rather than merely observing partisan exposure.

**Evidence**

- Two panel-based experiments found that exposure to arguments matching participants’ prior attitudes increased both attitude polarization and affective polarization more than exposure to opposing arguments. [S14]
- The same experiments found that algorithmic selection generally did not amplify the effects of like-minded exposure, although algorithmically selected arguments produced a small additional attitude-polarization effect for one topic. [S14]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

The best-supported synthesis is that algorithms are substantial mediators of political visibility and information flow, but their incremental effect on polarization is heterogeneous rather than uniformly polarization-increasing.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

This conclusion integrates randomized evidence across Facebook, Instagram, X, and German experimental settings while distinguishing exposure, opinion change, polarization, and concrete behavior.

**Evidence**

- Facebook and Instagram experiments show strong exposure and activity effects but null short-run effects on measured attitudes and polarization. [S9]
- The X experiment shows a directional shift in selected political opinions but no significant change in affective polarization or partisanship. [S11]
- The German panel experiments show that congenial exposure increases polarization more consistently than algorithmic selection itself. [S14]
- The accumulated evidence does not establish effects on voting, donations, protest, or other concrete offline political behavior, nor on years-long cumulative polarization. [S9] [S11] [S14]

### Finding 7

**Claim**

The literature reaches different conclusions because studies examine different causal pathways, platforms, interventions, time periods, populations, and outcomes.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The sources directly document these design and context differences. They explain why apparently conflicting findings need not be treated as mutually exclusive.

**Evidence**

- Some studies describe or measure ideological segregation and congenial exposure, while others randomly alter feed ranking or the content-selection process and measure subsequent attitudes. [S6] [S9] [S14]
- The Facebook intervention changed ranking order and feed structure; the German experiments separately manipulated whether content was like-minded and whether selection was algorithmic; the X experiment compared algorithmic and chronological feeds on a different platform. [S9] [S11] [S14]
- The X findings suggest persistent effects through newly followed accounts, whereas the Facebook study emphasizes existing networks, user choice, and content generated by users’ connections. [S9] [S11]
- The Facebook study covered three months around the 2020 U.S. election, while the X study covered seven weeks and the German experiments used different topics and a different national setting. [S9] [S11] [S14]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Broad summaries characterize personalization, engagement optimization, and echo chambers as polarization-enhancing, while randomized Facebook and Instagram studies found that changing feed ranking altered exposure without changing short-run attitudes or polarization. [S1] [S2] [S3] [S9]
- Facebook and Instagram experiments found null short-run attitude effects, whereas the X experiment found a conservative shift in selected opinions. The results differ by platform, algorithm, treatment direction, content environment, time period, and outcome; the X study also did not find increased affective polarization or partisanship. [S9] [S11] [S13]
- The evidence that congenial content increases polarization is stronger than the evidence that algorithmic selection itself consistently magnifies that effect. One German experiment found a small additional algorithmic effect for one topic, but not a consistent effect across topics. [S14]
- The retrieved evidence does not isolate the relative contributions of ranking algorithms, content supply, prior beliefs, social networks, partisan media, and users’ active choices. [S3] [S7] [S9] [S11]
- The evidence does not establish whether the observed short-run effects persist for years or translate into voting, sharing, donations, protest, offline hostility, or other concrete political behavior. [S9] [S11] [S14]

## Remaining Gaps

- The full effect sizes, confidence intervals, treatment compliance, and subgroup results for the X experiment are not included in the accumulated material.
- The persistence of Facebook, Instagram, X, and German experimental effects beyond the study windows remains unresolved.
- It is unclear why the X algorithm shifted selected opinions while not significantly changing affective polarization or partisanship.
- The generalizability of these results to TikTok, YouTube, Instagram Reels, other countries, and other recommender objectives is not established.
- No retrieved study establishes a causal effect on concrete political behavior such as voting, donations, protest, or offline political hostility.

## Conclusion

Recommendation algorithms contribute substantially to the political information environment, but the evidence does not support a simple claim that they uniformly cause political polarization. They clearly influence visibility, ideological segregation, engagement, and sometimes account-following behavior. They can also change selected political opinions, as shown in the X experiment. Yet Facebook and Instagram ranking changes produced no detectable short-run changes in polarization or attitudes, and separate experiments suggest that exposure to like-minded content—not algorithmic selection by itself—is often the more important driver. The literature differs because “algorithmic polarization” can refer to different outcomes: what users see, what they interact with, what they believe, affective hostility, or concrete political behavior. It also differs across platforms, content environments, interventions, and time horizons. The defensible overall conclusion is therefore substantial causal influence over exposure, occasional and context-dependent influence over opinions, and no established general effect on long-term polarization or concrete political behavior in the evidence accumulated here.
