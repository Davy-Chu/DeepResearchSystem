# Research Report

## Research Question

To what extent do social-media recommendation algorithms contribute to political polarization? Separate evidence that algorithms change what users see from evidence that they actually change political beliefs or behavior, and explain why the literature reaches different conclusions.

## Summary

The evidence strongly supports that recommendation systems change users’ political-information environments: they alter ranking, prominence, sequencing, content mix, engagement, and sometimes which accounts users follow. Evidence that these systems change political beliefs or polarization is more conditional. Some experiments find short-term changes in affective polarization or selected policy attitudes, while others find no significant change in issue polarization, affective polarization, knowledge, or partisanship despite substantial exposure changes. The best-supported interpretation is that algorithms can structure and sometimes amplify pre-existing political tendencies, but they are not shown to be a uniform or primary cause of broad polarization. The supplied studies do not establish effects on consequential political behavior such as voting, turnout, protest, donations, or offline participation.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Recommendation algorithms clearly change what users see, even when content is not removed.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The evidence includes randomized feed interventions and direct analyses of resulting feed content and user behavior.

**Evidence**

- Platforms use behavioral and predicted-relevance signals to rank, filter, and recommend content, distinguishing personalized feeds from chronological feeds. [S4] [S7]
- Randomized interventions on Meta and X changed exposure to political, ideological, uncivil, untrustworthy, moderate, mixed, and traditional-media content. [S7] [S11]
- On X, algorithmic exposure promoted conservative content, demoted traditional-media posts, increased engagement, and increased following of conservative activist accounts. [S11]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

Changes in exposure can produce measurable changes in political attitudes or affect, but the effects are narrow and heterogeneous rather than a uniform increase in polarization.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Several experiments directly manipulate feeds or exposure, but their results differ by platform, content, intervention, duration, and outcome.

**Evidence**

- A randomized X-feed experiment found shifts toward more conservative policy priorities and selected political perceptions, along with increased following of conservative activist accounts, but no significant change in affective polarization or self-reported partisanship. [S11]
- A separate X reranking experiment reportedly changed feelings toward the opposing party by roughly two points on a 100-point scale after one week when exposure to partisan-animosity and antidemocratic content was increased or decreased. [S5] [S20]
- Experiments found that like-minded exposure increased attitude and affective polarization, but algorithmic selection generally did not amplify that effect; only one topic showed a small additional attitude-polarization effect. [S12]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

Large changes in users’ on-platform experience do not necessarily change polarization or political knowledge.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

This conclusion is based on a randomized platform intervention that directly compared exposure outcomes with political-attitude outcomes.

**Evidence**

- In the 2020 Facebook and Instagram experiment, replacing personalized ranking with chronological feeds substantially changed exposure and reduced time spent and activity, but did not significantly change issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, or related attitudes over three months. [S7]
- The experiment increased some exposure to political and untrustworthy content while also increasing exposure to moderate friends and ideologically mixed sources on Facebook, showing that ranking changes do not uniformly move exposure toward extremity. [S7]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

Algorithms are better characterized as structuring and sometimes amplifying existing political tendencies than as independently creating polarization from nothing.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The evidence supports both algorithm-mediated influence and the importance of prior preferences, social networks, and broader platform dynamics.

**Evidence**

- Users’ preferences, networks, interactions, and content choices supply signals to ranking systems, creating feedback between self-selection and algorithmic curation. [S1] [S4] [S7] [S13] [S17]
- Like-minded exposure can polarize users even when algorithmic selection adds little additional effect, while X findings show that algorithmic exposure can nevertheless redirect selected attitudes and following behavior. [S11] [S12]
- A scholarly review reports that cross-cutting interactions and diverse news exposure may be more common than the simple echo-chamber account suggests, including through weak ties such as coworkers, relatives, and acquaintances. [S21]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

The supplied evidence does not establish that recommendation algorithms change consequential political behavior.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The supplied studies define a clear boundary around measured outcomes, but they are not a comprehensive review of all behavioral research.

**Evidence**

- The available experiments measured attitudes, affect, knowledge, platform engagement, and account-following behavior, but did not demonstrate effects on voting, turnout, protest, donations, or comparable political action. [S7] [S11] [S12] [S17] [S20]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

The literature reaches different conclusions because studies estimate different causal effects rather than one common phenomenon called algorithmic polarization.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The sources directly document differences in interventions, platforms, time horizons, samples, and dependent variables, explaining much of the apparent disagreement.

**Evidence**

- Studies manipulate different treatments: ideological exposure, reranking existing posts, replacing personalized feeds with chronological feeds, or comparing algorithmically selected with randomly selected material. [S5] [S7] [S11] [S12]
- They measure different endpoints, including exposure, engagement, policy attitudes, political perceptions, affective polarization, issue polarization, partisanship, knowledge, and following behavior. [S5] [S7] [S11] [S12]
- Results also vary by platform, election context, country, duration, participant population, algorithm version, and whether ranking alone or the broader social-media environment is changed. [S5] [S6] [S7] [S11] [S12] [S17]
- Short experiments may detect immediate exposure or attitude changes but not cumulative effects, while user self-selection and other media make long-term causal attribution difficult. [S7] [S13] [S17]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Targeted X reranking studies report short-term changes in affective polarization, whereas the Meta experiment found substantial exposure changes without significant polarization effects and the X feed experiment found policy-attitude and following effects without significant changes in affective polarization or partisanship. [S5] [S7] [S11] [S20]
- Some sources emphasize recommender pathways toward extreme or antagonistic content, while other evidence shows that ranking changes can increase exposure in multiple directions, including toward moderate or ideologically mixed sources; another experiment found little general amplification from algorithmic selection. [S3] [S4] [S7] [S12]
- Broad claims that personalization or engagement optimization causes polarization are less methodologically specific than the randomized studies reporting null or outcome-specific effects. [S14] [S15] [S16] [S7] [S11] [S12]
- The research stopped at the iteration limit. Long-term effects, consequential political behavior, relative causal contributions, and generalizability remain unresolved rather than answered. [S7] [S11] [S12] [S17]

## Remaining Gaps

- Whether short-term changes in affect, policy attitudes, or account-following persist over months or years.
- Whether recommendation algorithms affect voting, turnout, protest, donations, or other consequential political participation.
- The relative contribution of algorithmic ranking compared with user self-selection, partisan identity, network composition, political elites, and other media.
- How findings generalize across platforms, countries, elections, algorithm versions, and less politically engaged users.
- Why particular X interventions produce effects absent in Meta experiments or in studies separating ideological exposure from algorithmic selection.
- Complete methodological details, attrition, compliance, subgroup effects, and uncertainty estimates are not available for every reported study.

## Conclusion

Recommendation algorithms make a meaningful contribution to polarization risk by determining which political content becomes prominent and by sometimes reinforcing or redirecting users’ existing tendencies. The evidence for downstream effects is real but conditional: some interventions change affective polarization or selected policy attitudes, while others produce no detectable change in polarization despite large exposure effects. Thus, the literature does not support the claim that algorithms uniformly or primarily cause political polarization. It supports a more qualified account in which algorithms are socio-technical amplifiers and organizers of political exposure, interacting with user preferences, networks, content supply, and broader media environments. On the supplied evidence, effects on consequential political behavior remain unestablished.
