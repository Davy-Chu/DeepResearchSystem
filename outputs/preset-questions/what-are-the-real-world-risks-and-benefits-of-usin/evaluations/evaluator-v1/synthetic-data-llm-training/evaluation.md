## Comprehensiveness

### R1

- Coverage: 0.50
- Depth: 0.50
- Rationale: The report recognizes that training mixtures and evaluation use matter, but it does not fully define the relevant synthetic-data settings requested by the rubric.
- Candidate evidence:
  - Finding 1 states that synthetic data is most useful “as a supplement to real data” and contrasts hybrid training with replacement.
  - Finding 3 distinguishes recursive or replacement-based training from additive use alongside real data.
  - The report discusses synthetic evaluation data separately from synthetic training data in Findings 5 and 6.
- Missing:
  - It does not clearly distinguish synthetic data generated by LLMs from programmatically generated data.
  - It does not explicitly compare pretraining with fine-tuning or explain how risks differ between them.
  - The pure-synthetic versus mixed synthetic–real distinction is present mainly as replacement versus additive use, without a systematic treatment of why the distinction matters across settings.

### R2

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: The report provides substantial, evidence-linked benefits and appropriately makes them conditional, but its treatment of the full range of data-quality benefits is not exhaustive.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The Summary identifies benefits including “scarcity, annotation cost, privacy constraints, class imbalance, and rare-case coverage.”
  - Finding 1 lists expensive annotation, privacy-sensitive domains, rare or edge cases, rapid prototyping, and domain adaptation as use cases.
  - Finding 2 reports that smaller datasets passing human-defined or model-driven quality checks performed better or comparably to larger unvalidated datasets.
  - Finding 1 cites hybrid real-plus-synthetic training and targeted task coverage as beneficial.
- Missing:
  - The report does not develop scale, diversity, or broader distributional coverage as distinct quality benefits in much detail.
  - It gives limited explanation of the mechanisms by which synthetic generation improves representation or generalization, beyond labels, rare cases, and targeted coverage.
  - Some benefits, such as privacy and cost, are stated more directly than demonstrated improvements in model quality.

### R3

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: The report covers several important failure modes and links them to degradation, diversity loss, and misleading evaluation, but some requested mechanisms and conditions remain underdeveloped.
- Candidate evidence:
  - Finding 2 reports “task-critical errors” in generated instructions and ground-truth API-call sequences.
  - Finding 4 reports lower diversity and reproduction of generator errors, artifacts, and biases.
  - Finding 3 explains that recursive or replacement-based training can narrow the distribution, lose low-probability valid examples, and cause severe perplexity degradation.
  - Finding 1 and the Conflicts section state that indiscriminate mixing can cause degradation and that diversity improvements do not consistently improve classification performance.
  - Finding 5 identifies contamination, leakage, benchmark saturation, and weak correspondence with complex real-world behavior.
- Missing:
  - Repetition and deduplication are not discussed explicitly as standalone failure modes.
  - Distribution mismatch and its effects on downstream accuracy, robustness, or out-of-distribution generalization receive only limited treatment.
  - The conditions affecting factual or semantic error rates—such as generator capability, domain difficulty, prompting, or verification—are not developed systematically.
  - The report mentions contamination mainly for evaluation rather than fully connecting training-data contamination to model behavior.

### R4

- Coverage: 0.25
- Depth: 0.25
- Rationale: The report recognizes imbalance as a possible target and is appropriately cautious, but it does not substantially assess how synthetic data could reduce bias or how to establish that it did so.
- Candidate evidence:
  - Finding 1 identifies class imbalance and rare-case coverage as potential uses of synthetic data.
  - The report says synthetic data can fill targeted gaps and notes that evidence on subgroup fairness is insufficient.
  - The Remaining Gaps section calls for controlled comparisons of subgroup and demographic fairness for synthetic-versus-real mixtures.
- Missing:
  - It does not explain concrete bias-reduction mechanisms such as counterfactual generation, fairness-aware generation, subgroup reweighting, targeted filtering, or balancing procedures.
  - It does not distinguish dataset balance from demonstrated improvement in model behavior in a developed analysis.
  - It does not specify the comparisons or fairness evidence needed to substantiate a bias-reduction claim beyond generally calling for controlled comparisons.
  - There is no positive empirical example showing that synthetic data reduced harmful bias.

### R5

- Coverage: 0.50
- Depth: 0.50
- Rationale: The report covers generator bias, artifacts, self-preference, and diversity loss, but its treatment of demographic bias and downstream model amplification is limited.
- Candidate evidence:
  - Finding 4 states that synthetic data can reproduce generator-specific errors, biases, and distribution artifacts.
  - It reports lower diversity in synthetic requirements data and says outputs can reproduce biases from the generator, prompts, or filtering process.
  - Finding 4 discusses generator–evaluator self-bias, including smaller models performing better on their own generated data.
  - The Summary and Conclusion identify biased examples, reduced diversity, and iterative distribution collapse as risks.
- Missing:
  - Demographic and representational bias are not analyzed concretely by subgroup, protected characteristic, or class.
  - The report does not explicitly discuss stereotypical associations, spurious correlations, or minority underrepresentation as distinct mechanisms.
  - Amplification through large-scale iterative training is implied by model collapse but not separately connected to demographic bias.
  - It does not clearly separate bias measured in the generated dataset from bias measured in the downstream trained model; it mainly notes that controlled evidence is missing.

### R6

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: It offers a good practical evaluation direction with multiple dimensions and real-data checks, but lacks a fully articulated complementary metric framework and explicit discussion of metric limitations.
- Candidate evidence:
  - Finding 2 uses human-defined and model-driven quality checks and reports downstream tool-use performance.
  - Finding 4 addresses diversity and task performance, including the fact that higher diversity did not consistently improve classification performance.
  - Finding 5 compares synthetic benchmarks with real-data behavior across six datasets and three NLP tasks, and reports task-dependent validity.
  - Finding 6 recommends independent real-world or human-authored data and evaluation of reliability, robustness, fairness, reproducibility, safety, realistic application settings, and complex behavior.
  - The report warns about evaluation overfitting, contamination, leakage, benchmark saturation, and generator–evaluator overlap.
- Missing:
  - Factuality, semantic correctness, label correctness, fidelity to the intended distribution, and coverage are not organized into a clear multi-dimensional evaluation protocol.
  - The limitations of statistical metrics, human review, model-based evaluators, and perplexity-style metrics are not explicitly compared.
  - Training utility is discussed through selected experiments, but generalization to held-out real data is not specified as a systematic requirement across training settings.
  - The report does not clearly warn that generator-aligned or synthetic-only tests can be insufficient in the evaluation-methodology terms requested, although it strongly implies this.

### R7

- Coverage: 0.50
- Depth: 0.50
- Rationale: The report identifies the need for subgroup comparisons and acknowledges the evidence gap, but it does not provide the detailed fairness-evaluation procedure required.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The report states that evidence on subgroup fairness is insufficient and calls for “controlled comparisons of subgroup and demographic fairness for synthetic-versus-real fine-tuning mixtures.”
  - Finding 6 recommends evaluating fairness rather than relying only on static benchmark scores.
  - The report recommends independent real-world or human-authored evaluation and notes that privacy, safety, and fairness outcomes remain unresolved.
- Missing:
  - It does not explicitly prescribe a real-data-only or no-synthetic baseline for fairness comparisons.
  - It does not specify subgroup-sensitive metrics such as subgroup performance, error rates, representation, calibration, or disparity measures.
  - It does not directly warn that dataset balance or balanced prompts are insufficient evidence of fairness improvement.
  - The evaluation recommendations remain broad rather than giving a concrete experimental design for measuring representational and fairness effects.

### R8

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: The report reaches a well-supported conditional conclusion and gives sensible safeguards, but it does not fully operationalize all of the rubric’s requested conditioning factors.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The Summary says benefits are conditional on generation, validation, and mixture design.
  - Finding 1 recommends targeted supplementation rather than universal replacement.
  - Finding 3 recommends retaining real data and describes recursive replacement as risky.
  - Finding 6 recommends independent validation and real-world evaluation.
  - The Conclusion identifies quality-controlled, provenance-aware, additive use alongside real data as the defensible pattern and says synthetic data should be treated as a conditional instrument rather than an automatic substitute.
  - The Conflicts and Uncertainty section acknowledges domain-, task-, curation-, and configuration-dependent findings, unresolved fairness and deployment evidence, and conflicting results on diversity and utility.
- Missing:
  - Generator and target-model capability are not treated as explicit decision factors.
  - Synthetic-to-real ratio, prompting, training regime, and filtering thresholds are mentioned only indirectly or in the Remaining Gaps rather than integrated into the conclusion’s decision criteria.
  - Deployment safeguards are high-level; the report does not give concrete go/no-go thresholds or operational acceptance criteria.
  - The conclusion is conditional and appropriately uncertain, but some causal conditions are listed as unresolved rather than synthesized into a more actionable framework.

### Novel Value

- The report synthesizes task-dependent evidence rather than offering a universal verdict, especially in the contrast between selected gains and harmful indiscriminate mixing.
- It identifies a useful distinction between diversity metrics and downstream utility, noting that increased diversity did not consistently improve classification performance.
- It connects synthetic benchmark validity to generator–evaluator self-bias and task complexity, including the contrast between intent classification and named-entity recognition.
- It explicitly flags unresolved evidence on demographic fairness, out-of-distribution behavior, calibration, hallucination, safety, and privacy leakage rather than overstating conclusions.

## Citations

### Support

#### F1: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Synthetic data is most useful as a supplement to real data for targeted tasks, rather than as a universal replacement.
- Sources: S1, S4, S13
- Rationale: The sources support both key parts of the claim: synthetic data is useful for specific, task-oriented applications, and combining it with real data is generally preferable to replacing real data entirely. S1 explicitly states an “accumulate, don’t replace” rule and describes distinct use cases with different risks. S4 lists targeted applications and frames synthetic data as something that can supplement or, in some cases, substitute for authentic data. S13 reports that hybrid real-plus-synthetic training produced the best results in its requirements-classification study, while indiscriminate combination could hurt performance.
- Supporting text: S1: “The fix is not avoiding synthetic data, it's accumulating real data alongside it rather than replacing it”; it also identifies four distinct use cases. S4: synthetic data can “augment existing datasets” and address privacy, rare cases, and data scarcity. S13: “The best results were obtained through hybrid training strategies that combined real and synthetic data,” though indiscriminate combinations sometimes degraded performance.

#### F2: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Data quality and validation matter more than synthetic-data volume alone; validated synthetic data can outperform larger unvalidated datasets.
- Sources: S14, S13
- Rationale: S14 directly supports the claim: its experiments found that models trained on high-quality, validated data outperformed models trained on unvalidated data even when using a smaller dataset. S13 independently emphasizes prioritizing data quality and consistent annotations over marginal modeling improvements, though it does not directly compare validated and larger unvalidated datasets. Together, the sources support the claim, with the strongest direct evidence coming from S14.
- Supporting text: S14 reports that “models trained on high-quality data outperform those trained on unvalidated data, even when trained with a smaller quantity of data” and that a “smaller high-quality training dataset is more effective than a larger unvalidated set.” S13 states that prioritizing data quality and consistent, high-quality annotations is essential for reliable AI4RE systems.

#### F3: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Recursive or replacement-based training on model-generated data can narrow the data distribution and cause model collapse, whereas additive use with retained real data is presented as safer.
- Sources: S1, S2
- Rationale: S1 directly supports both components: it describes successive training on model outputs as losing the tails of the original distribution and identifies replacement of real data as producing unbounded error, while additive accumulation alongside real data has bounded error and low risk. S2 independently supports the recursive-training/model-collapse portion. The sources present additive use with retained real data as the safer regime.
- Supporting text: S1: “successive generations ... progressively lose the tails of the original data distribution”; “test error ... grows without bound when [synthetic data] replaces real data,” whereas accumulating synthetic data alongside real data gives a “finite upper bound.” S2: “Recursive training on synthetic data causes model collapse, where distributions narrow until outputs degrade.”

#### F4: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED

- Claim: Synthetic data can reproduce generator-specific errors, biases, and distribution artifacts; current evidence is stronger for reduced diversity and self-bias than for demographic fairness effects.
- Sources: S13, S11, S12, S1, S4, S5
- Rationale: The sources support narrower portions of the claim: S13 reports that synthetic requirements exhibited lower diversity than real data, and S11/S12 report generator-specific self-bias, especially in smaller LLMs. S1 and S5 describe distribution degradation, model-collapse risks, compounding errors, and biased or duplicated synthetic content. However, the supplied snapshots do not provide direct evidence about demographic fairness effects, nor do they explicitly establish that evidence for reduced diversity and self-bias is stronger than evidence for demographic fairness effects. S4 mainly gives general background and does not substantiate the comparative evidence assessment.
- Supporting text: S13: the synthetic data “exhibited lower diversity than real data.” S11: “smaller LLMs exhibit biases towards their own generated data,” while larger models show little or no bias. S5: repeated training on model-generated content can cause capabilities to “stagnate or degrade,” and synthetic data introduces risks when it lacks novelty or reliable external feedback.

#### F5: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED

- Claim: Synthetic evaluation sets can be useful for simple or targeted tasks, but their validity declines for more complex tasks and can produce misleading model comparisons.
- Sources: S11, S12, S1, S5
- Rationale: S11 and S12 directly support the claim that synthetic benchmarks are effective for simpler tasks and less reliable for complex tasks. S11 specifically evaluates whether relative rankings are preserved between synthetic and real data, and notes bias when the same model generates and solves the task, which supports concern about misleading comparisons. However, the saved excerpts do not explicitly state that synthetic evaluation sets produce misleading model comparisons; they describe ranking changes and generator/model bias rather than using that exact conclusion. S1 and S5 provide only general, indirect discussion of synthetic-data or benchmark risks and do not materially strengthen this specific claim.
- Supporting text: S11 says synthetic data can capture performance for simpler tasks such as intent classification but falls short for complex tasks such as named entity recognition; it evaluates changes in relative method rankings between synthetic and real datasets and reports self-generated-data bias in smaller models. S12 similarly says synthetic benchmarks are highly effective for intent detection but have diminishing reliability for complex NER scenarios.

#### F6: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED

- Claim: Evaluation should combine synthetic tests with independent real-world or human-authored data and assess more than aggregate benchmark scores.
- Sources: S4, S5, S11, S12
- Rationale: The sources support using synthetic evaluation alongside real-data comparisons and assessing dimensions beyond aggregate benchmark scores. S5 explicitly says benchmark scores alone are insufficient and that evaluation should reflect realistic, reliable, safe, and robust operation. S11 directly compares synthetic and real benchmarks using both absolute performance and relative method rankings, and identifies bias from using the same LLM for generation and evaluation. However, the sources do not clearly prescribe that the real-world or human-authored data must be independent, nor do they establish a general requirement to combine both data types in every evaluation.
- Supporting text: S5: “Determining whether an LLM is truly stronger is not as simple as looking at a few benchmark scores” and models may perform well on standard benchmarks while behaving unreliably in complex real-world tasks. S11: the study compares performance on “real versus synthetic datasets” using absolute performance and relative rankings, while examining generator/solver bias. S4 describes synthetic evaluation datasets for edge cases and diverse scenarios.

#### F7: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Synthetic data may reduce privacy and sharing barriers, but generating synthetic records does not by itself establish privacy protection.
- Sources: S4, S1, S2
- Rationale: S4 directly supports the claim’s two important points: synthetic data can help address privacy-sensitive data constraints and facilitate sharing by removing direct links to individual records, while also stating that synthetic data quality and utility depend on validation. S1 and S2 independently describe privacy substitution or privacy constraints as a use case, but do not undermine the qualification that generation alone is insufficient to establish privacy protection. The sources do not explicitly state the exact phrase “does not by itself establish privacy protection,” so that portion is supported as a reasonable qualification rather than verbatim wording.
- Supporting text: S4 says synthetic data is valuable for “privacy-sensitive information” and describes “Privacy-Preserving Data Sharing,” where generated data can remove direct links to individual records. It also says quality and utility depend critically on “validation methods.” S1 identifies “privacy substitution” as a distinct use case and warns of “GDPR pseudonymization risk”; S2 says synthetic data can address privacy constraints but warns that ungoverned synthetic data creates audit gaps.
