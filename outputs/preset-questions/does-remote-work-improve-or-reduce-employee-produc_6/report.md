# Does Remote Work Improve or Reduce Employee Productivity?

## Executive summary

The evidence does not support a single answer. Remote work can increase productivity for some jobs and employees, but fully remote arrangements can also reduce output when work is highly interdependent, coordination-intensive, or dependent on rapid learning. The most defensible conclusion is:

> **Remote work tends to improve individual productivity in focused, measurable, autonomy-friendly jobs, while it can reduce team-level productivity, innovation, and learning when coordination and knowledge sharing are important. Hybrid work often captures much of the productivity benefit while limiting the costs.**

Different studies conflict largely because they examine different forms of remote work, workers, tasks, outcomes, and periods:

- **Controlled experiments** generally find positive or neutral effects, especially when remote work is voluntary and well-supported.
- **Observational studies** often find weaker, heterogeneous, or negative effects because they include selection effects, disruptions, inexperienced remote workers, and team-level coordination costs.
- **Company-level data** are especially mixed: some firms report gains from remote work, while others observe slower innovation, weaker collaboration, or lower output after moving to fully remote work.

The answer depends less on “remote versus office” in the abstract than on **task structure, work design, management quality, employee preferences, and whether the relevant outcome is individual output or organizational performance**.

---

## 1. What does “productivity” mean?

Productivity is not one outcome. Studies measure several different things:

1. **Individual output**
   - Calls handled
   - Code written
   - Cases processed
   - Sales completed
   - Tasks or applications reviewed

2. **Quality**
   - Error rates
   - Customer satisfaction
   - Rework
   - Defect rates

3. **Efficiency**
   - Output per hour
   - Time per task
   - Revenue per employee

4. **Team and organizational outcomes**
   - Project completion
   - Innovation
   - Knowledge sharing
   - Product launches
   - Employee retention
   - Promotion and development

Remote work may improve one outcome while harming another. For example, an employee may complete more individually measurable tasks at home while the organization generates fewer new ideas or takes longer to coordinate complex projects.

A major source of disagreement is therefore that one study measures **individual throughput**, while another measures **team innovation or long-term firm performance**.

# 2. Evidence from controlled experiments

Controlled experiments provide the strongest evidence about causal effects because they can compare similar workers under different work arrangements.

## 2.1 The Ctrip experiment: a positive effect from working at home

The best-known study is Bloom et al. (2015), which studied employees of Ctrip, a large Chinese travel agency.

### Design

- About 1,000 call-center employees volunteered for the study.
- Roughly half were randomly assigned to work from home for nine months.
- The control group remained in the office.
- Participants worked in relatively standardized, individually measurable jobs.

### Findings

The work-from-home group:

- Increased performance by approximately **13%**, based on calls handled per minute and hours worked.
- Had lower attrition.
- Reported greater job satisfaction.
- Used fewer sick days.
- Experienced fewer interruptions and benefited from a quieter work environment.

The productivity increase came from both:

- More calls completed per minute, and
- More time spent working because employees took fewer breaks and sick days.

### Important qualifications

The experiment does not show that remote work universally raises productivity.

First, these were call-center jobs with:

- Clearly measurable outputs
- Limited need for face-to-face collaboration
- Established procedures
- Relatively autonomous work

Second, participants volunteered for the experiment. They may have been unusually suited to home working. Third, the study examined a structured home-work arrangement with equipment and management support—not the improvised pandemic model used by many firms.

## 2.2 The later Ctrip “choice” experiment: hybrid work and retention

Bloom and colleagues later conducted a second experiment at Ctrip in which employees were allowed to choose whether to work from home or the office on particular days after a randomized trial period.

The study found that hybrid work:

- Reduced employee attrition substantially—roughly by one-third.
- Improved job satisfaction.
- Did not reduce performance.
- Increased the rate at which employees accepted the opportunity to work from home.

This result is important because it suggests that the most effective arrangement may not be fully remote work. Employees often value flexibility, but organizations may retain performance by combining remote work with office-based interaction.

**Source:** Bloom et al., “Hybrid working from home improves retention without damaging performance,” *Nature* (2024).

## 2.3 Microsoft India randomized hybrid-work experiment

A large randomized experiment involving Microsoft employees in India compared different work schedules, including fully office-based, fully remote, and hybrid arrangements.

The study found that:

- Hybrid work did not materially reduce individual productivity.
- Employees working remotely or in hybrid arrangements had fewer opportunities for informal communication and cross-team interaction.
- Communication networks became more siloed, especially across organizational boundaries.

This is a useful distinction: **individual productivity can remain stable while collaboration patterns deteriorate**.

**Source:** Choudhury et al., “Work-from-anywhere: The productivity effects of geographic flexibility,” and related Microsoft research on hybrid work and communication networks.

## 2.4 What experiments generally imply

Controlled evidence tends to show:

- **Positive effects** for routine, individually measurable work.
- **Neutral effects** for many knowledge-work tasks when employees and managers adapt.
- **Potential collaboration costs** even when individual output does not fall.
- **Benefits from hybrid work**, particularly through improved retention and employee satisfaction.

However, experiments have limitations:

- They often cover one firm or occupation.
- Participation is frequently voluntary.
- Treatment periods may be too short to capture long-term innovation or career effects.
- Productivity is easier to measure in call centers than in research, management, design, or team-based work.

# 3. Evidence from observational studies

Observational research uses real-world data rather than random assignment. It covers more occupations and organizations but is more vulnerable to confounding factors.

## 3.1 Studies finding productivity gains

### U.S. Patent and Trademark Office

Research on patent examiners at the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office found that greater work-from-home flexibility was associated with higher productivity, without a corresponding decline in quality.

Patent examiners are a favorable context for remote work:

- Work is highly individual
- Output is measurable
- Employees have substantial autonomy
- Digital tools support the work
- Tasks require concentration more than physical co-presence

This study suggests that remote work can be particularly effective when workers are experienced and performance can be monitored through objective output.

**Source:** Choudhury, Foroughi, and Larson, “Work-from-anywhere: The productivity effects of geographic flexibility,” *Strategic Management Journal* (2021).

### Call-center and customer-service studies

Several observational studies of customer-service and back-office work report stable or improved productivity after remote-work adoption, especially when firms improve digital monitoring, training, and workflow systems.

These results are consistent with the Ctrip experiment, but they may partly reflect firms allowing remote work selectively to employees who have already demonstrated strong performance.

## 3.2 Studies finding productivity declines

### Pandemic-era evidence

A large body of research during 2020–2021 found apparent productivity declines or longer working hours despite remote work. For example, studies using email, calendar, collaboration, and project data frequently found:

- More meetings
- Longer working days
- More fragmented work
- Less uninterrupted focus
- More within-team communication but less cross-team communication
- Slower coordination

Some studies interpreted these changes as evidence of lower productivity. But the interpretation is difficult because pandemic work-from-home was not a normal remote-work treatment. Employees faced:

- School and childcare closures
- Health concerns
- Economic uncertainty
- Sudden relocation to home offices
- Inadequate equipment
- No preparation or training
- Simultaneous remote work by colleagues, clients, and managers

Thus, pandemic studies often measure the effect of **remote work under a major social crisis**, not the effect of a well-designed remote-work policy.

### Real-estate and technology firms

Research involving large technology and financial firms has sometimes found that remote work reduced productivity or slowed the completion of projects. These effects tend to be strongest when:

- Teams depend heavily on one another
- Employees are junior
- Work requires frequent feedback
- Tasks are difficult to specify in advance
- Innovation and problem-solving matter more than routine throughput

A widely cited study of programmers at a large Indian technology company found that working from home reduced productivity relative to office work, particularly for employees whose jobs required extensive coordination. The authors emphasized that home working reduced interruptions for some tasks but made collaboration more difficult.

## 3.3 The Microsoft communication study

Research using Microsoft data during the pandemic found that remote work caused employees’ collaboration networks to become more internally focused:

- Fewer cross-group ties
- More communication within existing teams
- Less spontaneous information exchange
- More asynchronous communication

This does not prove that output fell. But it identifies a mechanism through which long-term remote work could reduce:

- Innovation
- Organizational learning
- Integration of new employees
- Awareness of opportunities outside one’s immediate team

**Source:** Yang et al., “The effects of remote work on collaboration among information workers,” *Nature Human Behaviour* (2022).

## 3.4 Studies of working hours and productivity

Many remote employees work longer hours. That can mean either:

- Higher output, or
- A productivity problem masked by extra labor input.

A firm might maintain output only because employees work substantially more hours. In that case, output per employee could be stable while output per hour falls.

This is one reason studies that use “tasks completed” can reach different conclusions from studies that calculate output per hour or examine employee well-being.

# 4. Evidence from company-level data

Company-level evidence is useful because it captures organizational outcomes, but it is harder to interpret causally. Firms that adopt remote work differ systematically from firms that do not.

## 4.1 Firms reporting gains

Companies that report productivity improvements often have some combination of:

- Digitally delivered products
- High employee autonomy
- Strong documentation
- Mature collaboration software
- Output-based management
- Geographically distributed teams
- Low dependence on physical equipment
- Employees who are experienced and self-select into remote work

Examples include many software, professional-services, and customer-support firms. Remote work can reduce:

- Office costs
- Commuting-related fatigue
- Absenteeism
- Geographic hiring constraints
- Employee turnover

These cost and retention effects can raise total firm performance even if measured individual productivity is unchanged.

## 4.2 Firms reporting losses

Other firms have reported difficulties with fully remote work, particularly in:

- Product development
- Design
- Media production
- Financial trading and deal-making
- Apprenticeship-heavy professions
- Research and development
- Complex project management

The problems commonly reported are:

- Slower decisions
- More meetings
- Weaker mentoring
- Reduced spontaneous problem solving
- Loss of organizational culture
- Difficulty onboarding new employees
- More difficulty detecting underperformance
- Less cross-functional innovation

Some of these are productivity effects; others are management and culture effects that may appear only over a longer period.

## 4.3 Large-scale labor-market evidence

Firm surveys conducted after the pandemic generally find that employers view remote work as:

- Positive or neutral for employee productivity in many jobs
- Valuable for recruiting and retention
- More difficult for creativity, collaboration, and training
- More suitable for experienced workers than new hires

Surveys are informative but should be treated cautiously. Managers may infer productivity from visibility, hours, or responsiveness rather than actual output. Employees may also overestimate their own productivity.

# 5. Why do studies reach conflicting conclusions?

## 5.1 Different types of remote work

“Remote work” can mean:

- One or two days per week at home
- Fully remote work
- Work from anywhere
- Emergency work from home
- Individual remote work within an office-based team
- A fully distributed organization

These arrangements are not equivalent. Hybrid work may preserve office-based coordination while providing concentration and flexibility. Fully remote work creates larger risks for communication, onboarding, and social integration.

## 5.2 Different occupations and tasks

Remote work is more likely to improve productivity when work is:

- Individually performed
- Digitally delivered
- Easy to measure
- Repetitive or structured
- Concentration-intensive
- Low in physical or social interdependence

It is more likely to reduce productivity when work requires:

- Tacit knowledge exchange
- Apprenticeship
- Rapid feedback
- High coordination
- Creative brainstorming
- Cross-functional problem solving
- Access to specialized physical equipment

A call-center agent, software engineer, patent examiner, surgeon, and product-design team should not be expected to respond identically.

## 5.3 Selection effects

Employees who choose remote work may differ from those who prefer the office:

- They may be more disciplined.
- They may have better home environments.
- They may be more experienced.
- They may have fewer caregiving constraints.
- They may work in roles more suitable for remote work.

Likewise, firms that successfully adopt remote work may already have better technology and management practices.

Randomized experiments reduce selection bias; observational company comparisons generally do not.

## 5.4 Individual versus team productivity

An employee can be individually productive while making the team less productive if:

- Information is not shared
- Responses are delayed
- Work becomes duplicated
- Dependencies are poorly coordinated
- New employees cannot learn informally

Conversely, office workers may look busy and responsive while producing less individual output because of interruptions and commuting.

The level of analysis matters:

| Level | Possible remote-work effect |
|---|---|
| Individual focused work | Positive |
| Routine measurable output | Positive or neutral |
| Team coordination | Neutral to negative |
| Cross-team knowledge sharing | Often negative |
| Retention and recruitment | Positive |
| Innovation and onboarding | Uncertain, often negative for fully remote work |
| Office and commuting costs | Positive for employers or employees, depending on arrangements |

## 5.5 Short-term versus long-term effects

Remote work may initially improve productivity because employees gain:

- More focus
- Less commuting
- Greater schedule control

Over time, however, firms may experience:

- Weaker social ties
- Reduced mentoring
- Slower promotion of remote workers
- Less transfer of tacit knowledge
- Lower innovation
- Greater coordination costs

Some costs appear only after months or years, making short experiments insufficient to capture them.

## 5.6 Management adaptation

Remote work requires different management practices:

- Clear goals
- Output-based evaluation
- Written documentation
- Deliberate communication norms
- Effective onboarding
- Structured team meetings
- Appropriate use of synchronous and asynchronous work
- Regular opportunities for relationship-building

Organizations that simply move office routines online may see productivity decline. Organizations that redesign processes may see productivity gains.

## 5.7 Employee preferences and well-being

Productivity depends partly on whether the arrangement fits the employee.

Remote work can reduce productivity when employees face:

- Poor workspace
- Care responsibilities
- Isolation
- Weak boundaries between work and home
- Low motivation
- Unreliable internet or equipment

It can improve productivity through:

- Reduced commuting
- Greater control over the workday
- Fewer office interruptions
- Better accommodation of disability or health needs
- Improved work-life fit

Allowing employees to choose can produce better outcomes than imposing one arrangement on everyone, although individual preferences do not eliminate team-coordination requirements.

# 6. What does the strongest evidence imply?

A reasonable synthesis of the research is:

## Fully remote work

**Likely positive or neutral** for:

- Routine digital work
- Call-center operations
- Individual analysis
- Experienced software and knowledge workers
- Jobs with objective output measures

**More likely negative** for:

- New hires
- Junior employees
- Creative and interdependent teams
- Jobs requiring mentoring
- Work dependent on tacit knowledge
- Organizations with weak documentation and management systems

## Hybrid work

Hybrid arrangements have the strongest overall evidence because they combine:

- Focus and flexibility at home
- Social interaction and coordination in person
- Better retention
- Lower commuting burden
- Periodic mentoring and team-building

However, hybrid work is not automatically effective. If employees come into the office on different days, office time may fail to produce meaningful collaboration. Firms need coordinated “anchor days,” clear purposes for in-person work, and digital inclusion for people who are remote.

## Office work

Office work remains advantageous when:

- Work is highly collaborative
- Employees are inexperienced
- Learning occurs through observation
- Rapid informal coordination matters
- Physical tools or facilities are important
- Organizational culture and innovation are central

But office work can also impose substantial costs through commuting, interruptions, excessive meetings, and reduced autonomy.

# 7. Implications for employers and policymakers

Employers should avoid asking whether remote work is productive in general. They should instead evaluate:

1. **Which tasks can be measured objectively?**
2. **How much coordination is required?**
3. **Are employees experienced and self-directed?**
4. **How important are mentoring and informal learning?**
5. **Can the firm document processes effectively?**
6. **What happens to quality, innovation, retention, and promotion—not just short-term output?**

Useful evaluation practices include:

- Randomized or phased pilots where feasible
- Comparing output per hour, not just total output
- Tracking quality and rework
- Measuring turnover and absenteeism
- Monitoring onboarding and promotion
- Surveying collaboration and employee well-being
- Separating individual from team-level outcomes
- Evaluating hybrid schedules rather than treating all remote work as one category

The main policy danger is relying on visibility as a substitute for productivity measurement. Employees may be highly productive at home but less visible, while office presence can create an impression of effort without proving output.

---

# Conclusion

The best research does not show that remote work uniformly improves or reduces productivity.

- **Controlled experiments** generally find positive or neutral effects in suitable jobs, with especially strong evidence for improved productivity or retention in structured work.
- **Observational studies** reveal substantial heterogeneity and often identify costs to coordination, communication, and innovation.
- **Company-level data** show that remote work can lower turnover and operating costs while also creating long-term challenges for mentoring, creativity, and cross-team collaboration.

The apparent contradiction disappears once productivity is treated as multidimensional. Remote work often improves **individual focus and retention**, but fully remote work can weaken **coordination, learning, and innovation**. Hybrid work frequently offers the best compromise, provided that firms deliberately design when and why employees meet in person.

The most accurate general conclusion is therefore:

> **Remote work improves productivity when the work is measurable, autonomous, and digitally transferable; it reduces productivity when success depends on frequent coordination, informal learning, and tacit knowledge exchange. The productivity effect is contingent—not universal.**
