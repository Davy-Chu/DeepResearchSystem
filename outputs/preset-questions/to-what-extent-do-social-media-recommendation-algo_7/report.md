# Research Report

## Research Question

To what extent do social-media recommendation algorithms contribute to political polarization? Separate evidence that algorithms change what users see from evidence that they actually change political beliefs or behavior, and explain why the literature reaches different conclusions.

## Summary

The supplied evidence supports a qualified, context-dependent conclusion. Recommendation algorithms can causally change users’ exposure, engagement, source mix, and visibility of political content, but changes in exposure do not reliably produce changes in political beliefs, polarization, or behavior. Some experiments report attitude shifts or increased polarization under particular platforms, content conditions, or algorithmic systems, while other randomized evidence finds substantial exposure changes with no measurable change in political attitudes. User selection, pre-existing preferences, network structure, content alignment, platform design, political context, outcome definitions, and study duration all complicate estimates of the algorithms’ independent contribution. The ledger does not support a reliable estimate of average effect size or conclusions about persistence, voting, participation, or offline behavior.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Recommendation algorithms can change the political content, source mix, ideological visibility, and engagement that users encounter, but the direction and magnitude of exposure effects are platform- and intervention-dependent rather than uniformly producing less viewpoint diversity.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The ledger includes direct randomized evidence of changed exposure and engagement, alongside direct counterevidence against a universal filter-bubble effect. The supplied material lacks comparable numerical effect sizes and full primary-study details, limiting estimates of average magnitude.

**Evidence**

- A randomized Facebook and Instagram experiment during the 2020 US election replaced default algorithmic feeds with reverse-chronological feeds. The intervention changed time spent, activity, political and untrustworthy content, and—on Facebook—uncivil/slur content and the visibility of moderate friends and ideologically mixed sources. [S10]
- A randomized seven-week experiment among active US-based X users found that the algorithmic feed increased engagement, increased conservative activist content, and reduced the visibility of traditional news sources. [S8]
- Experimental panel studies varied both algorithmic versus random selection and attitude-consistent versus attitude-inconsistent arguments, directly separating content alignment from selection method. [S6]
- A review reports that ranking algorithms had limited effects on the ideological balance of news consumption on Facebook or Google in some empirical studies, while weak ties could expose users to ideologically diverse information. [S12]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

Changes in what users see should not be treated as evidence that recommendation algorithms changed political beliefs, polarization, or behavior.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The randomized Facebook and Instagram null result is direct evidence for that intervention, and the open gaps explicitly state that persistence and offline behavioral consequences remain unestablished. This finding does not imply that attitude effects never occur; it limits generalization from exposure effects alone.

**Evidence**

- The Facebook and Instagram randomized feed intervention substantially changed exposure and on-platform experience but found no statistically significant change in issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, or other key political attitudes over three months. [S10]
- The review characterizes the literature as nuanced and reports limited effects of ranking algorithms on ideological news balance, qualifying broad claims that algorithmic curation reliably increases polarization. [S12]
- The ledger identifies no supplied evidence establishing effects on voting, political participation, or other offline political behavior; the experiments primarily measure attitudes, polarization, engagement, and exposure. [S10] [S6] [S8]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

Some experiments provide causal or comparatively well-supported evidence that algorithmic systems can affect political attitudes, but the effects are selective and do not consistently amount to greater polarization.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The ledger preserves both direct positive or selective findings and direct null findings. The evidence supports the possibility of causal attitude effects in particular settings, not a general or consistently positive effect of recommendation algorithms on polarization.

**Evidence**

- Two German experimental panel studies found that like-minded political exposure increased attitude and affective polarization more than opposing exposure. Algorithmic selection generally did not amplify those effects, although it produced a small direct attitude-polarization effect for one topic relative to random selection. [S6]
- A randomized seven-week X field experiment among active US-based users found that switching to the algorithmic feed shifted political opinions toward more conservative positions, while producing no major change in partisanship or polarization. [S8]
- A theoretical model links engagement-weighted and personalized ranking to feedback loops involving extreme engagement, misinformation, and polarizing content. Survey evidence around Facebook’s 2018 update was reported as consistent with increased ideological extremism and affective polarization, but the supplied material does not establish causal identification, comparison conditions, or effect sizes. [S11]
- Contrary direct evidence comes from the randomized Facebook and Instagram intervention, which found no significant change in issue or affective polarization or other key attitudes despite substantial exposure changes. [S10]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

Users’ own choices, pre-existing preferences, confirmation bias, social networks, and content alignment are complementary contributors to politically homogeneous exposure and polarization; recommendation algorithms are not established as the sole cause.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Multiple claims support an interaction-based account. However, the supplied evidence does not quantify the independent contribution of recommendation ranking relative to selection, homophily, confirmation bias, or network effects.

**Evidence**

- The sources explicitly identify user behavior alongside algorithmic filtering as a contributor to polarization, while describing curation as partly based on users’ interactions and preferences. [S1] [S2]
- The German experiments found stronger polarization effects for like-minded content exposure than for algorithmic selection itself in general. [S6]
- The Facebook and Instagram intervention isolated feed ranking from users’ existing networks while acknowledging that user choice and network composition also shape content. [S10]
- The model describes a feedback loop between user engagement and algorithmic ranking, and the review identifies weak ties and network processes as sources of ideological diversity. [S11] [S12]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

The literature reaches different conclusions because studies examine different platforms and ranking systems, populations, political contexts, timeframes, interventions, exposure conditions, and definitions of polarization; these differences remain insufficiently resolved in the supplied evidence.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The cross-study differences are directly represented in the ledger, while the unresolved gap confirms that the evidence is not sufficient to determine which sources of heterogeneity explain the disagreement or how much each matters.

**Evidence**

- The evidence contrasts a three-month Facebook and Instagram feed-ranking intervention that changed exposure without changing measured political attitudes, a seven-week X intervention that shifted opinions without substantially changing polarization, and German panel experiments in which like-minded exposure increased polarization while algorithmic selection generally did not amplify it. [S10] [S8] [S6]
- The studies measure different outcomes, including source visibility, content exposure, engagement, issue polarization, affective polarization, political opinions, partisanship, and political knowledge. These outcomes need not respond in the same way. [S6] [S8] [S10]
- The ledger states that the available sources do not adequately explain heterogeneity across platforms, algorithm designs, populations, political contexts, periods, exposure intensity, intervention types, or short- versus long-term effects. [S12] [S10] [S6] [S8]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Direct randomized evidence conflicts on downstream political effects: Facebook and Instagram showed substantial exposure changes but no significant attitude or polarization changes, whereas X produced a conservative opinion shift, and German studies found polarization effects from like-minded exposure plus a small topic-specific effect of algorithmic selection. [S10] [S8] [S6]
- Broad claims that ranking reduces ideological diversity are qualified by a review reporting limited effects on ideological news balance and possible diversity through weak ties. [S12]
- Evidence around Facebook’s 2018 update is consistent with increased extremism and affective polarization, but the supplied material does not establish causal identification or exclude concurrent causes. [S11]
- The supplied evidence does not establish whether short- or medium-term attitude effects persist, accumulate, or translate into voting, participation, or other offline political behavior. [S10] [S6] [S8]

## Remaining Gaps

- Primary-study details and comparable effect sizes are insufficient to estimate how much recommendation ranking independently changes political exposure or ideological diversity.
- The independent effects of recommendation ranking cannot be cleanly separated from user selection, confirmation bias, homophily, network effects, and general social-media use across the supplied sources.
- The evidence does not adequately resolve heterogeneity by platform, algorithm design, population, political context, study period, exposure intensity, intervention type, or outcome definition.
- The supplied experiments do not establish persistence, cumulative effects, voting effects, participation effects, or other offline behavioral consequences.
- The verification process stopped because the maximum number of iterations was reached; no additional verification records were supplied.

## Conclusion

Recommendation algorithms make a demonstrable contribution to political information exposure: controlled interventions changed what users saw, which sources or political content were visible, and how much they engaged. The evidence for changing political beliefs or polarization is weaker and heterogeneous. Like-minded exposure can increase polarization, and at least one field experiment shifted political opinions, but other randomized evidence found no attitude or polarization effects despite substantial exposure changes, while some algorithmic interventions changed opinion without increasing measured polarization. The best-supported overall judgment is therefore that algorithms are plausible and sometimes consequential contributors within a broader user-and-network system, not a consistently demonstrated primary cause of political polarization. A reliable average magnitude, durable effect, or effect on political behavior cannot currently be drawn from the supplied ledger.
