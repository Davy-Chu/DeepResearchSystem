# Research Report

## Research Question

To what extent do social-media recommendation algorithms contribute to political polarization? Separate evidence that algorithms change what users see from evidence that they actually change political beliefs or behavior, and explain why the literature reaches different conclusions.

## Summary

Recommendation algorithms clearly influence political exposure: they rank, filter, and amplify some political content, and changing ranking systems can substantially alter the content users encounter. The evidence that these exposure changes translate into durable changes in political beliefs or behavior is much weaker and more mixed. Large platform-scale experiments, especially on Facebook and Instagram during the 2020 U.S. election, generally found sizable effects on content exposure but little or no short-run effect on measured political attitudes, affective polarization, or political behavior. Other studies find that exposure to opposing political content can increase polarization, while observational and laboratory evidence links engagement-based ranking and repeated exposure to more extreme or hostile material. The best-supported conclusion is therefore that algorithms are important contributors to the information environment and can intensify polarization under some conditions, but they are not demonstrated to be a dominant, general-purpose cause of polarization. User self-selection, preexisting polarization, offline identities, media supply, platform-specific designs, and the difficulty of measuring slow-moving beliefs explain much of the disagreement.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Recommendation systems substantially change what political content users see.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

This finding is supported by direct platform experiments and by convergent observational evidence across major platforms.

**Evidence**

- Facebook users' own ideological choices and the ranking algorithm both affected the diversity of political news in feeds. In the 2015 observational study, algorithmic ranking reduced exposure to cross-cutting content, although users' choices accounted for a larger share of the reduction.
- A large-scale Facebook experiment that randomized users between the normal personalized feed and a reverse-chronological feed changed the political content people encountered, including the prevalence of like-minded and cross-cutting sources.
- A parallel Instagram experiment found that changing the feed from algorithmically ranked to chronological altered exposure to political content and the kinds of posts users engaged with.
- Research on Twitter/X shows that the engagement-based home timeline can amplify messages from political actors and sources that are more emotionally charged or ideologically distinct than those seen in a chronological timeline.

### Finding 2

**Claim**

Algorithms are not the only, and often not the largest, source of selective exposure; users actively choose whom to follow, click, read, and share.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The distinction between algorithmic filtering and user selection is consistently observed and is central to experimental interpretations.

**Evidence**

- Bakshy, Messing, and Adamic estimated that individual choices were responsible for more ideological segregation in Facebook news exposure than algorithmic ranking, though ranking still had a measurable incremental effect.
- Studies of online news consumption find that people frequently use ideologically congenial sources and that social networks, search behavior, and broader media markets shape exposure alongside recommendation systems.
- The Facebook and Instagram feed experiments changed ranking while leaving users' networks, subscriptions, and much of the available content unchanged; this design illustrates that ranking is only one layer in a larger selection process.

### Finding 3

**Claim**

Changing exposure does not reliably produce immediate changes in political beliefs, affective polarization, or political behavior.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

These are among the strongest causal tests available: large randomized interventions conducted on real platforms. Their main limitation is that they mostly measure short-run outcomes.

**Evidence**

- The 2023 Facebook feed experiment found that switching users from the ranked feed to a chronological feed substantially changed political content exposure and engagement, but did not detect meaningful short-run changes in political knowledge, polarization, political attitudes, or well-being across the main outcomes.
- The companion Instagram experiment likewise found large changes in exposure and use but little evidence of short-run changes in ideological attitudes or political evaluations.
- A separate Facebook intervention that reduced reshared political content changed the composition of users' feeds and reduced political content consumption, but did not produce clear changes in key political attitudes during the study period.
- A large Facebook deactivation experiment during the 2020 election reduced exposure to political news and altered some reported political experiences, but found limited evidence of effects on measured political knowledge, polarization, or political participation over several weeks.

### Finding 4

**Claim**

There is credible evidence that some forms of algorithmically mediated exposure can increase polarization, especially when users are deliberately exposed to opposing political content or when content is emotionally provocative.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The evidence is persuasive that effects can occur in particular contexts, but estimates vary by platform, political group, intervention, and outcome; some prominent claims rely on observational rather than randomized data.

**Evidence**

- In a randomized Twitter study, exposure to opposing political views led Republican participants to become more conservative and produced mixed but sometimes polarizing effects among Democrats. The intervention did not simulate every component of a platform recommender, but it demonstrates that exposure itself can backfire rather than moderate attitudes.
- Laboratory and field studies of social-media sharing show that moral-emotional language and identity-congruent content receive greater diffusion, potentially increasing out-group hostility and the visibility of polarizing material.
- Research on YouTube and other recommendation systems reports pathways through which repeated recommendations can lead users toward more ideologically extreme or conspiratorial material, although the magnitude and generality of this effect are contested and difficult to identify causally.
- Observational studies associate ideologically homogeneous networks and exposure to partisan or hostile content with stronger affective polarization, but these associations are compatible with reverse causality: already-polarized users may seek and produce such content.

### Finding 5

**Claim**

Algorithms can affect political behavior even when they do not measurably change general political beliefs, but the behavioral evidence is narrower and less consistent than the exposure evidence.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The literature clearly documents changes in on-platform behavior, but evidence for downstream electoral behavior is sparse, difficult to measure, and often underpowered for small effects.

**Evidence**

- Feed-ranking interventions changed likes, comments, resharing, time spent, and the amount of political material consumed. These are genuine behavioral effects, but they do not necessarily imply changes in voting, persuasion, or democratic participation.
- Studies of social influence and political mobilization find that platform design can affect whether users encounter calls to action, civic information, and mobilizing messages, making effects on turnout or participation plausible in specific campaigns.
- Large-scale deactivation and feed interventions have generally found small or uncertain effects on turnout-related outcomes, political knowledge, and vote choice compared with their effects on platform use and content exposure.

### Finding 6

**Claim**

The literature reaches different conclusions because studies examine different platforms, algorithms, populations, interventions, and definitions of polarization.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

These methodological differences are explicit in the studies and account for much of the apparent contradiction.

**Evidence**

- A feed-ranking experiment tests the marginal effect of ordering posts from an existing network; it does not test the effect of joining a platform, acquiring a new partisan network, or being recommended into a new community. These interventions can reasonably produce different results.
- Political polarization can mean ideological extremity, partisan issue attitudes, dislike of the opposing party, perceived disagreement, ideological segregation, hostility, or partisan behavior. Algorithms may increase one dimension, such as hostile content exposure, without changing another, such as vote choice.
- Effects are heterogeneous: people with strong prior identities may resist or assimilate new information differently; opposing content can moderate some users but provoke backlash in others; and partisan asymmetries have been reported in both exposure and response.
- Short randomized interventions have strong causal identification but limited duration, while long-term observational studies capture cumulative exposure but cannot cleanly separate algorithmic effects from self-selection and confounding.
- Platforms continuously modify ranking objectives, recommendation systems, moderation, and available content. Results from Facebook's 2020 feed or Twitter's prior ranking system therefore cannot automatically be generalized to current systems or to TikTok, YouTube, or Instagram.

### Finding 7

**Claim**

The strongest overall interpretation is that algorithms are an amplifier and gatekeeper of polarization, not a sufficient explanation for it.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

This conclusion reconciles the strongest causal and observational findings without treating either exposure changes or null attitude results as dispositive.

**Evidence**

- Algorithms influence visibility, repetition, social reinforcement, and the speed of diffusion, creating mechanisms through which partisan and inflammatory content can spread.
- Yet the largest causal feed experiments found exposure effects without comparable short-run attitude effects, and user choice and preexisting partisan networks often explain more segregation than ranking alone.
- Political polarization predates social media and is also driven by party sorting, elite rhetoric, traditional media, geography, institutions, and offline social identities. Social platforms can intensify these forces without being their primary origin.

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Observational studies often report links between recommendation systems, extreme content, and polarization, whereas randomized platform experiments often find little attitude change. The former may capture cumulative or self-selected effects; the latter provide cleaner causal estimates but usually last only weeks or months.
- The effect of exposure to opposing views is unsettled. Some interventions increase understanding or reduce misperceptions, while others trigger backlash and greater partisan extremity.
- Public evidence about proprietary recommendation systems is incomplete. Researchers frequently lack full ranking logs, user-level exposure histories, recommendation candidates, and information about model updates.
- There is greater evidence for effects on engagement, content consumption, and ideological segregation than for effects on vote choice, turnout, or durable political belief change.
- Most causal evidence comes from the United States and from a limited number of platforms, so effects may differ substantially in other countries, languages, media systems, and political environments.

## Remaining Gaps

- Longer-term randomized or quasi-experimental studies that track the same users across multiple election cycles.
- Better measurement of durable belief change, political knowledge, affective polarization, turnout, and offline political behavior rather than only clicks and engagement.
- Access to recommendation logs and counterfactual ranking data across YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, Facebook, and X.
- Research on adolescents, low-information users, politically disengaged people, and populations outside the United States.
- Studies that separate the effects of ranking from the effects of content production, targeted advertising, social-network formation, and moderation.
- More research on heterogeneous effects, including partisan asymmetry and the possibility that a small susceptible subgroup experiences large effects even when population-average effects are near zero.

## Conclusion

Social-media recommendation algorithms demonstrably change political exposure: they determine which posts are prominent, repeated, and socially reinforced, and ranking experiments show that these changes can be large. But exposure is not equivalent to persuasion. The best causal evidence so far indicates that altering feed ranking or reducing political content can change consumption and on-platform behavior without producing large immediate changes in political beliefs, affective polarization, or voting-related behavior. At the same time, particular recommendation environments can intensify polarization through backlash to opposing views, emotional and identity-based amplification, repeated exposure, and reinforcement of homogeneous networks. The most defensible answer is therefore conditional: algorithms contribute meaningfully to polarization as part of a broader ecosystem, especially by amplifying and organizing polarizing material, but the claim that they generally and directly cause mass political belief change is not supported by the strongest available evidence. Differences in platform design, user selection, outcome definitions, time horizon, and research method explain why the literature appears divided.
