# Research Report

## Research Question

Can carbon capture and storage make a significant contribution to reducing global CO₂ emissions? Evaluate its demonstrated effectiveness, costs, scalability, energy requirements, and major arguments for and against large-scale deployment.

## Summary

The accumulated evidence supports a qualified conclusion. CCS is technically feasible and has achieved meaningful process-level performance in selected facilities. However, current deployment remains very small relative to global emissions, commercial-scale power-sector experience is limited, project-wide and lifecycle performance is heterogeneous, and costs, energy requirements, transport, storage, liability, and permanence remain important constraints. CCS could make a significant contribution in targeted hard-to-abate sectors—especially cement, steel, chemicals, hydrogen, selected industrial facilities, and some carbon-removal applications—but the evidence does not justify treating it as a dominant global solution or as a general substitute for rapid emissions reductions, electrification, renewable energy, and efficiency.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

CCS is technically demonstrated, but demonstrated climate effectiveness is narrower than headline capture rates suggest.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Government project documentation and EIA information establish technical operation and the limited slipstream scope. The exact whole-system and lifecycle performance remains unresolved.

**Evidence**

- Petra Nova operated a commercial-scale post-combustion capture system, treating a 240-MW-equivalent flue-gas slipstream, transporting CO₂ through an 81-mile pipeline, and injecting it for enhanced oil recovery. Its project report records approximately 3.54 million metric tonnes of CO₂ captured during the three-year demonstration period. [S24]
- Petra Nova’s reported process-level capture efficiency was 92.4%, exceeding its 90% target when the capture system was operating. However, the system treated only about 37% of the host unit’s emissions, corresponding to approximately 33% of total Unit 8 emissions under the stated configuration. [S26] [S19]
- The DOE project report demonstrates construction, operation, transport, injection, and monitoring, but does not by itself establish whole-plant net emissions reductions, full lifecycle climate benefit, or permanent storage independent of EOR. [S24] [S14]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

The broader project record includes both high-performing facilities and significant underperformance, so technical feasibility should not be equated with consistently achieved abatement.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The sources provide complementary evidence of successful projects, operational problems, and the small number of commercial-scale power demonstrations, although some project classifications come from interested organizations.

**Evidence**

- CATF identifies Sleipner, the Alberta Carbon Trunk Line, and Quest as projects that consistently achieved high technical performance, while also documenting operational challenges and noting that many projects were driven by commercial or demonstration objectives rather than maximum climate benefit. [S20]
- IEEFA reports that Petra Nova experienced downtime involving the capture plant, host coal unit, pipeline, and EOR field, was taken offline in May 2020, and had substantial impairment and ownership-sale losses. [S21]
- The IEEAGHG report identifies only four thermal power plants with commercial-scale capture deployment—Boundary Dam, Petra Nova, Jinjie, and Taizhou—while describing other projects as pilots or proposals. [S11]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

Current global CCS deployment is too small to constitute a major global emissions-reduction solution today, despite a growing pipeline.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Recent IEA and industry data independently show an operating capacity in the tens of millions of tonnes per year and a rapidly expanding pipeline, while the IEA explicitly says the current trajectory is insufficient. The exact total is definition-sensitive.

**Evidence**

- The IEA reports just over 50 MtCO₂ per year of capture-and-storage capacity in operation in the first quarter of 2025 and projects approximately 430 MtCO₂ per year of capture capacity by 2030 from the existing pipeline. It states that current trends remain insufficient for a net-zero-by-mid-century pathway. [S9]
- The Global CCS Institute, as reported by the Journal of Petroleum Technology, lists 77 operating facilities with 64 MtCO₂ per year of storage capability, plus 44 MtCO₂ per year under construction and a projected fivefold increase in operating capture capacity by 2030. [S6]
- The evidence-based review reports that, despite more than $40 billion in global investment, CCS captures and stores less than 0.1% of annual global CO₂ emissions. [S1]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

CCS has its strongest prospective role in hard-to-abate sectors and selected existing assets, rather than as a universal technology for the power system.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The targeted-sector conclusion is consistently supported by the IEA and scholarly review material. Quantitative future potential is modeled, not demonstrated.

**Evidence**

- The IEA identifies cement, iron and steel, chemicals, retrofits of existing industrial and power facilities, low-carbon hydrogen, and carbon removal using bioenergy or direct air capture as strategic applications where alternatives may be limited. [S10]
- The IEA states that CCUS could address emissions from existing plants that might otherwise emit 8 GtCO₂ in 2050, but presents this as strategic potential rather than achieved abatement. [S10]
- The evidence-based review similarly recommends strategic deployment in hard-to-abate sectors such as cement and legacy-infrastructure retrofits while warning against generalized reliance on CCS. [S1]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

CCS is capital-intensive and its costs vary greatly by application, CO₂ concentration, technology, transport, storage conditions, and project design.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The sources directly document broad cost ranges and project-specific capital requirements. They do not support a single universal CCS cost, and the supplied material lacks common assumptions for comparing sectors.

**Evidence**

- The evidence-based review gives an indicative energy requirement of 2.5–8 GJ per tonne of CO₂ and a cost range of approximately $50–600 per tonne of CO₂, emphasizing strong variation across contexts. [S1]
- Petra Nova involved approximately $1 billion of original investment, including capture, cogeneration, pipeline, storage-site improvements, and related costs; EIA reports a comparable retrofit cost of about $4,200 per kilowatt. [S24] [S19]
- Kemper’s estimated capital cost increased from roughly $2.4–3 billion to more than $7.5 billion, after which its planned coal-gasification CCS approach was abandoned. [S19] [S21]
- The sustainability review characterizes CCS as one of the more expensive and technically challenging abatement options and argues that full lifecycle costs must include capital, energy, operating, environmental, and social factors. [S3]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

Energy requirements can materially reduce the net benefit of CCS, particularly in power applications.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The need for additional energy and the broad energy range are directly documented. The precise net energy penalty is not settled because sources use different boundaries and metrics.

**Evidence**

- The review estimates energy requirements of 2.5–8 GJ per tonne of CO₂. [S1]
- Petra Nova required a dedicated natural-gas cogeneration unit to supply process steam and power for capture, illustrating the need for additional energy infrastructure. [S19] [S24]
- The IEA technical report notes that further reductions in capture capital and operating costs are essential, especially as conventional generators may operate less frequently alongside variable renewables. [S11]
- A favorable technical account reports lower-than-anticipated power and steam requirements at Petra Nova, but it does not provide a common net-energy metric including the associated gas-fired supply. [S26]

### Finding 7

**Claim**

Large-scale deployment would require extensive transport and storage infrastructure, robust monitoring, and long-term liability arrangements.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The infrastructure and governance requirements are directly described across technical and review sources. The retrieved material does not quantify globally available storage, leakage probabilities, or long-term liability performance.

**Evidence**

- CCS involves integrated capture, transport, and geological storage; climate-oriented projects may need saline-aquifer storage and longer or more complex transport routes, adding cost beyond high-purity commercial CO₂ streams. [S20]
- The ACR methodology requires accounting for capture, transport, storage, and atmospheric leakage and requires monitoring, reporting, verification, validation, and verification procedures. [S13]
- Sources identify suitable storage, geological uncertainty, permanence, leakage, safety, environmental impacts, and transfer of long-term public liability as material concerns. [S1] [S3] [S4]
- The IEA and Global CCS Institute report substantial growth in pipelines, hubs, storage capacity, and projects under construction, but announced or nominal capacity is not equivalent to sustained, independently verified permanent storage. [S6] [S9]

### Finding 8

**Claim**

Enhanced oil recovery complicates the assessment of current CCS climate benefits.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The prevalence of EOR and the need for lifecycle accounting are consistently documented. The sources do not establish one universal net climate result for EOR.

**Evidence**

- Zero Carbon Analytics reports that 82.5% of existing CCS capacity used captured CO₂ for enhanced oil recovery and that almost all of the 41 operational projects it assessed were connected to oil and gas. [S7]
- Petra Nova transported captured CO₂ to an oilfield for EOR and produced more than 4.2 million barrels of oil during the project period. [S24]
- The Frontiers review reports that some lifecycle analyses find CO₂-EOR can be net carbon negative under particular operational choices, but also notes that results differ because studies use different boundaries, including whether downstream refining and fuel combustion are counted. [S14]
- CATF states that many operating projects were motivated by commercial CO₂ sales for EOR rather than by maximizing atmospheric emissions reductions. [S20]

### Finding 9

**Claim**

The principal arguments for large-scale CCS are targeted emissions control, preservation of options for difficult industrial processes, possible retrofit of existing facilities, dispatchable low-carbon power in selected systems, and carbon removal when paired with bioenergy or direct air capture.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The strategic rationale is well documented, but power-system and future-scale benefits are conditional on modeling assumptions and are not equivalent to demonstrated global performance.

**Evidence**

- The IEA identifies hard-to-abate industry, existing-asset retrofits, low-carbon hydrogen, and carbon removal as strategic applications for CCUS. [S10]
- The IEAGHG report’s UK modeling finds power CCS can provide flexible, dispatchable generation and can be cost-effective under some modeled system configurations. [S11]
- CCS can capture concentrated industrial emissions and transport them to geological storage; this integrated capability is directly demonstrated by Petra Nova and other projects. [S24] [S20]

### Finding 10

**Claim**

The principal arguments against broad deployment are limited demonstrated scale, uncertain whole-system performance, high and variable costs, energy penalties, EOR-related fossil-fuel lock-in, storage and liability risks, and the possibility of delaying alternatives.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

These objections recur across technical, governmental, scholarly, and critical assessments. Their relative importance varies by sector and project, and the supplied material lacks a uniform quantified comparison of opportunity costs.

**Evidence**

- The IEA describes CCS deployment as historically slow, with unmet expectations and limited impact on global CO₂ emissions; current trends remain insufficient for net zero. [S9] [S10]
- Project evidence documents partial capture at Petra Nova, downtime, disputed effective capture rates, major capital requirements, and the abandonment of Kemper’s planned CCS approach. [S19] [S21] [S24]
- Reviews warn that CCS can divert resources from renewables, efficiency, electrification, bio-based removals, mineralization, and other mitigation options if deployed indiscriminately. [S1] [S3] [S15]
- EOR-based deployment can support additional oil production, and its climate benefit depends on lifecycle accounting rather than injected CO₂ alone. [S7] [S14] [S20]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Operating-capacity estimates differ: the IEA reports just over 50 MtCO₂ per year of capture-and-storage capacity in early 2025, while the Global CCS Institute reports 64 MtCO₂ per year of storage capability across 77 facilities. The difference likely reflects definitions, facility boundaries, and capture-versus-storage measures; the figures should not be merged into one precise total. [S6] [S9]
- Petra Nova’s reported 92.4% capture efficiency applies to the treated stream when operating, whereas EIA data show that only about 37% of the host unit’s emissions entered the capture system. IEEFA estimates a lower whole-system effective rate after including gas-fired energy emissions, but that estimate is disputed and the underlying owner data were not publicly provided. [S19] [S21] [S26]
- Petra Nova is described as a technical success by its project report and CCS engineering assessors, but as a commercial and system-level failure by IEEFA because of shutdown, downtime, impairments, and ownership-sale losses. These views use different definitions of success: process performance and demonstration versus sustained whole-project economics and net climate benefit. [S21] [S24] [S26]
- Some sources identify high-performing projects such as Sleipner and Quest, while others emphasize underperformance in Petra Nova, Kemper, In Salah, and Boundary Dam. This indicates heterogeneous performance rather than a settled average applicable to all future projects. [S17] [S20] [S21]
- Power CCS is modeled as cost-effective and useful for dispatchable generation in some systems, while other evidence favors high-renewable systems and warns that fossil-fuel CCS raises costs. The difference depends on assumptions about renewable penetration, utilization, fuel prices, storage, hydrogen, and the value of dispatchability. [S11] [S15]
- EOR lifecycle studies reach different conclusions about net climate benefit because they use different operational choices and emissions boundaries. The accumulated sources do not resolve how recovered-oil emissions should be consistently counted. [S7] [S14] [S24]

## Remaining Gaps

- No single independently verified dataset in the accumulated material reconciles capture rate, uptime, energy use, whole-facility emissions, lifecycle emissions, and permanent storage across major CCS projects.
- The exact global quantity of CO₂ permanently stored, separated from nominal capture capacity, utilization, and EOR-associated injection, remains unresolved.
- Application-specific costs and energy penalties for cement, steel, hydrogen, chemicals, power, direct air capture, and saline-aquifer storage are not presented on a common methodological basis.
- The share of announced 2030 capacity that will reach final investment decision, construction completion, sustained operation, and high capture performance is uncertain.
- The retrieved evidence does not quantify globally available geological storage, transport constraints, leakage risk, monitoring effectiveness, or long-term liability arrangements at multi-billion-tonne annual scale.
- The evidence does not establish how representative high-performing projects are of future deployments in sectors beyond natural-gas processing and EOR.

## Conclusion

CCS can make a significant contribution to global CO₂ reduction, but the defensible case is targeted and conditional rather than universal. Its technical feasibility is demonstrated, including commercial-scale capture, transport, and geological injection, and high-performing projects show that strong process performance is achievable. Yet current capacity is only in the tens of millions of tonnes per year, power-sector demonstrations are few, and project-wide results depend on uptime, auxiliary energy, transport, storage, and lifecycle accounting. Costs are high and highly application-dependent, with substantial energy and infrastructure requirements. EOR-linked projects further complicate claims of net climate benefit because additional oil production and downstream emissions must be counted. The strongest deployment case is therefore for genuinely hard-to-abate industrial emissions, selected existing facilities, low-carbon hydrogen where appropriate, and carefully governed carbon-removal applications. CCS should complement—not replace—direct emissions avoidance, efficiency, electrification, renewable energy, and process change. On the accumulated evidence, it is a potentially important component of a broad mitigation portfolio, but not yet a demonstrated dominant or stand-alone global solution.
