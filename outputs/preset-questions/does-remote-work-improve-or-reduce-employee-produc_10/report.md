# Research Report

## Research Question

Does remote work improve or reduce employee productivity? Compare evidence from controlled experiments, observational studies, and company-level data, and explain why different studies reach conflicting conclusions.

## Summary

The accumulated evidence does not support a universal productivity effect. Hybrid work appears capable of preserving measured individual performance while improving retention. Mostly remote work produced a sizable output increase in one randomized call-center experiment, but that result was specific to a measurable occupation and was driven substantially by more minutes worked. Observational, survey, and industry-level evidence is mixed and generally weaker for causal inference. Apparent conflicts largely reflect differences in work arrangement, occupation, task, outcome measure, worker selection, timing, and organizational adaptation.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Remote work has no single productivity effect; outcomes depend on the arrangement, occupation, task, intensity of remote work, and management context.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

This conditional pattern is consistent across randomized studies, official analyses, and a systematic review, although the evidence does not establish a universal effect for all occupations.

**Evidence**

- A systematic review found mixed consequences for employee performance and well-being and noted that studies use overlapping terms for fully remote, hybrid, and other arrangements. [S5]
- Official and review sources identify task type, technology, home environment, worker motivation, worker experience, and management practices as factors affecting outcomes; they report positive effects for some focused tasks but mixed or negative effects for some collaborative, learning-intensive, or newly hired roles. [S1] [S3] [S5]
- The main controlled studies examine substantially different interventions: four days per week at home for call-center workers versus two days per week at home for professional employees. [S8] [S9] [S10] [S12] [S14]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

Controlled experiments provide the clearest evidence: mostly remote work increased measured output in a narrowly defined call-center setting, while hybrid work preserved performance in a larger professional workforce.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The findings come from randomized experiments, including the primary Nature article and original CTrip study reports. External validity remains limited by the firms, occupations, schedules, and outcome measures studied.

**Evidence**

- In the randomized CTrip experiment, eligible volunteer call-center employees assigned to work from home four days per week increased measured performance by about 13% over nine months, with no measurable decline in call quality. [S10] [S12] [S14]
- In the randomized Trip.com trial of 1,612 professional employees, working from home two days per week had no meaningful effect on performance reviews, promotions, coding output, or marketing revenue relative to full-time office work. [S8] [S9]
- The Trip.com trial also found resignations fell by approximately one-third among hybrid workers. [S8] [S9]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

The CTrip productivity increase reflected both more effective time worked and higher output per minute, so it is not simply evidence of higher hourly productivity.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The decomposition and post-trial selection are reported consistently in the original study accounts. The welfare implications of working more minutes, however, remain unresolved.

**Evidence**

- Approximately 9 percentage points of the roughly 13% performance increase came from more minutes worked, associated with fewer breaks and sick days; approximately 4 percentage points came from more calls per minute, attributed to quieter home conditions. [S10] [S12] [S14]
- After the experiment, employees could reselect home or office work and the reported gain rose to about 22%, but this post-trial estimate was subject to self-selection and was not equivalent to the randomized estimate. [S10] [S12] [S14]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

Observational and survey evidence is mixed and weaker for establishing causality than the controlled experiments.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

These studies are useful for documenting perceptions, mechanisms, and adaptation, but self-reporting, selection into remote work and firms, heterogeneous designs, and the absence of a clear causal control group limit interpretation.

**Evidence**

- Small-business owners reported substantial productivity declines early in 2020, but the median assessment became positive by 2021 after technology investment, training, task redesign, and improved remote-management practices. [S2]
- A systematic review of 20 peer-reviewed studies found mixed effects on performance and well-being rather than a consistent increase or decrease. [S5]
- Survey-based comparisons of remote-supporting or high-performing companies report favorable perceived effort and adaptability, but use attitudinal measures and do not establish that remote work caused higher objective output. [S15]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

Company- and industry-level data do not establish a consistent aggregate productivity effect.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Official sources directly report both positive and near-null aggregate estimates and explain why such estimates are not clean causal tests.

**Evidence**

- One BLS specification found a positive association between increased remote work and total-factor productivity growth across 61 industries, after accounting for pre-pandemic productivity trends. [S3]
- The same BLS material and a Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco analysis of 43 industries found little relationship between teleworkability and productivity growth in separate analyses. [S3] [S11]
- The industry analyses are observational and may reflect economic cycles, supply disruptions, industry composition, task differences, and other pandemic-era factors rather than the causal effect of remote work. [S3] [S11]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

Different studies reach conflicting conclusions mainly because they study different treatments, populations, outcomes, time periods, and mechanisms.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The sources explicitly document differences in treatment, measurement, selection, mechanisms, and adaptation, providing a coherent explanation for divergent findings.

**Evidence**

- The studies compare fully or mostly remote work with hybrid work and examine call-center employees versus professional employees in engineering, marketing, accounting, and finance. [S8] [S9] [S10] [S12] [S14]
- Productivity is measured variously through calls, calls per minute, minutes worked, coding, revenue, performance reviews, promotions, turnover, survey perceptions, labor productivity, and total-factor productivity. [S1] [S2] [S3] [S9] [S10] [S15]
- Remote work may reduce interruptions and commuting-related constraints while making coordination, idea sharing, mentoring, and learning more difficult; technology, training, task redesign, management quality, and organizational learning can alter the balance over time. [S1] [S2] [S3] [S5] [S11]
- Remote-work adoption is nonrandom because workers and firms in teleworkable occupations are more likely to adopt it, creating selection concerns in observational comparisons. [S2] [S3]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- The CTrip randomized experiment found higher measured output under mostly remote work, whereas the Trip.com randomized experiment found performance equivalence under hybrid work. These are not direct tests of the same intervention: they differ in occupation, remote-work intensity, employee population, and outcome measures. [S9] [S10] [S12] [S14]
- Aggregate estimates range from a positive BLS association between remote-work growth and total-factor productivity to near-null BLS and FRBSF estimates. The analyses differ in samples, periods, productivity measures, and specifications, and none provides the same causal identification as the randomized trials. [S3] [S11]
- Employer perceptions shifted from predominantly negative early-pandemic assessments to more positive later assessments after organizational learning and adaptation. This may reflect genuine adjustment, changes in work design, or changes in respondents' perceptions, but the supplied evidence does not fully separate these possibilities. [S2]
- Commercial sources make strongly favorable claims about remote productivity, but their underlying methods and samples are not sufficiently documented in the retrieved material. Their findings are therefore less persuasive than the primary experiments and official analyses. [S6] [S7] [S15]

## Remaining Gaps

- The evidence does not identify a general causal effect of fully remote work across occupations and industries outside narrowly measurable jobs such as the studied call-center setting.
- It remains unclear how much of the CTrip gain reflected higher productivity per hour versus longer effective working time, fewer breaks, and fewer absences, and what those changes imply for employee welfare.
- Long-run effects on innovation, mentoring, knowledge transfer, promotion, and career development remain insufficiently measured.
- The relative importance of voluntary choice, home working conditions, management quality, collaboration technology, and worker experience remains uncertain.
- The supplied material does not provide all experimental details, including complete confidence intervals, treatment compliance, and all subgroup estimates.

## Conclusion

Remote work neither uniformly improves nor uniformly reduces productivity. The strongest causal evidence supports a nuanced answer: a mostly remote schedule increased measured output in one selected call-center population, while a two-days-per-week hybrid schedule maintained performance in a larger professional workforce and substantially reduced turnover. Observational and survey evidence is mixed, and company- or industry-level analyses do not show a consistent aggregate effect. The apparent disagreement is therefore largely explained by differences in tasks, work intensity, populations, measurement, selection, timing, and management adaptation. The best-supported practical conclusion is that hybrid work can preserve measured individual performance, while the productivity consequences of fully remote work remain context-dependent and insufficiently established outside narrowly studied occupations.
