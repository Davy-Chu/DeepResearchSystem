# Research Report

## Research Question

To what extent do social-media recommendation algorithms contribute to political polarization? Separate evidence that algorithms change what users see from evidence that they actually change political beliefs or behavior, and explain why the literature reaches different conclusions.

## Summary

The accumulated evidence supports a conditional conclusion. Recommendation algorithms clearly change users’ information exposure, engagement, and on-platform activity. Evidence that they change political beliefs or polarization is more mixed: randomized studies on Meta found substantial exposure changes but no significant average changes in measured attitudes, whereas randomized studies on X found selected shifts in partisan warmth or policy opinions and changes in whom users followed. However, the X evidence did not show significant changes in affective polarization or self-reported partisanship, and the supplied research does not establish effects on voting, offline conduct, or other broader political behavior. Differences in platform, algorithm, treatment design, content targeted, timing, duration, and outcome measures help explain the divergent findings.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Recommendation algorithms have strong, directly measured effects on what users see and do on platforms.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Multiple controlled platform studies directly measured changes in exposure, engagement, time use, and activity.

**Evidence**

- The randomized Facebook/Instagram experiment found that replacing default algorithmic feeds with chronological feeds substantially changed time spent, activity, and exposure to political, untrustworthy, uncivil, moderate, and ideologically mixed content. [S6]
- Meta studies found substantial ideological segregation in political-news exposure; changes to ranking, resharing, and same-ideology recommendations altered users’ on-platform experiences even when political attitudes did not change. [S4] [S6]
- The X reranking experiment changed the order of the same posts, increasing or decreasing exposure to antidemocratic and partisan-animosity content without removing the underlying content. [S1] [S5]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

There is credible causal evidence that some algorithmic interventions change selected political attitudes, but the effects are not uniform across outcomes.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The X findings come from randomized field experiments and are reported in a primary Nature abstract and related institutional summary. The results are nevertheless outcome-specific rather than evidence of a universal polarization effect.

**Evidence**

- In a randomized field experiment involving 4,965 active U.S. X users over approximately seven weeks, switching from a chronological to an algorithmic feed increased engagement and shifted opinions toward more conservative positions on selected policy and current-affairs questions. [S10] [S11]
- A separate one-week X reranking experiment found that reducing exposure to antidemocratic and partisan-animosity content made users about 2.11 points warmer toward the opposing party, while increasing such exposure made them about 2.48 points cooler on a 100-point scale. [S1] [S5]
- The new X study found no significant effect on affective polarization or self-reported partisanship, indicating that evidence for selected policy-attitude change is stronger than evidence for generalized partisan hostility or identity change. [S10]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

The strongest counterevidence comes from randomized Meta experiments, which found large exposure effects but no significant average changes in measured political attitudes or polarization during the study periods.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

These findings are based on randomized interventions with direct measures of both media exposure and political outcomes, although one source is a news account of the studies.

**Evidence**

- Moving users from algorithmic to chronological Facebook and Instagram feeds changed exposure, time, and activity but did not significantly alter issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, or other key attitudes over three months. [S6]
- Additional Meta interventions reduced resharing or same-ideology recommendations and changed exposure to untrustworthy or political content, yet produced no significant changes in reported political attitudes, polarization, misinformation susceptibility, or extremist views. [S4]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

The X evidence suggests one pathway from algorithmic exposure to persistent online political behavior: users exposed to X’s algorithm were more likely to follow conservative political activist accounts, and those follows persisted after returning to a chronological feed.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The behavior is directly reported from the experiment, but the supplied material does not establish how long the effect persists or whether it affects sharing, participation, voting, or offline behavior.

**Evidence**

- The X study found that the algorithm promoted conservative content and demoted traditional media; algorithmically exposed users were more likely to follow conservative activist accounts and continued following them after the algorithm was switched off. [S10] [S11]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

User preferences and algorithmic ranking operate in a feedback loop, so algorithms may reinforce polarization without being its sole or initial cause.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The feedback-loop account is consistently described, but the supplied sources provide a mixture of conceptual, theoretical, survey, and experimental evidence rather than a single design that fully separates user selection from algorithmic influence.

**Evidence**

- The Meta researchers interpreted their findings as showing that users seek content aligned with existing views and that algorithms make this selection easier. [S4]
- The review describes algorithms as reinforcing existing social drivers, while user behavior, social networks, and platform architecture mutually shape recommendations and exposure. [S2] [S3] [S6]
- A theoretical model argues that engagement-based ranking can amplify misinformation and polarization because ideologically extreme users may engage disproportionately; personalization is predicted to intensify this feedback. [S7]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

The literature’s different conclusions are best understood as conditional findings rather than a simple contradiction about whether algorithms matter.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

These design differences and outcome contrasts are explicit in the accumulated sources, though the evidence does not identify one single factor as the definitive explanation.

**Evidence**

- Meta’s broad replacement of personalized feeds with chronological feeds produced major exposure changes but null average attitude effects, whereas X experiments either targeted partisan-animosity content or found platform-specific shifts in policy opinions and following behavior. [S6] [S10] [S1]
- The X study found an asymmetry: turning the algorithm on produced effects, while turning it off produced no comparable effects. Persistent follows provide one explanation for why removing algorithmic curation may not reverse earlier influence. [S10] [S11]
- The studies differed in platform, algorithmic system, treatment direction, content being amplified, intervention duration, political context, and outcome measures. [S6] [S10] [S1] [S2]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Meta experiments found no significant average effects on issue or affective polarization after broad feed changes, while X experiments found shifts in selected political opinions or partisan warmth. [S6] [S10] [S1]
- The X study found changes in selected policy attitudes but no significant changes in affective polarization or self-reported partisanship. Thus, “political attitudes” and “polarization” should not be treated as interchangeable outcomes. [S10] [S1]
- The evidence for online following behavior is stronger than the evidence for broader political behavior. The supplied studies do not establish effects on voting, political participation, sharing, offline hostility, or offline conduct. [S10] [S11] [S6]
- A theoretical and survey-based account links engagement-oriented ranking to increased extremism and affective polarization, while the randomized Meta feed experiment found null average attitude effects. These studies concern different platform features, periods, and research designs. [S7] [S6]

## Remaining Gaps

- Whether X-induced opinion and following changes persist substantially beyond the approximately seven-week study.
- Whether changes in follows or on-platform engagement lead to changes in sharing, voting, political participation, or offline behavior.
- Why targeted exposure to partisan animosity on X produced affective effects while broad chronological-feed replacement on Meta did not produce comparable average effects.
- How findings generalize beyond active U.S. users, the studied elections and political environments, and the particular ranking systems of X, Facebook, and Instagram.
- How effects vary with users’ prior ideology, political engagement, network composition, and exposure to competing information.

## Conclusion

Algorithms contribute meaningfully to the political information environment: the evidence that they alter exposure, ranking, engagement, and on-platform activity is strong. The evidence that they broadly polarize political beliefs or behavior is narrower and conditional. Some X experiments show causal changes in partisan warmth, selected policy opinions, and account-following behavior, but they do not show a general shift in affective polarization or partisanship. Meta experiments show that large changes in exposure can coexist with null average changes in measured attitudes. The most defensible synthesis is therefore that recommendation algorithms are capable of influencing political attitudes and online behavior, but their effects depend heavily on platform design, the content and ranking rule manipulated, users’ existing preferences, and the outcome and time horizon being measured. The accumulated evidence does not support attributing overall political polarization, voting behavior, or offline conduct primarily to recommendation algorithms alone.
