#### F1: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Controlled and quasi-experimental evidence indicates heterogeneous effects rather than a single causal direction.
- Sources: S1, S9, S10, S8
- Rationale: The cited sources describe randomized and experimental evidence with differing results: positive effects in the 2015 Ctrip trial, negative effects in the India data-entry experiment and among some fully remote roles, and null performance effects in the Trip.com hybrid trial. They also report differences by employee preferences, role, management status, and work arrangement. This directly supports heterogeneous causal effects rather than one uniform direction.
- Supporting text: S10 reports that work from home was nearly 20% less productive in one randomized experiment, while employees preferring home work differed in ability and experienced different productivity effects. It also reports no statistically significant performance effect in the Trip.com hybrid experiment. S1 and S9 summarize contrasting findings, including a 13% gain in the Ctrip randomized trial, declines in some settings, and no effect in the 2024 randomized trial. S8 reports improved retention but no performance-grade or promotion effect in a six-month randomized trial.

#### F2: SUPPORTED

- Claim: The precise Ctrip estimate and study description remain unresolved, although the supplied evidence agrees on a positive direction.
- Sources: S1, S9
- Rationale: The sources give differing Ctrip estimates and study descriptions: S1 reports 13.5% more calls among 249 employees in a four-days-per-week remote assignment, while S9 reports a 13% increase among 16,000 call-center employees and also describes a 22% result when workers chose their location. Despite these discrepancies, both sources describe a positive productivity effect.
- Supporting text: S1: “249 call center employees” and “13.5% more calls.” S9: “16,000 Chinese call center employees” and “13% overall performance improvement”; it also reports productivity “jump[ing] to 22%” with choice.

#### F3: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Observational and survey-based evidence generally reports stable or higher perceived productivity among remote workers, but a reliable causal conclusion about employee productivity cannot currently be drawn from this evidence alone.
- Sources: S2, S4, S6, S7, S5
- Rationale: The saved sources collectively report stable or improved productivity, largely based on surveys, perceptions, or associations: S4 describes stable or improved productivity in a two-year study and higher engagement among fully remote workers; S7 reports that the median business owner came to perceive a positive productivity impact, while explicitly presenting firm and worker survey evidence; S2 and S6 summarize survey-based reports of higher or stable output and associations rather than definitive causal effects. The claim’s caution that these sources alone do not establish a reliable causal conclusion is consistent with the evidence’s observational and perception-based character.
- Supporting text: S4: “Great Place To Work's two-year study of more than 800,000 employees found stable or improved productivity after transitioning to remote work.” S7: “the median business owner reported a positive productivity impact of remote work by 2021,” based on “surveys of small business owners and employees.” S5: the study analyzes “remote work effectiveness perceived by managers” and found assessments were “socially diverse.”

#### F4: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED

- Claim: Company- and industry-level evidence is mixed and does not identify a common individual employee causal effect.
- Sources: S3
- Rationale: S3 supports that evidence differs across firm-level studies and that industry-level findings are not uniform: some randomized firm experiments find small positive individual-productivity effects, while some single-firm case studies find short-run declines; it also reports differing aggregate-industry results. However, the source does not establish the broader conclusion that there is no common individual employee causal effect, particularly because the cited studies are limited and the industry evidence is correlational/aggregate.
- Supporting text: The source reports “small positive effects” in a few randomized firm experiments, but declines in “a couple of single-firm case studies.” It also says one industry-level analysis found little relationship, whereas the article’s own analysis found positive associations between remote work and TFP growth across 61 industries.

#### F5: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Hybrid or remote work can generate organizational benefits through lower turnover even when measured individual output is unchanged.
- Sources: S1, S8, S10, S3
- Rationale: The saved sources directly support the claim, especially for hybrid work. They report randomized evidence of lower attrition or quit rates alongside no statistically significant difference in performance reviews, promotions, lines of code, or other measured output. S3 additionally states that lower turnover can substantially reduce firms’ hiring costs. The evidence is strongest for hybrid work; the sources also mention similar turnover benefits in some fully remote settings.
- Supporting text: S8: The randomized trial found that hybrid working “reduced quit rates by one-third” while “not affect[ing] performance grades.” S10 likewise reports 33% lower attrition and no statistically significant impact on performance reviews, promotion rates, or lines of code. S3 says randomized studies found lower job turnover as job satisfaction rose, which “could substantially reduce firms’ hiring costs.”

#### F6: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Conflicting conclusions plausibly reflect differences in tasks and outcomes: focused individual work may improve remotely, while collaboration, knowledge sharing, mentoring, learning, and innovation may be weaker under fully remote arrangements.
- Sources: S1, S2, S6, S5, S7
- Rationale: The saved sources directly support both parts of the claim. S1 reports positive effects for individual focused tasks but negative effects for collaborative tasks, and says fully remote work reduced output in roles requiring high collaboration. S2 similarly describes improved remote focus and weaker office-free collaboration, knowledge sharing, mentoring, innovation, and learning. S7 adds that remote work may involve reduced learning and fewer chance encounters that spur innovation. S5 supports the role of communication and technical limitations in team and external-cooperation effectiveness. S6 supports easier focused work remotely, though its supplied text is less direct on the collaboration/learning drawbacks.
- Supporting text: S1: “the median productivity impact of remote work is +10% for individual focused tasks and -4% for collaborative tasks”; “fully remote work reduced output by 10-20% for some roles, particularly those requiring high collaboration.” S2: “remote work improves individual focus productivity,” while “cross-team interaction decreases when work becomes fully remote,” reducing “knowledge sharing and innovation”; in-office work benefits “mentoring junior employees” and “organizational learning.” S7: remote work faces “reduced opportunities for chance encounters that could spur innovation,” and owners believed remote employees were “less able to learn new skills.”

#### F7: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Remote-work effects may also differ with schedule intensity, worker characteristics, management, technology, onboarding, and time period, but these moderators are not fully established by the supplied evidence.
- Sources: S7, S10

S10 reports differing effects by work-from-home intensity, preferences, ability, caretaking demands, income, age, gender, managerial status, and schedule composition, while noting that some effects could not be fully explained.
