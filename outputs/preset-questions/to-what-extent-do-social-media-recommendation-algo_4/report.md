# Research Report

## Research Question

To what extent do social-media recommendation algorithms contribute to political polarization? Separate evidence that algorithms change what users see from evidence that they actually change political beliefs or behavior, and explain why the literature reaches different conclusions.

## Summary

The supplied evidence supports a clear causal effect of feed-ranking interventions on political-content exposure, but provides more limited and mixed evidence that those changes produce downstream political polarization. Randomized and quasi-experimental studies show that changing ranking or selection can alter the prominence, quantity, ideological mix, and source characteristics of political content users encounter. Downstream effects vary: some short interventions produced small changes in affective or attitudinal polarization, whereas a three-month Facebook/Instagram experiment found no significant changes in issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, or other key attitudes. No supplied study establishes durable belief change, voting effects, offline participation, or other political behavior effects. Differences in platform, intervention, topic, population, outcome, duration, and research design likely explain much of the divergent literature, but the supplied sources do not permit a systematic comparison of all such factors.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Recommendation or feed-ranking systems can causally change what users see, even when users’ followed accounts and the available content remain unchanged.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

These claims are directly supported by randomized or researcher-controlled feed interventions, although some interventions replaced or simulated platform ranking rather than testing every platform’s naturally occurring production algorithm.

**Evidence**

- The X reranking experiment changed the ordering and prominence of posts while preserving users’ followed accounts, the available posts, and the underlying content. [S4] [S5] [S7]
- A randomized Facebook/Instagram experiment replacing machine-learning ranking with reverse-chronological feeds substantially changed political-content exposure, including the amount of political and untrustworthy content and, on Facebook, exposure to moderate friends and ideologically mixed sources. [S6]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

The supplied evidence documents ideological separation in political-news exposure and interaction, but observational evidence cannot isolate the contribution of algorithmic ranking from users’ own choices.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The observational analysis is large and directly documents exposure and engagement patterns, but follows multiple stages at which user follows, clicks, and interactions can shape what is seen or consumed.

**Evidence**

- An analysis of approximately 208 million U.S. Facebook adults found limited overlap between liberal and conservative political-news consumption and increasing ideological segregation from algorithmic selection to user exposure and then interaction. [S8]
- The same analysis found asymmetric political-news engagement, with conservatives interacting with political news more often than liberals, but the evidence does not identify algorithmic ranking as the cause of these later-stage differences. [S8]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

Changing algorithmic exposure can sometimes produce short-term changes in affective or attitudinal polarization, but the effects are not uniform or consistently attributable to algorithmic selection itself.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The German study is directly described in the supplied article abstract, and multiple supplied accounts report the X result. The effects are nevertheless limited to particular topics, samples, platforms, and short timeframes; the X evidence is supplied partly through institutional or commentary sources rather than the primary report.

**Evidence**

- In two German quasi-experiments, exposure to like-minded arguments increased attitude and affective polarization more than exposure to opposing arguments; algorithmic selection generally did not amplify those effects, though it produced a small additional attitude-polarization effect for one topic. [S1]
- In a one-week randomized X reranking experiment involving 1,256 U.S. users during the 2024 presidential campaign, increasing exposure to antidemocratic attitudes and partisan animosity made opposing-party evaluations approximately 2.48 points cooler, while reducing such exposure made them approximately 2.11 points warmer on a 100-point scale. [S4] [S5] [S7]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

Substantial algorithmically induced changes in exposure do not necessarily produce measurable average changes in political attitudes.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The null downstream result is directly tied to a randomized three-month experiment, while the review provides a broader interpretive framework. The result does not establish that longer-term or subgroup effects never occur.

**Evidence**

- The randomized Facebook/Instagram experiment found substantial changes in political-content exposure after replacing default machine-learning ranking with chronological ordering, but no significant changes in issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, or other key attitudes over three months. [S6]
- The supplied review characterizes the relationship between algorithmic mechanisms and polarization as non-straightforward and suggests that algorithms often reinforce pre-existing social drivers rather than acting independently of users and broader social dynamics. [S3]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

The overall causal chain is better established for exposure than for durable political beliefs or behavior: the supplied studies show ranking and exposure effects, limited short-term attitude effects in some settings, and no established effects on durable beliefs or offline political behavior.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

This is the latest qualified wording for C5. It preserves mixed downstream findings and does not convert the absence of demonstrated effects into proof that durable or behavioral effects cannot occur.

**Evidence**

- The evidence distinguishes feed-ranking and exposure changes from downstream outcomes; some short-term interventions changed affective or attitudinal polarization, while the Facebook/Instagram experiment found no significant changes in key political attitudes over three months. [S1] [S5] [S6] [S7]
- The supplied studies do not provide direct evidence about voting, political participation, contacting politicians, sharing as a political behavior, or other offline activity, and they do not establish durable belief change. [S1] [S3] [S5] [S6] [S7]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

The literature reaches different conclusions because the studies manipulate or observe different stages of the causal process and use different platforms, interventions, populations, outcomes, and time horizons.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The supplied evidence supports these methodological and contextual contrasts, but the open gap remains because the sources do not provide a systematic comparison of polarization definitions, intervention intensity, sample selection, and platform algorithms across the full literature.

**Evidence**

- The supplied evidence compares a three-month Facebook/Instagram ranking intervention with a one-week X reranking intervention and two German panel quasi-experiments; these studies differ in platform, country, campaign context, topics, intervention design, and duration. [S1] [S5] [S6] [S7]
- The large Facebook observational analysis measures selection, exposure, and interaction, but cannot separate algorithmic ranking from user behavior, whereas the randomized and quasi-experimental studies provide stronger causal leverage but study narrower interventions and samples. [S1] [S6] [S8]
- The review describes feedback between algorithmic mechanisms and pre-existing social drivers, supporting the possibility that user preferences and social dynamics moderate or mediate algorithmic effects. [S3]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- The evidence is substantively mixed rather than uniformly positive or null: the X reranking experiment and the German experiments report some short-term affective or attitudinal effects, while the three-month Facebook/Instagram experiment reports no significant average changes in key political attitudes. These results should not be collapsed into a single estimate because they concern different interventions, platforms, populations, outcomes, and timeframes. [S1] [S4] [S5] [S6] [S7]
- Observational evidence shows ideological segregation in political-news exposure and interaction, but cannot establish that recommendation ranking caused the segregation independently of follows, clicks, searches, and other user choices. [S8]

## Remaining Gaps

- The supplied evidence does not adequately quantify changes in the quantity, ideological diversity, source mix, or slant of ordinary users’ political exposure across major platforms while separating algorithmic ranking from users’ own choices.
- No supplied source directly examines algorithm-driven effects on voting, political participation, contacting politicians, or other offline political behavior; durable belief change remains unresolved.
- Causal attitude evidence is limited to short interventions and specific contexts, especially a German panel study and a one-week U.S. X experiment; generalizability across platforms, countries, populations, issues, and longer time horizons is unresolved.
- The supplied sources do not permit a systematic comparison of polarization definitions, outcome measures, intervention intensity, sample selection, and platform algorithms across observational, experimental, and quasi-experimental studies.
- The research process stopped because of the maximum-iteration limit. The counter-search for durable beliefs and political behavior produced S8, but that source added observational exposure and engagement evidence rather than resolving the behavioral gap.

## Conclusion

To the extent shown by the supplied ledger, recommendation algorithms contribute reliably to political polarization at the exposure stage: controlled changes to ranking can alter which political content is prominent, how much of it users encounter, and in some cases its ideological or source mix. Evidence that these exposure changes alter political beliefs or behavior is weaker and mixed. Some short-term interventions changed affective or attitudinal polarization, but another substantial three-month intervention found no significant average changes in key political attitudes. Durable belief change and political behavior effects are not established. The most defensible synthesis is therefore neither that algorithms are harmless nor that they are a general, sufficient cause of polarization: they can shape the informational environment and may amplify polarization under particular conditions, while downstream effects depend on platform, users, content, intervention, outcome, and time horizon.
