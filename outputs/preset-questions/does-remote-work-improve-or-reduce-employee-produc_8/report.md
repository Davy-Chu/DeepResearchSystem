# Research Report

## Research Question

Does remote work improve or reduce employee productivity? Compare evidence from controlled experiments, observational studies, and company-level data, and explain why different studies reach conflicting conclusions.

## Summary

The best evidence does not support a universal conclusion that remote work either raises or lowers productivity. Effects depend strongly on the job, the degree of remote work, employee selection, management practices, and how productivity is measured. Controlled experiments generally find that remote work can improve measured output in highly measurable, individual-oriented jobs, while hybrid work often preserves productivity and improves retention. Observational studies are more mixed: some find productivity losses, especially when remote work is imposed abruptly, coordination-intensive, or poorly managed; others find gains when employees have autonomy and jobs are suitable for remote work. Company-level evidence is noisy and often confounded by changes in demand, workforce composition, technology, and office policy.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

In controlled experiments, fully remote work can increase productivity in jobs with individually measurable output, but the result is not generalizable to all occupations.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The study was randomized and measured actual performance, but it covered one company and one occupational setting.

**Evidence**

- In a randomized experiment at Ctrip, a Chinese travel agency call center, employees assigned to work from home for nine months achieved about 13% higher performance, largely through more minutes worked and fewer breaks, and attrition was roughly halved.
- The setting involved call-center work with highly observable output and relatively limited dependence on face-to-face collaboration, conditions that favor remote productivity gains.

### Finding 2

**Claim**

Hybrid work appears capable of maintaining performance while improving retention, at least for knowledge workers whose jobs can be performed partly remotely.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The randomized design and large sample provide strong causal evidence, although the sample consisted mainly of educated employees in a technology/travel-services company and tested a specific hybrid schedule.

**Evidence**

- A large randomized trial at Trip.com assigned thousands of employees to either hybrid work or full-time office work. Hybrid employees showed no detectable decline in performance reviews, promotion rates, or code output, while resignations fell by approximately one-third.
- The experiment suggests that two remote days per week can produce retention benefits without the productivity penalty observed by some employers when remote work is treated as fully detached from office collaboration.

### Finding 3

**Claim**

Other controlled or quasi-controlled studies find remote-work productivity gains, especially where workers benefit from geographic flexibility and have clear individual output measures.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

These studies use strong institutional or policy variation, but not all are pure randomized experiments and the occupations have unusually measurable individual output.

**Evidence**

- A study of the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office found that a work-from-anywhere policy increased examiner productivity by roughly 4.4%, while also improving worker retention and geographic flexibility.
- A randomized or policy-based study of call-center employees found that working from home increased measured productivity, but remote workers received fewer promotions, indicating that productivity and career outcomes can diverge.

### Finding 4

**Claim**

Observational studies often report productivity losses when remote work is introduced during disruption, especially when hours rise without a proportional increase in output.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The study uses rich administrative data, but the pandemic involved simultaneous shocks to health, childcare, demand, routines, and technology, making the estimated effect specific to crisis conditions.

**Evidence**

- Using detailed records from an Indian information-technology company during the pandemic, researchers found that working from home reduced productivity by roughly 8% to 19%, depending on the specification; employees worked longer hours, but output per hour fell.
- The same study found that coordination and communication consumed more time under remote work, helping explain why longer working hours did not translate into higher productivity.

### Finding 5

**Claim**

Observational evidence also indicates that remote work can impair collaboration and innovation even when individual task completion is unaffected.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The dataset is large and behaviorally detailed, but collaboration patterns are proxies rather than direct measures of productivity.

**Evidence**

- Analysis of Microsoft collaboration data found that remote work made workers' communication networks more static and siloed, with fewer cross-group connections and less synchronous interaction.
- The study did not directly establish lower output or profits, but it identifies a mechanism through which remote work could reduce knowledge sharing, mentoring, creativity, and long-run organizational productivity.

### Finding 6

**Claim**

Surveys and broad observational datasets usually find a smaller average effect than simple claims of either a large productivity gain or a large productivity loss.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

Survey evidence covers broad populations but is vulnerable to self-reporting, question wording, selection, and differences between perceived and measured productivity.

**Evidence**

- The Survey of Working Arrangements and Attitudes and related U.S. research find that employees and managers generally perceive working from home as modestly less productive than office work, but the gap is small and varies sharply by occupation and worker circumstances.
- Post-pandemic evidence shows that employers have largely settled on hybrid arrangements rather than either universal remote work or a complete return to the office, consistent with heterogeneous effects rather than one dominant productivity result.

### Finding 7

**Claim**

Company-level productivity data do not provide a clean verdict because firms changed many things at once and commonly measure output imperfectly.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The identification problem is well established: firm-level before-and-after comparisons lack a credible counterfactual unless work location was assigned or changed for reasons unrelated to expected productivity.

**Evidence**

- Firm-level studies and company reports have documented both stable/increasing output under remote or hybrid work and declines in innovation, coordination, or speed of execution. These outcomes often coexist within the same company across teams.
- Aggregate productivity statistics during and after the pandemic reflect industry composition, labor hoarding, supply constraints, demand shifts, digitization, and employee turnover in addition to work location; they therefore cannot identify the causal effect of remote work by themselves.

### Finding 8

**Claim**

The main reason studies conflict is that they estimate different treatment effects for different people, jobs, schedules, and periods rather than estimating one common effect.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

These mechanisms are supported by randomized findings, administrative studies, and consistent patterns across occupations, though their exact magnitudes vary.

**Evidence**

- Remote work is more favorable for focused, individually measurable tasks and less favorable for tasks requiring frequent coordination, rapid informal feedback, physical equipment, apprenticeship, or creative interaction.
- Voluntary remote work differs from involuntary pandemic remote work: employees who choose remote work may have suitable homes, strong self-management, and jobs compatible with distance, whereas forced remote work can include childcare disruption, inadequate equipment, and organizational dislocation.
- Full-time remote work and hybrid work are different interventions. Hybrid work can preserve face-to-face coordination while reducing commuting and improving retention, which helps explain why the Trip.com experiment found no output penalty even though some full-remote studies found losses.

### Finding 9

**Claim**

Measurement choices substantially affect the apparent productivity effect.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Different operational definitions produce different estimates, and several important organizational outcomes are difficult to observe over short study windows.

**Evidence**

- Call volume, completed patent applications, software commits, and hours worked capture short-run individual output, but may miss mentoring, innovation, quality, customer relationships, and coordination costs.
- Some studies count longer hours as evidence of productivity while others calculate output per hour; remote work can raise total output but reduce productivity per hour, or preserve current output while increasing future coordination or innovation costs.

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Controlled call-center and patent-examiner studies find productivity gains, while the Indian IT study finds productivity losses. The difference may reflect occupation, management quality, pandemic disruption, and whether work is individually measurable.
- The Microsoft collaboration study documents more siloed communication but does not prove that company-wide productivity fell. Collaboration proxies should not be treated as direct output measures.
- Employee and manager perceptions sometimes disagree, and perceived productivity may not match administrative performance data.
- The strongest hybrid experiment tested one schedule and one company. It cannot establish that every hybrid policy, industry, or managerial system will have the same effect.
- Long-run effects on innovation, onboarding, promotion equity, and organizational learning remain less precisely estimated than short-run individual output.

## Remaining Gaps

- More multi-company randomized trials across industries, occupations, and countries are needed.
- Longer follow-up is needed for innovation, mentoring, promotion, employee development, and organizational learning.
- Studies should distinguish output per hour from total output and measure quality, rework, customer outcomes, and coordination costs.
- Evidence is still limited on how remote work affects less experienced employees, new hires, managers, and workers with unequal home-office conditions.
- More evidence is needed on how office attendance mandates affect productivity through selection, morale, attrition, and recruiting.

## Conclusion

Remote work neither universally improves nor universally reduces productivity. The most defensible conclusion is conditional: full-time remote work often improves short-run individual output in jobs with clear, measurable tasks and substantial commuting or distraction costs, but can reduce productivity in coordination-heavy work or when introduced abruptly without adequate systems. Hybrid work has the strongest evidence of being productivity-neutral on average while improving retention. Conflicting findings arise because studies examine different occupations, worker populations, schedules, periods, and outcome measures. Employers should therefore evaluate remote-work policies by job family and use broad metrics—quality, output per hour, retention, collaboration, innovation, and promotion—not hours online or office attendance alone.
