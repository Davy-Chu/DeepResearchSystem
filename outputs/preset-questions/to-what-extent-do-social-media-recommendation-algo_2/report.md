# Research Report

## Research Question

To what extent do social-media recommendation algorithms contribute to political polarization? Separate evidence that algorithms change what users see from evidence that they actually change political beliefs or behavior, and explain why the literature reaches different conclusions.

## Summary

The supplied evidence strongly supports that recommendation and ranking algorithms change users’ political exposure and on-platform experiences. Evidence that these changes produce political polarization is mixed: some experiments find modest, short-term shifts in attitudes or affective feelings, while larger randomized feed interventions find substantial exposure changes without significant changes in measured political attitudes. The evidence is insufficient to determine whether algorithmic curation produces durable changes in political behavior, including voting, participation, sharing, or offline action. Differences in platforms, interventions, content, outcomes, time horizons, and the difficulty of separating algorithmic effects from user-driven selective exposure help explain the divergent findings.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Recommendation and ranking algorithms can substantially change what political content users see or encounter first, even when the underlying pool of posts or followed accounts is unchanged.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Direct experimental evidence consistently shows that feed-ranking and recommendation interventions alter exposure and on-platform experience.

**Evidence**

- Experiments directly manipulated whether political arguments were algorithmically or randomly selected, demonstrating that curation changes exposure. [S1]
- A browser-extension intervention reranked existing posts in X feeds in real time, changing their order while leaving the same posts available. [S5]
- A randomized Facebook/Instagram intervention replaced algorithmic ranking with chronological ordering and changed the amount and type of political, ideological, uncivil, and other content users encountered. [S6]
- Summaries of the Facebook/Instagram intervention report substantial exposure changes after users moved from default algorithmic feeds to reverse-chronological feeds. [S8] [S9]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

Evidence that algorithmic curation changes political attitudes or polarization is positive in some settings but modest and context-dependent, rather than consistently large or general.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The ledger preserves both positive findings from shorter or more targeted interventions and null findings from a large randomized feed intervention. Together they support a bounded, context-dependent conclusion rather than a uniform claim that algorithms strongly polarize users.

**Evidence**

- Two experiments found that exposure to like-minded arguments increased attitude and affective polarization relative to opposing arguments, but algorithmic selection generally did not amplify those exposure effects; it produced only a slight additional attitude-polarization effect for one topic. [S1]
- A reported X reranking experiment found approximately two-point shifts in feelings toward the opposing party after one week when exposure to antidemocratic and partisan-animosity content was increased or reduced. [S4] [S5]
- In contrast, a randomized Facebook/Instagram experiment found substantial algorithm-induced changes in exposure, time spent, and activity without significant changes over three months in issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, or other key attitudes. [S6] [S8] [S9]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

Some evidence indicates that algorithmic ranking can shift affective political feelings without users consciously noticing the intervention, but this evidence is limited to one reported experiment and does not establish effects on all political beliefs or behaviors.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The two sources report the same experiment rather than independent replications, and the finding concerns perceived experience and affective feelings rather than broad or durable political change.

**Evidence**

- The reported X experiment found that most participants did not perceive an impact on their experience despite changed exposure and measurable shifts in partisan feelings. [S4] [S5]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

The supplied evidence does not establish that recommendation algorithms produce durable changes in political behavior, such as voting, participation, political sharing, following, or offline political action.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The available evidence either reports null off-platform effects in one bounded intervention or concerns general social-media use and correlational associations. It does not provide sufficient causal evidence about algorithm-specific, durable political behavior effects.

**Evidence**

- The three-month Facebook/Instagram intervention did not notably affect reported off-platform behaviors, but this does not resolve all forms of political behavior or longer-term effects. [S8]
- A review characterizes most evidence linking social-media use to offline civic participation as correlational and presents algorithmic pathways to offline participation as mechanisms for future research rather than established causal findings. [S10]
- A cross-national study reports associations between general social-media use and online political participation but does not isolate recommendation algorithms or establish algorithm-caused behavior. [S11]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

The literature reaches different conclusions partly because algorithmic curation and user-driven selective exposure are difficult to disentangle; users’ preferences and engagement influence recommendations, while recommendations may reinforce those existing tendencies.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The sources directly describe personalization, user choice, network composition, and reciprocal algorithm–user influence as causal complications.

**Evidence**

- The experimental study identifies failure to separate exposure to like-minded content from effects caused specifically by algorithms as a source of inconsistent prior evidence. [S1]
- The review describes a feedback loop between social drivers and algorithmic mechanisms and concludes that algorithms often reinforce existing social drivers, making their effects on polarization non-linear or difficult to isolate. [S3]
- Recommendations use signals such as likes, shares, comments, watch time, follows, searches, and prior engagement, linking platform curation to user behavior. [S2]
- The randomized feed study notes that personalization, users’ choices, and network composition are alternative determinants of what users see, complicating the counterfactual attribution of effects to ranking algorithms alone. [S6]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Evidence conflicts over whether changing algorithmic feeds changes political attitudes. S1 and S4/S5 report modest attitude or affective-polarization shifts in targeted or short interventions, whereas S6, summarized by S8 and S9, reports large exposure changes but no significant changes in key political attitudes over three months. [S1] [S4] [S5] [S6] [S8] [S9]
- The positive affective findings are not directly comparable with the null findings because the studies differ in platform, ranking intervention, content being increased or reduced, participant setting, outcomes, and duration. The ledger does not establish which design feature explains the difference. [S1] [S4] [S5] [S6]
- The evidence on political behavior is insufficient: the available intervention evidence includes limited null findings for off-platform behavior, while other sources are correlational or theoretical and do not identify algorithm-specific causal effects. [S8] [S10] [S11]

## Remaining Gaps

- Whether algorithm-induced exposure changes or affective-attitude shifts translate into voting, political participation, sharing, following, or offline political action remains unresolved.
- The durability and population-level scale of observed attitude and affective-polarization effects remain unresolved because the supplied experiments examine short interventions or bounded study periods rather than long-term outcomes.
- The supplied evidence does not permit systematic comparison across platforms, countries, demographic groups, or recommendation designs, so generalizability beyond the studied settings remains unresolved.

## Conclusion

Recommendation algorithms clearly contribute to political polarization in the limited sense that they alter the political material users encounter, including its composition and ordering. Their contribution to changed political beliefs or polarization is more limited and conditional: some studies find modest short-term shifts, especially in affective feelings or under particular content manipulations, while a major randomized feed intervention found no significant attitude changes despite substantial exposure changes. The supplied evidence does not support a reliable conclusion that recommendation algorithms cause durable political behavior changes. Divergent results most plausibly reflect differences in interventions, platforms, content, outcomes, time horizons, and the reciprocal relationship between user preferences and algorithmic ranking.
