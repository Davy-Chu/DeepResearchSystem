# Social-media recommendation algorithms and political polarization

## Executive conclusion

Social-media recommendation algorithms clearly influence **what political content users encounter**, but the evidence that they substantially change **political beliefs, partisan identities, or political behavior** is much weaker and more mixed.

The best-supported conclusions are:

1. **Algorithms reshape exposure.** Ranking systems amplify some posts, sources, and topics while reducing others. They often increase the visibility of emotionally engaging, partisan, or out-group-hostile material. This effect is real, although its size varies by platform, user, topic, and definition of “polarization.”

2. **Users’ choices remain at least as important as ranking.** People self-select into platforms, communities, accounts, and conversations. In many studies, user preferences and networks explain more of the ideological composition of feeds than the recommendation system itself.

3. **Exposure does not automatically produce persuasion.** Seeing more like-minded or hostile material can reinforce attitudes, but it can also produce resistance, fatigue, disengagement, or counter-mobilization. Political beliefs are shaped by identity, social networks, news, offline experience, and elite cues—not by feeds alone.

4. **Large randomized studies generally find modest or null short-term effects on political attitudes.** Major platform experiments around the 2020 U.S. election found that changing Facebook’s ranking system or moving users toward chronological feeds substantially changed what they saw, but did not measurably change most political attitudes over the study period.

5. **The strongest plausible effect may be indirect and cumulative.** Algorithms can help create communities, norms, and information environments that facilitate radicalization, affective polarization, harassment, or political mobilization. These longer-term effects are harder to identify than immediate attitude change and remain insufficiently measured.

Thus, algorithms are best understood as **important agenda-setting and exposure-shaping institutions, but not as a single dominant cause of polarization**. They contribute to polarization under some conditions, especially by amplifying identity-relevant and antagonistic content, but the literature does not support the simple claim that recommendation algorithms broadly convert politically moderate users into extremists.

---

## 1. What does “political polarization” mean?

The literature often uses the term to describe different phenomena:

- **Ideological polarization:** greater distance between policy preferences.
- **Affective polarization:** increased dislike, distrust, or hostility toward members of the opposing party.
- **Perceived polarization:** believing that political opponents are more extreme than they actually are.
- **Social or network polarization:** separation into ideologically homogeneous communities.
- **Behavioral polarization:** partisan voting, political participation, protest, donation, sharing, or hostility.
- **Epistemic polarization:** divergent beliefs about facts, institutions, or election legitimacy.

Algorithms could affect these outcomes through different pathways. A system that increases exposure to partisan news may alter perceived polarization without changing policy preferences. A system that promotes inflammatory material may increase affective hostility without changing voting behavior. A system that recommends activist communities may influence participation without causing ideological conversion.

A major source of disagreement in the literature is that studies sometimes treat these outcomes as interchangeable.

# Part I: Evidence that algorithms change what users see

## 2. Ranking systems measurably alter exposure

Most major platforms do not display posts in simple chronological order. Their systems rank content using predicted engagement, relevance, past interactions, social connections, watch time, and other signals. As a result, users’ feeds are partly personalized and partly socially constructed.

Research consistently finds that algorithmic ranking changes:

- which news sources users encounter;
- how often they see posts from ideologically similar or dissimilar users;
- the prominence of emotionally arousing and antagonistic content;
- the political topics that become salient;
- opportunities for cross-cutting exposure;
- the spread of false, misleading, or low-quality content.

### Facebook

Bakshy, Messing, and Adamic’s influential study of Facebook users during the 2014 U.S. midterm period found that both **user choice** and **algorithmic ranking** limited exposure to ideologically cross-cutting news. The study estimated that users’ own choices explained more of the reduction in cross-cutting exposure than Facebook’s ranking algorithm, although ranking still had a measurable effect (Bakshy, Messing, and Adamic 2015).

This finding is important because it undermines two overly simple accounts:

- “The algorithm creates ideological bubbles from otherwise neutral users.”
- “Algorithms do nothing; users alone determine their feeds.”

The evidence instead suggests a joint process: people preferentially follow and click on congenial sources, and ranking systems further prioritize content likely to generate engagement.

Flaxman, Goel, and Rao similarly found that social media and search engines were associated with greater ideological segregation in news exposure, but also with increased exposure to some opposing views. Their results did not support a universal “echo chamber” effect; the relationship depended on the platform and the user’s existing news habits (Flaxman, Goel, and Rao 2016).

### Twitter/X

Research on Twitter has found that algorithmic timelines can amplify certain political messages relative to chronological presentation. Huszár and colleagues conducted a large-scale analysis of Twitter’s ranking system and found that political content from right-leaning actors was, on average, amplified more than content from left-leaning actors in several countries, including the United States. The study measured visibility and engagement, not persuasion, and did not establish that the amplification was intentionally ideological or that it changed users’ votes (Huszár et al. 2022).

Other studies show that Twitter networks are strongly organized by ideology. Partisan users tend to follow, retweet, and interact with people who share their views. This can produce highly segregated information flows even before ranking algorithms intervene.

### YouTube

YouTube’s recommendation system has attracted particular attention because it can guide users from mainstream political videos toward increasingly partisan, conspiratorial, or extremist content. Studies have documented recommendation pathways containing such material, especially for users already interested in related topics.

However, the size and generality of this effect are contested. Some audits found evidence of rabbit-hole-like sequences; others found that recommendations often lead to popular mainstream content or reflect users’ prior searches and subscriptions. A user who watches conspiratorial videos may receive more conspiratorial recommendations, but this does not show that recommendations caused the initial radicalization or that watching the recommendations changed the user’s beliefs.

The distinction is crucial:

\[
\text{Recommendation of extreme content} \neq \text{conversion to extreme beliefs}
\]

### Instagram, TikTok, and newer platforms

Evidence on TikTok and Instagram suggests that recommendation systems can rapidly expose users to political content, including content from accounts they do not follow. Short-video systems may be especially powerful agenda setters because they combine high-frequency exposure with personalization and emotionally vivid audiovisual material.

But causal evidence concerning political persuasion is still limited. Many studies are content audits or observational analyses. They show what systems recommend, not whether recommendations alter political attitudes or behavior.

---

## 3. Algorithms can amplify emotional and hostile content

A recurring finding is that engagement-based systems tend to favor content that provokes strong reactions. Political anger, moral outrage, fear, ridicule, and group-based hostility are often more likely to generate comments, reshares, and prolonged attention than nuanced or ambiguous material.

Research by Brady and colleagues found that moral-emotional language increased diffusion on Twitter, particularly within users’ own political networks (Brady et al. 2017). Other work has shown that out-group language is especially effective at generating engagement.

This creates a plausible mechanism for polarization:

1. Political and identity-based content produces strong emotional responses.
2. Strong responses generate engagement.
3. Engagement increases algorithmic distribution.
4. Increased distribution raises the visibility and perceived prevalence of hostile content.
5. Repeated exposure may normalize antagonism and intensify group conflict.

Yet even here, two qualifications matter.

First, platforms rank what users are likely to engage with; they do not independently invent all of the demand for outrage. Political elites, activist organizations, media outlets, and users deliberately produce antagonistic content.

Second, greater visibility of hostile content does not necessarily produce greater hostility. Some users may become mobilized, some may become more polarized, and others may disengage or reject the content.

# Part II: Evidence that algorithms change political beliefs or behavior

## 4. Observational evidence is suggestive but difficult to interpret

A large body of observational research finds that heavy social-media use is associated with:

- greater affective polarization;
- more extreme political attitudes;
- belief in misinformation;
- partisan hostility;
- political activism;
- distrust in opposing groups.

These associations are consistent with an algorithmic effect, but they do not establish one.

Several alternative explanations are possible:

- politically extreme people use social media more intensely;
- people choose platforms and communities that match their views;
- politically engaged users are more likely to share and comment;
- offline polarization causes both online behavior and political attitudes;
- political campaigns target people who are already receptive;
- users who see hostile content may be responding to events rather than being changed by them.

This is a classic selection problem. If people who already dislike the opposing party choose partisan communities, researchers may incorrectly infer that those communities caused their hostility.

---

## 5. Experiments changing exposure generally find small or mixed attitude effects

The strongest evidence comes from experiments that alter users’ feeds or temporarily remove access to a platform.

### Facebook’s 2020 election studies

A large collaboration between Meta and independent academic researchers conducted randomized interventions involving tens of thousands of U.S. Facebook and Instagram users during the 2020 election campaign. The studies changed aspects of the Facebook News Feed, including:

- switching some users from algorithmic ranking to chronological ordering;
- reducing reshared content;
- altering exposure to political content;
- temporarily deactivating Facebook or Instagram;
- changing exposure to like-minded or cross-cutting sources.

The results were notable because the interventions changed users’ information environments substantially. Users saw different content, encountered different sources, and engaged in different ways. Yet the interventions generally produced **little or no measurable short-term change in core political attitudes**, including measures related to affective polarization, ideological beliefs, and political knowledge, depending on the particular study.

For example, Guess and colleagues reported that switching from algorithmic to chronological Facebook feeds changed the composition of users’ exposure and reduced some forms of political engagement, but did not significantly alter most political attitudes during the study period. Other studies in the same project found that reducing resharing changed the amount and type of content users encountered without producing large changes in political views.

These studies do not show that algorithms never matter. They show that a major alteration to one platform’s feed, over a limited period, did not produce large immediate changes in measured political attitudes.

### Deactivation experiments

Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer, and Gentzkow randomly assigned some Facebook users to deactivate their accounts for four weeks before the 2018 U.S. midterm election. Deactivation reduced Facebook use, political knowledge, and political activity, while improving some measures of well-being. The study did not find strong evidence of large changes in political polarization or voting-related outcomes over that period (Allcott et al. 2020).

The interpretation is mixed:

- Facebook clearly affected information access and participation.
- But removing Facebook temporarily did not substantially transform partisan attitudes.
- The experiment may have been too short to detect cumulative effects.
- Users could obtain political information elsewhere.
- Deactivation affected the entire platform, not a precisely isolated recommendation mechanism.

### Twitter deactivation and political attitudes

A more recent randomized deactivation study involving Twitter users found that suspending use for several weeks affected political knowledge, news consumption, and some political attitudes, but the effects were not uniformly large and depended on the outcome being measured. Deactivation studies also cannot cleanly distinguish the effect of recommendations from the effects of social connection, posting, news exposure, and interpersonal interaction.

The overall pattern is that social-media use can influence some beliefs and behaviors, but the average effect of changing feed exposure is usually smaller and less consistent than the rhetoric surrounding “algorithmic radicalization” suggests.

---

## 6. Cross-cutting exposure can sometimes increase polarization

It would be a mistake to assume that exposure to opposing views necessarily reduces polarization.

Bail and colleagues randomly exposed Twitter users to messages from opposing political elites. Rather than moderating attitudes, exposure sometimes increased polarization, particularly among users who were strongly identified with their political group (Bail et al. 2018).

Possible explanations include:

- **motivated reasoning:** people counterargue information from hostile sources;
- **identity threat:** opposing messages reinforce group boundaries;
- **reactance:** users resist content they perceive as imposed or hostile;
- **selective interpretation:** the same message is read differently by different groups.

This finding helps explain why algorithms that increase cross-cutting exposure may not depolarize users. The effects of exposure depend on source credibility, tone, prior beliefs, social identity, and whether users choose the exposure voluntarily.

---

## 7. Algorithms may affect behavior more readily than beliefs

Political behavior includes more than voting preferences. Recommendation systems may affect:

- whether users encounter a protest or campaign;
- whether they donate or sign a petition;
- whether they share political content;
- whether they join a group;
- whether they contact politicians;
- whether they participate in harassment or collective action;
- whether they abstain from political discussion.

Behavioral effects may occur without persuasion. A user can be mobilized by content that confirms existing beliefs. In this sense, algorithms may be more influential as **mobilization and coordination systems** than as engines of ideological conversion.

For example, ranking can determine which political event becomes visible, which claim receives social proof, or which outrage episode becomes salient. This may change participation even when people’s underlying preferences remain stable.

The evidence for such effects is strongest for online behaviors—viewing, sharing, commenting, following, and joining—and weaker for offline actions such as voting. Algorithms can change the probability that users perform a political action, but translating that into election outcomes is difficult because effects are distributed across millions of users and campaigns operate through many channels.

# Part III: Why the literature reaches different conclusions

## 8. Different platforms use different mechanisms

“Social media” is not one information environment.

- **Facebook:** combines social-network posts, news, groups, and engagement-based ranking.
- **Twitter/X:** emphasizes public, real-time political discourse and retweet cascades.
- **YouTube:** recommends videos based heavily on viewing histories and watch time.
- **TikTok:** uses highly personalized short-video recommendations and often exposes users to content from accounts they do not follow.
- **Instagram:** blends social connections, influencers, short video, and visual identity.

An algorithm that ranks posts from known friends may have different effects from one that recommends unfamiliar videos. Findings from Facebook in 2020 should not automatically be generalized to YouTube or TikTok.

---

## 9. Exposure, attention, persuasion, and behavior are different stages

A platform may affect one stage but not another:

\[
\text{Exposure} \rightarrow \text{Attention} \rightarrow \text{Interpretation} \rightarrow \text{Belief} \rightarrow \text{Behavior}
\]

Evidence is strongest at the first stage and weaker at each subsequent stage.

Algorithms can:

- make a claim visible without making users believe it;
- make a source salient without making it credible;
- increase anger without changing policy preferences;
- mobilize already committed users without persuading moderates;
- increase perceptions of polarization without increasing actual ideological distance.

Studies that measure only exposure will tend to find large effects. Studies that measure beliefs several weeks later may find small effects because many intervening influences dilute the initial exposure.

---

## 10. User selection and algorithmic selection are difficult to separate

Users do not passively receive feeds. They:

- choose whom to follow;
- decide what to click;
- mute or block accounts;
- join groups;
- search for political material;
- share content;
- produce engagement signals that train the ranking system.

Consequently, observed ideological segregation may reflect:

\[
\text{Total segregation} =
\text{user choice} + \text{algorithmic ranking} + \text{network structure} + \text{content supply}
\]

Studies using platform data often cannot observe all four components. Even when researchers observe that extreme content is overrepresented, they may not know whether the system promoted it or whether users actively sought it.

Randomized feed experiments solve part of this problem, but they are expensive, platform-specific, and usually short-term.

---

## 11. “Algorithmic amplification” is not the same as “algorithmic persuasion”

An algorithm may amplify a message because it predicts engagement. This can increase the message’s reach without increasing its persuasive power.

A useful distinction is:

- **Distribution effect:** more people see a message.
- **Attention effect:** people spend more time with it.
- **Interpretation effect:** people understand or frame it differently.
- **Persuasion effect:** people change their beliefs.
- **Behavioral effect:** people act differently.

Most public debate jumps from the first claim to the fourth. The empirical literature usually supports the first more strongly than the fourth.

---

## 12. Effects may be heterogeneous rather than average

Average treatment effects can conceal important subgroup effects. Algorithms may affect:

- highly engaged partisans more than politically inattentive users;
- young users more than older users;
- people with weak prior beliefs more than committed partisans;
- users exposed to local political content more than national content;
- users who consume video more than text;
- people in isolated communities more than those with diverse offline networks.

A study may find no average change in polarization even if a small but politically consequential subgroup becomes more radicalized. Conversely, a small average effect may be socially important if it affects political violence, conspiracy communities, or election administration.

At present, the evidence for large subgroup effects is plausible but not yet sufficiently measured across platforms.

---

## 13. Short experiments may miss cumulative and network effects

Most randomized studies last days or weeks. But polarization may develop through:

- repeated exposure over months or years;
- migration into increasingly extreme communities;
- friendship and follower-network changes;
- normalization of hostility;
- learning that opposing groups are dangerous;
- feedback between online and offline politics.

A short-term experiment can find no change in attitudes while still missing long-term processes. On the other hand, long-term observational studies face stronger confounding. This creates a tradeoff:

- **Experiments:** stronger causal identification, limited duration.
- **Longitudinal observation:** longer time horizon, weaker causal identification.
- **Platform audits:** excellent exposure data, limited evidence about individual change.

No single design resolves the entire question.

---

## 14. Measurement choices produce different conclusions

Studies reach different results partly because they measure different outcomes.

A study may report “polarization” using:

- feeling thermometers;
- willingness to date or work with an opposing partisan;
- ideological self-placement;
- policy preferences;
- factual beliefs;
- partisan news exposure;
- hostile language;
- network segregation;
- voting or turnout.

These measures need not move together. An algorithm may increase hostile language and partisan sharing while leaving policy positions unchanged. It may increase affective polarization among activists but not among the broader population.

Researchers also disagree about what counts as politically consequential exposure. One incidental partisan video may have little effect; repeated exposure inside a trusted community may have much more.

# Part IV: A synthesis of the evidence

## 15. What can be stated with relatively high confidence?

### High confidence

- Recommendation algorithms change the composition and ordering of users’ feeds.
- They affect the visibility of political content, sources, and topics.
- User preferences and social networks are major contributors to ideological segregation.
- Engagement-oriented systems often favor emotionally arousing and conflictual content.
- Algorithms influence online political engagement, sharing, and attention.

### Moderate confidence

- Algorithms contribute to affective polarization by increasing the visibility and salience of out-group hostility.
- They can intensify polarization among highly engaged or already committed users.
- They can facilitate mobilization, identity formation, and the growth of political communities.
- Platform ranking can amplify misinformation and extreme content under some conditions.

### Low or mixed confidence

- Algorithms broadly convert politically moderate users into extremists.
- Algorithmic ranking is the primary cause of the rise in political polarization.
- Exposure to like-minded content generally changes policy beliefs.
- Switching from algorithmic to chronological feeds substantially reduces polarization.
- Exposure to opposing views generally depolarizes users.
- Effects observed on one platform generalize to all platforms.

---

## 16. The most defensible causal model

The evidence supports a conditional model rather than a universal one:

\[
\text{Algorithmic ranking}
\rightarrow
\text{different exposure and attention}
\rightarrow
\begin{cases}
\text{reinforcement} \\
\text{mobilization} \\
\text{backfire} \\
\text{disengagement} \\
\text{little durable change}
\end{cases}
\]

Which outcome occurs depends on:

- the user’s prior beliefs;
- strength of partisan identity;
- source credibility;
- emotional tone;
- whether exposure is voluntary;
- network context;
- repetition;
- platform design;
- political events;
- offline media and social relationships.

Algorithms therefore act less like ideological “mind-control” systems and more like **selective amplifiers**. They shape the environment in which political judgment occurs, but users and political actors supply much of the content, meaning, and motivation.

---

# 17. Policy and research implications

## Policy implications

The evidence does not justify treating all personalization as inherently harmful. More targeted interventions would focus on:

- transparency about ranking and recommendation criteria;
- independent access to platform data;
- auditing amplification of low-quality and hostile content;
- friction before mass resharing;
- user controls over recommendation systems;
- stronger protections for minors;
- reducing incentives for outrage-based engagement;
- preserving privacy while enabling public-interest research;
- interventions aimed at harassment, incitement, and coordinated manipulation rather than viewpoint suppression.

Chronological feeds alone are unlikely to eliminate polarization because users can still choose partisan sources and networks.

## Research priorities

Future work should:

1. distinguish exposure effects from persuasion effects;
2. study multiple platforms using comparable measures;
3. conduct longer-term randomized or quasi-experimental research;
4. examine subgroup and network effects;
5. measure offline behavior, not only clicks and likes;
6. study political video and short-form recommendation systems;
7. separate affective, ideological, epistemic, and behavioral polarization;
8. improve access to platform data for independent researchers;
9. investigate whether algorithmic changes have effects during crises, elections, or political violence that are absent in ordinary periods.

---

# Conclusion

Social-media recommendation algorithms contribute to political polarization primarily by shaping **visibility, attention, social reinforcement, and political mobilization**. The evidence that they change users’ underlying political beliefs is considerably less conclusive.

The literature reaches different conclusions because it studies different platforms, outcomes, time periods, and populations; because user choice is difficult to separate from algorithmic ranking; and because exposure can produce reinforcement, resistance, mobilization, or no durable effect. Large randomized interventions show that feeds can be changed substantially without producing large immediate changes in political attitudes. At the same time, observational and network evidence indicates that algorithms can amplify hostile, partisan, and emotionally arousing content and may have important cumulative effects for particular groups.

The strongest overall conclusion is therefore:

> Recommendation algorithms are significant contributors to polarized political information environments, but the evidence does not show that they are, by themselves, the main cause of mass political polarization or that they routinely transform users’ political beliefs.

They are best viewed as part of a broader system involving partisan identity, political elites, media markets, social networks, institutional distrust, and offline conflict.
