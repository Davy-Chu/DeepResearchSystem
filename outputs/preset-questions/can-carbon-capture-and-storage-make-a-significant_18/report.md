# Research Report

## Research Question

Can carbon capture and storage make a significant contribution to reducing global CO₂ emissions? Evaluate its demonstrated effectiveness, costs, scalability, energy requirements, and major arguments for and against large-scale deployment.

## Summary

This report summarizes the current landscape of carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies, assessing their effectiveness, costs, scalability, and energy requirements while discussing the major arguments for and against large-scale deployment. Current evidence suggests that while CCS has potential, significant barriers remain.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) has captured and stored less than 0.1% of annual global CO₂ emissions despite over $40 billion investment.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

New evidence supports the idea that capacity increases could bolster CCS effectiveness, yet its current low capture rate remains a concern.

**Evidence**

- CCS has captured less than 0.1% of global emissions, with over $40 billion invested. [S1]
- Studies note that CCS currently captures exceedingly low amounts of CO2 compared to total emissions. [S11]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

The scalability of carbon capture and storage (CCS) is constrained by high costs and limited storage site availability.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

While government incentives can enhance viability, the high costs remain a persistent challenge, demanding further empirical support for broader scalability.

**Evidence**

- High costs and limited storage sites pose significant challenges for CCS deployment. [S2]
- The rapid deployment of carbon capture technologies is hampered by existing high costs. [S6]
- Significant financial incentives from governments, like the U.S. Inflation Reduction Act, create compelling economic viability for CCS despite high costs. [S27]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

The energy requirements for operating carbon capture and storage systems range from 2.5 to 8 GJ/tCO₂.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Additional claims reinforce the energy requirements but need more corroboration.

**Evidence**

- Energy demand for CCS technologies ranges from 2.5 to 8 GJ/tCO₂. [S1]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

Despite the potential effectiveness of CCS, alternatives such as renewables and mineralization may offer higher scalability and fewer risks.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Support for alternatives strengthens against CCS as the main decarbonization strategy.

**Evidence**

- Critics point out that alternatives like renewable energy may provide better solutions than CCS. [S12]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

The costs associated with implementing carbon capture and storage technologies at a large scale are currently high and pose significant barriers to deployment.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The financial analysis reinforces concerns regarding current CCS costs, emphasizing the need for clear evidence on cost reduction strategies.

**Evidence**

- High costs and lack of economic competitiveness hinder widespread adoption of carbon capture technologies. [S6]
- Projected increases in CCS capacity could lower costs due to scale effects and improved technologies. [S16]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) requires a tenfold increase in capacity by 2025 to significantly contribute to reducing global CO₂ emissions.

**Confidence:** Low

**Why this confidence level**

New information raises significant doubts about the viability of scaling CCS, leading to a lower confidence level.

**Evidence**

- The International Energy Agency states a tenfold increase in CCS capacity is needed by 2025. [S11]

### Finding 7

**Claim**

CCS technology is associated with significant environmental risks and requires a capital-intensive infrastructure.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

Emerging evidence strengthens awareness of environmental risks associated with CCS.

**Evidence**

- The implementation of CCS faces significant environmental impacts and infrastructure requirements. [S12]

### Finding 8

**Claim**

CCUS technology is capital-intensive and does not address upstream emissions from fossil fuel extraction.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Consistent evidence reinforces the claim about CCUS's cost issues and its failure to address upstream emissions.

**Evidence**

- CCUS incurs high costs and does not mitigate upstream emissions associated with fossil fuels. [S18]

### Finding 9

**Claim**

Current carbon capture technologies face diminishing returns with increased efficiency demands.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

Acknowledged challenges in achieving higher efficiencies highlight economic concerns.

**Evidence**

- Achieving efficiencies above 90% in CCS incurs greater costs and energy requirements. [S20]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- CCS has never reached its target CO2 capture rate, leading to doubts about its effectiveness. [S26]
- Critics argue that CCS is an expensive and unproven technology that distracts from necessary decarbonization. [S26]
- Despite advancements, many projects remain delayed, emphasizing an ongoing burden of high costs in attaining necessary scale. [S25]

## Remaining Gaps

- Further empirical evidence and case studies on the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of CCS projects are required.

## Conclusion

While carbon capture and storage technologies show potential benefits, substantial barriers, including high costs, scalability issues, and environmental risks, hinder their current effectiveness in reducing global CO₂ emissions. Alternative renewable solutions may offer more reliable pathways to decarbonization.
