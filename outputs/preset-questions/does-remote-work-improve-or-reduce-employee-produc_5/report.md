# Does Remote Work Improve or Reduce Employee Productivity?

## Executive summary

The evidence does **not** support a single answer. Remote work can raise productivity in some settings, reduce it in others, and have little effect in many cases. The most defensible conclusion is:

> **Remote work tends to improve individual productivity when work is measurable, independent, and compatible with a quiet home environment; it tends to reduce productivity when work depends heavily on spontaneous coordination, mentoring, innovation, or strong managerial infrastructure. Hybrid arrangements often perform better than either universal office work or fully remote work, particularly for knowledge workers.**

The strongest causal studies generally find:

- **Fully remote work:** outcomes range from substantially negative to modestly positive, depending on the job and implementation.
- **Hybrid work:** often neutral or positive for productivity and retention.
- **Employee self-reports:** usually favorable, but may overstate output because workers report perceived productivity rather than measured results.
- **Company-level data:** mixed and difficult to interpret because firms changed technology, staffing, processes, and work practices at the same time as they adopted remote work.

Different studies conflict largely because they study different populations, work arrangements, time periods, and definitions of productivity.

---

## 1. What “productivity” means

Productivity is not one outcome. Studies variously measure:

1. **Task output** — calls handled, code produced, claims processed, sales closed.
2. **Quality** — errors, customer satisfaction, rework, defect rates.
3. **Speed or hours** — time per task, working hours, response time.
4. **Innovation and collaboration** — patents, new ideas, cross-team knowledge flow.
5. **Firm productivity** — revenue or value added per worker or per hour.
6. **Employee-reported productivity** — whether workers believe they are more effective.

These measures can move in opposite directions. For example, remote employees may complete more individual tasks while generating fewer new ideas or collaborating less effectively. They may also work longer hours, raising output but not productivity per hour.

A useful distinction is:

\[
\text{Productivity} = \frac{\text{Output}}{\text{Labor input}}
\]

A worker who produces more while working many additional hours is not necessarily more productive.

# 2. Evidence from controlled experiments

Randomized or quasi-randomized studies provide the clearest evidence about causality, although they are often limited to particular jobs.

## 2.1 The Ctrip experiment: fully remote work improved measured performance

The best-known experiment was conducted by Nicholas Bloom and coauthors at Ctrip, a large Chinese travel agency.

### Design

- About 1,000 call-center employees volunteered.
- Roughly half were randomly assigned to work from home for nine months.
- The control group remained in the office.
- The jobs were highly measurable: booking travel and handling calls.

### Findings

The work-from-home group:

- Increased performance by approximately **13%**.
- Worked more minutes per shift, largely because of fewer breaks and sick days.
- Had lower attrition.
- Reported higher job satisfaction.
- Improved retention enough to create substantial savings for the firm.

The productivity increase was partly due to greater work time, not necessarily greater output per minute. The experiment also found that employees who chose to work remotely were not always the same people who performed best remotely. This is important: **employee preference and employee productivity are not identical.**

### Interpretation

The setting favored remote work:

- Individual tasks were easy to measure.
- Employees needed relatively little face-to-face coordination.
- The company had established processes and monitoring.
- Workers had dedicated home workspaces.
- The experiment included voluntary participation and a relatively long trial period.

**Source:** Bloom et al., “Does Working from Home Work? Evidence from a Chinese Experiment,” *Quarterly Journal of Economics*, 2015.  
DOI: 10.1093/qje/qju032.

---

## 2.2 The follow-up Ctrip study: choice of work location mattered

A later Ctrip study allowed employees to choose between home and office after the experiment.

The results showed that:

- Some workers performed better at home.
- Others performed better in the office.
- Employees were often poor at predicting where they would be most productive.
- Giving workers choice improved retention and satisfaction.

This suggests that a universal policy may be inferior to a flexible one. The productivity effect depends not only on the job but also on the individual’s home environment, personality, commuting burden, and ability to self-manage.

**Source:** Bloom et al., “Does Working from Home Work? Evidence from a Chinese Experiment,” and related analysis discussed by Stanford’s Institute for Economic Policy Research.

---

## 2.3 The Trip.com hybrid experiment: hybrid work improved retention without reducing performance

A more recent randomized trial examined hybrid work among more than 1,600 employees at Trip.com, a large technology and travel company.

### Design

- Employees were randomly assigned either to:
  - work entirely in the office, or
  - work from home two days per week.
- The sample included engineers, marketing staff, and other knowledge workers.
- The trial lasted six months, with subsequent effects tracked over a longer period.

### Findings

Hybrid work:

- Had **no significant negative effect** on performance reviews, promotions, or code output.
- Reduced resignations by roughly one-third.
- Improved job satisfaction.
- Particularly benefited women and employees with long commutes.

The study did not show that hybrid work increased measured productivity. Rather, it showed that hybrid work could preserve performance while reducing turnover.

That is economically important: even if output per employee is unchanged, lower attrition can raise firm productivity by reducing recruitment, training, and vacancy costs.

**Source:** Bloom et al., “Hybrid working from home improves retention without damaging performance,” *Nature*, 2024.  
DOI: 10.1038/s41586-024-07500-2.

---

## 2.4 Other experimental and quasi-experimental evidence

Evidence from randomized field experiments in call centers, customer service, and similar occupations tends to be more favorable to remote work than evidence from collaborative knowledge work. These jobs usually have:

- clear individual output metrics;
- limited need for spontaneous interaction;
- standardized software and workflows;
- relatively low dependence on informal learning.

By contrast, experiments and quasi-experiments involving software development, consulting, research, and product teams often find more complicated effects. Individual output may be stable, while collaboration patterns, innovation, or cross-team communication weaken.

A major limitation is that there are relatively few randomized experiments covering senior professionals, creative work, managers, and highly interdependent teams. The best causal evidence is therefore strongest for specific occupational settings, not for the entire economy.

# 3. Evidence from observational studies

Observational studies examine naturally occurring remote work rather than randomly assigning workers. They often use large administrative datasets, surveys, digital activity records, or linked employer-employee data. Their advantage is scale; their weakness is that remote workers differ systematically from office workers.

## 3.1 Microsoft: remote work changed communication networks

A large study of Microsoft employees compared collaboration patterns before and after the company shifted to remote work during the pandemic.

It found that fully remote work:

- Reduced communication across organizational boundaries.
- Increased communication within existing teams.
- Made collaboration networks more “siloed.”
- Reduced synchronous conversations and increased asynchronous communication.
- Did not necessarily reduce total communication volume.

The implication is not that employees became less busy. Rather, they may have become less connected to people outside their immediate group. This could harm knowledge sharing and innovation over time, even if short-term individual output remains stable.

**Source:** Yang et al., “The effects of remote work on collaboration among information workers,” *Nature Human Behaviour*, 2022.  
DOI: 10.1038/s41562-021-01196-4.

The study is observational, so it cannot cleanly distinguish the effect of remote work from the extraordinary conditions of the pandemic. Nonetheless, it provides strong evidence that remote work changes the structure—not merely the amount—of collaboration.

---

## 3.2 GitHub and software development

Studies of software developers have often found:

- More focused individual work when interruptions decline.
- Stable or increased coding activity in some teams.
- Longer working hours and more fragmented schedules.
- Reduced interaction with colleagues outside immediate teams.
- Possible declines in code-review speed or cross-team coordination.

Code volume is an imperfect productivity measure. More lines of code can mean more output, or simply more verbose or duplicated code. Software quality, architectural coherence, maintenance costs, and innovation are harder to observe.

This is a recurring problem in remote-work research: **digital traces are abundant, but meaningful output is often difficult to measure.**

---

## 3.3 Survey evidence

Employee surveys conducted during and after the pandemic commonly report that workers feel as productive or more productive at home. For example, surveys by the U.S. Census Bureau, Stanford’s WFH Research project, Microsoft, Gallup, and consulting firms generally find that many workers report:

- fewer interruptions;
- no commute;
- greater control over work time;
- improved concentration;
- more working hours or schedule flexibility.

However, self-reported productivity has limitations:

- People may compare home work with an unusually disrupted office period.
- Workers may report effort rather than actual output.
- Employees may be unaware of downstream effects on coordination or innovation.
- Responses may reflect satisfaction or preference rather than productivity.

Survey evidence is valuable for understanding worker experience, but it should not be treated as equivalent to experimentally measured output.

---

## 3.4 Productivity and working hours in national data

Research using time-use surveys generally finds that remote employees often work longer hours, especially because they save commuting time and may extend the workday. Whether this constitutes greater productivity depends on output.

Some studies using business surveys report that employers became more favorable toward remote work after gaining experience with it. Others show that managers remain concerned about mentoring, creativity, and coordination.

National productivity statistics have not shown a clear, universal remote-work effect. Aggregate labor productivity is influenced by:

- industry composition;
- capital investment;
- technology adoption;
- labor hoarding;
- measurement changes;
- pandemic disruptions;
- changes in hours worked;
- firm entry and exit.

It is therefore difficult to infer the effect of remote work from economy-wide productivity alone.

# 4. Evidence from company-level data

Company-level evidence can capture retention, revenue, costs, and organizational performance, but it is particularly vulnerable to confounding. Companies that adopt remote work may also be more technologically advanced, better managed, faster-growing, or located in expensive labor markets.

## 4.1 Firms that successfully shifted to remote work

Many technology, finance, professional-services, and information firms maintained or increased output after moving away from offices. This is consistent with remote work being feasible in knowledge-intensive jobs, but it does not prove that remote work caused higher productivity.

These firms often had:

- cloud-based systems;
- digital communication tools;
- highly educated employees;
- established performance metrics;
- flexible management practices;
- experience with distributed teams.

Their success may reflect **organizational readiness**, not remote work alone.

## 4.2 Firm productivity during the pandemic

Some firms reported strong productivity during the mass shift to remote work. But many simultaneous changes occurred:

- demand surged in sectors such as technology and online retail;
- employees worked longer hours;
- firms reduced travel and office costs;
- managers intensified digital monitoring;
- workers postponed vacations;
- governments supported employment;
- office disruption made the comparison period abnormal.

Thus, “output stayed high while people worked remotely” does not necessarily mean remote work increased productivity. It may mean firms successfully adapted under emergency conditions.

## 4.3 Revenue, profit, and retention

Remote and hybrid policies can improve firm performance through channels other than output per hour:

- access to a larger labor pool;
- lower geographic wage pressure;
- reduced office costs;
- lower commuting-related absence;
- improved employee retention;
- greater inclusion of caregivers and people with disabilities;
- longer employee tenure.

The Trip.com experiment is especially relevant because it found no performance loss but a substantial reduction in resignations. In many firms, this may be the most reliable business case for hybrid work.

# 5. Why studies reach conflicting conclusions

## 5.1 Different types of work

Remote work is more likely to help when tasks are:

- individually performed;
- measurable;
- routine or standardized;
- digitally mediated;
- low in interdependence.

It is more likely to hurt when tasks require:

- rapid coordination;
- tacit knowledge;
- mentoring;
- creativity through informal interaction;
- trust building;
- negotiation;
- frequent problem-solving across teams.

A call-center worker and a product designer should not be expected to respond identically to remote work.

---

## 5.2 Fully remote versus hybrid work

“Remote work” often combines two distinct arrangements:

- **Fully remote:** no regular office presence.
- **Hybrid:** some work at home and some in the office.

The evidence increasingly suggests that hybrid work may capture many benefits of remote work—less commuting, greater flexibility, improved retention—while preserving opportunities for in-person coordination.

A negative finding for fully remote work should not automatically be interpreted as evidence against hybrid work.

---

## 5.3 Selection effects

Remote workers are not randomly selected in most studies. They may differ in:

- occupation;
- seniority;
- income;
- motivation;
- home office quality;
- caregiving responsibilities;
- commuting distance;
- employer quality;
- ability to self-manage.

High-performing employees may be more likely to obtain remote options, making remote work look better than it is. Conversely, workers assigned to remote work during the pandemic may have been in distressed organizations or difficult personal circumstances, making remote work look worse.

Random assignment helps address this problem, but randomized studies remain relatively uncommon.

---

## 5.4 Measurement and time horizon

Remote work may produce different short- and long-term effects.

### Short term

- fewer interruptions;
- more focused work;
- rapid adaptation;
- longer hours;
- stable individual output.

### Long term

- weaker mentoring;
- fewer weak-tie relationships;
- reduced organizational identification;
- less innovation;
- more siloed teams;
- improved retention for some workers.

A study lasting several weeks may detect concentration benefits but miss effects on career development or innovation that emerge over years.

---

## 5.5 Pandemic versus normal conditions

Much of the early evidence was collected during 2020–2021, when:

- schools and childcare facilities were closed;
- workers faced illness and uncertainty;
- offices were unavailable;
- firms were adopting unfamiliar software;
- managers were learning how to supervise remotely;
- labor markets were unusually volatile.

These conditions make pandemic findings difficult to generalize. A later, voluntary hybrid arrangement with functioning schools and mature tools is not equivalent to emergency work from home.

---

## 5.6 Management quality and technology

Remote work magnifies existing management strengths and weaknesses.

Effective remote organizations tend to use:

- outcome-based performance measurement;
- clear priorities;
- documented processes;
- reliable communication norms;
- deliberate onboarding and mentoring;
- scheduled rather than constant meetings;
- well-designed collaboration tools.

Poorly managed organizations may replace supervision with excessive meetings and monitoring, raising work intensity without improving output.

---

## 5.7 Home environment and commuting

Remote work can substantially improve productivity for employees who:

- have long commutes;
- need quiet concentration;
- have suitable workspaces;
- experience office interruptions;
- value schedule flexibility.

It can reduce productivity for employees who:

- lack a dedicated workspace;
- share housing;
- have caregiving demands;
- experience isolation;
- depend on office equipment or social support.

Average effects conceal large individual differences.

---

# 6. A synthesis of the evidence

| Evidence type | Typical finding | Main strength | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Randomized call-center experiments | Remote work can raise measured output | Strong causal identification | Narrow, highly measurable jobs |
| Randomized hybrid knowledge-work trial | Hybrid work did not reduce performance and improved retention | Causal and organizationally relevant | One company and specific workforce |
| Employee surveys | Many workers feel equally or more productive remotely | Captures worker experience at scale | Self-report and perception bias |
| Digital collaboration studies | Remote work can increase within-team focus but reduce cross-team ties | Reveals mechanisms | Observational; output measures imperfect |
| Firm-level data | Results range from positive to negative | Captures real business outcomes | Confounded by technology, management, and selection |
| Aggregate productivity data | No clear universal effect | Broad coverage | Too many simultaneous economic changes |

The overall pattern is not random inconsistency. It is conditional:

\[
\text{Effect of remote work}
= f(\text{task design, coordination, worker fit, management, technology, arrangement})
\]

---

# 7. Practical conclusions for employers

## For individually measurable work

Remote or hybrid arrangements are likely to work well when employers can define outputs clearly and quality can be monitored without constant physical supervision.

Examples include:

- customer support;
- claims processing;
- certain forms of programming;
- writing and analysis;
- back-office operations.

## For highly collaborative work

Organizations should be cautious about fully remote arrangements when work relies on:

- innovation;
- apprenticeship;
- informal knowledge exchange;
- cross-functional coordination;
- relationship development.

Hybrid policies should specify when in-person presence is valuable rather than requiring attendance simply for visibility.

## For all work arrangements

The most important policies are likely to be:

1. Measure outcomes, not online presence.
2. Establish clear communication expectations.
3. Design intentional in-person collaboration.
4. Protect mentoring and onboarding.
5. Track quality, innovation, retention, and employee hours—not just immediate output.
6. Permit flexibility where workers and managers can demonstrate effective performance.
7. Evaluate policies by team and task rather than applying one universal rule.

---

# Conclusion

Remote work does not inherently improve or reduce productivity. The strongest evidence shows a **heterogeneous effect**:

- Fully remote work can increase output in structured, measurable jobs.
- It can weaken collaboration and organizational connectivity in interdependent knowledge work.
- Hybrid work often preserves performance while improving retention and employee satisfaction.
- The largest benefits may come not from higher output per hour, but from lower turnover, wider recruitment, reduced commuting, and better worker-job fit.

The apparent disagreement among studies is therefore largely explained by differences in **occupation, work design, measurement, selection, time horizon, management quality, and whether the arrangement is fully remote or hybrid**. The best general policy is not “remote for everyone” or “back to the office for everyone,” but an evidence-based design matched to the work being performed.
