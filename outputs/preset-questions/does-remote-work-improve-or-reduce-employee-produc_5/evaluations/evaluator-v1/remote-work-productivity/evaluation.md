### R2

- Depth: 1.00
- Rationale: The report supplies treatment, comparison, setting, outcomes, approximate effects, causal interpretation, and external-validity limits for major controlled studies.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The Ctrip experiment is described with approximately 1,000 volunteers, random assignment, a nine-month work-from-home treatment, an office control group, measurable call-center work, and an approximately 13% performance increase.
  - The report explains that the Ctrip increase was partly due to more work minutes rather than necessarily higher output per minute.
  - The Trip.com randomized hybrid trial is described as assigning more than 1,600 employees to either full office work or two home days per week, finding no significant negative effect on performance reviews, promotions, or code output and roughly one-third lower resignations.
  - It discusses limits including voluntary participation, dedicated home workspaces, established processes, narrow occupational settings, one-company evidence, and the scarcity of experiments involving senior professionals, creative work, managers, and highly interdependent teams.

### R3

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: The report gives strong methodological discussion and useful observational mechanisms, but the requirement calls for comparison groups and estimated effects across observational or quasi-experimental evidence; those details are limited.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The Microsoft study is identified as observational and linked to a pandemic shift to remote work; the report describes reduced cross-organizational communication, more siloed networks, and increased asynchronous communication.
  - The report explicitly states that the Microsoft study cannot cleanly distinguish remote work from extraordinary pandemic conditions.
  - It discusses observational selection problems, including differences in occupation, seniority, income, motivation, home-office quality, caregiving, commuting distance, and employer quality.
  - It warns that survey responses may reflect effort, satisfaction, or preference rather than actual output, and that pandemic productivity comparisons involve many concurrent changes.
  - It explains that aggregate time-use and productivity data are affected by industry composition, technology, labor hoarding, measurement changes, pandemic disruptions, hours, and firm entry and exit.
- Missing:
  - The observational section provides few concrete estimated productivity effects or explicit comparison-group designs beyond the Microsoft before-and-after comparison.
  - It discusses threats to causal inference well, but does not systematically distinguish the credibility of particular quasi-experimental or natural-experiment estimates from descriptive associations.
  - The software-development discussion is largely qualitative and does not identify a specific study, treatment contrast, or measured effect.

- Depth: 0.75

- Candidate evidence:
  - A separate company-level evidence section discusses output, retention, revenue, costs, organizational performance, and firm adaptation.

  - It identifies survey limitations, including self-report, comparison with an abnormal pandemic office period, and respondents’ inability to observe downstream coordination or innovation effects.

  - It does not provide much evidence about representativeness across industries or firms beyond general selection and composition concerns.

### R5

- Coverage: 1.00
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: It directly answers the question and provides a strong cross-method, evidence-weighted synthesis, but the precision and quantitative comparability of findings are not developed as fully as the other dimensions.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The executive summary states that the strongest causal evidence ranges from substantially negative to modestly positive for fully remote work and is often neutral or positive for hybrid work.
  - The synthesis table contrasts randomized call-center experiments, the randomized hybrid trial, surveys, digital collaboration studies, firm-level data, and aggregate productivity data by typical finding, strength, and limitation.
  - The conclusion gives a qualified answer: remote work does not inherently improve or reduce productivity; effects are heterogeneous by job and arrangement.
  - The report distinguishes stronger causal evidence from weaker evidence by stating that randomized studies provide the clearest causal evidence, surveys measure perceptions, observational digital-trace studies have imperfect output measures, and aggregate data are confounded.
- Missing:
  - The comparison of precision is mostly implicit through phrases such as 'significant,' 'no clear universal effect,' and 'mixed'; it does not systematically discuss confidence intervals, statistical uncertainty, or the relative precision of estimates.
  - The report does not quantitatively synthesize effect sizes across methods, so magnitude comparisons remain broad rather than directly comparable.

### R7

- Coverage: 1.00
- Depth: 1.00
- Rationale: The report addresses heterogeneity, underrepresented settings, generalization limits, and uncertainty about longer-term collaboration, innovation, mentoring, and career effects while distinguishing them from immediate measured output.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The report repeatedly identifies heterogeneity by occupation, task design, worker fit, home environment, management quality, technology, and remote-work intensity.
  - It states that randomized evidence is limited for senior professionals, creative work, managers, and highly interdependent teams, identifying important external-validity gaps.
  - It separates short-term concentration, interruption, hours, and individual-output effects from longer-term risks involving mentoring, weak ties, innovation, organizational identification, siloing, and career development.
  - It notes that individual workers can perform differently at home or in the office and that average effects conceal large individual differences.

### Novel Value

- The report offers a useful conditional synthesis rather than a universal answer: effects depend on task design, coordination demands, worker fit, management, technology, and whether work is fully remote or hybrid.
- It highlights that hybrid work may preserve measured performance while improving retention, and that the main business benefits may arise through retention, recruitment, office costs, and worker-job fit rather than output per hour alone.
- It explains apparent disagreement across methods through concrete differences in populations, measures, study horizons, pandemic conditions, and organizational implementation.
