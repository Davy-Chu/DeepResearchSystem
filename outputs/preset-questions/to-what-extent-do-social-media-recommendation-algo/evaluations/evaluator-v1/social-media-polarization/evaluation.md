### Support

#### F1: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED

- Claim: Recommendation algorithms reliably change what users see and how they engage with platforms.
- Sources: S2, S10
- Rationale: The sources support that recommendation/feed algorithms change users’ content exposure and can affect engagement. However, “reliably” is too broad: S10 reports substantial changes in time spent and activity when users were moved to chronological feeds, but effects may vary and were not consistent across all outcomes. S2 also describes algorithmic influence but includes qualified, platform- and context-specific evidence.
- Supporting text: S10 reports that moving users out of algorithmic feeds “substantially decreased the time they spent on the platforms and their activity” and changed the political and ideological content they saw. S2 states that algorithms “rank and recommend content according to user behaviour, shaping what they see most frequently.”

#### F2: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Changes in exposure should not be treated as evidence that algorithms necessarily change political beliefs or polarization.
- Sources: S10, S1
- Rationale: S10 directly reports substantial changes in users’ exposure after replacing algorithmic feeds, while finding no significant change in issue polarization or affective polarization. S1 likewise states that exposure to like-minded arguments increased polarization, but algorithmic selection did not amplify those effects, indicating that exposure changes do not necessarily demonstrate an algorithm-induced change in beliefs or polarization.
- Supporting text: S10: “Despite these substantial changes in users’ on-platform experience, the chronological feed did not significantly alter levels of issue polarization [or] affective polarization.” S1: “These effects were not amplified by algorithmic selection,” although algorithmically selected arguments had a slight direct effect for one topic.

#### F3: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Some experiments do find that ranking or curation can change political attitudes and belief updating under particular conditions.
- Sources: S9, S7
- Rationale: Both saved sources describe experiments in which changing content ranking or curation produced changes in political attitudes, polarization, factual judgments, or belief updating. S9 reports that experimentally varying exposure to hostile and antidemocratic content altered polarization, emotions, and perceptions of the opposing political group, while noting the effects were context-specific. S7 describes a belief-updating experiment comparing five ranking algorithms and reports differences in polarization, consensus, and accuracy, including more polarized and less accurate beliefs under engagement-based ranking.
- Supporting text: S9: “greater or lesser exposure to this type of content alters polarisation” and altering exposure “also alters emotions and perceptions regarding the opposing political group.” S7: participants viewed feeds “selected and ranked by one of the five algorithms” and then revised their beliefs; the study reports that engagement-based ranking led to “more polarized and less accurate beliefs,” while alternative rankings improved consensus or factual accuracy.

#### F4: SUPPORTED

- Claim: The best-supported synthesis is that algorithms can contribute to polarization conditionally, but they are not shown here to be a universal or dominant cause.
- Sources: S9, S10, S1, S3, S8
- Rationale: The sources collectively support both parts of the synthesis. S9 reports causal effects from amplifying or reducing hostile, anti-democratic content on X, while explicitly limiting generalizability to a specific platform, election period, population, and short interval. S1 similarly finds that like-minded exposure increased polarization but that algorithmic selection did not amplify those effects, with only a small direct effect for one topic. S10 found no significant change in polarization when users were moved from algorithmic to chronological feeds. S3 characterizes the role of algorithms as “far from straightforward,” saying they mostly reinforce existing social drivers and that further research is needed. These findings support conditional contribution rather than a universal or dominant causal role. S8 supports the broader existence of polarization-related associations with digital media, but does not specifically establish algorithmic dominance.
- Supporting text: S9: effects were observed when hostile, emotionally charged content was amplified or reduced, but the study’s scope was limited and generalizability was uncertain. S1: like-minded arguments increased polarization, yet algorithmic curation did not amplify those effects and produced only a small direct effect for one topic. S10: changing from algorithmic to chronological feeds did not significantly alter polarization. S3: the role of algorithms is “far from straightforward”; existing evidence suggests they mostly reinforce existing social drivers.

#### F5: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED

- Claim: The retrieved evidence does not establish that recommendation algorithms change concrete political behavior such as voting, turnout, political participation, or offline hostility.
- Sources: S1, S4, S5, S7, S9, S10
- Rationale: The sources support the narrower conclusion that the retrieved studies do not establish effects on voting, turnout, or broader civic participation: S9 explicitly says the real impact on voting and other citizen participation is unclear, while S10 reports no significant changes in several political attitudes but does not establish the full set of behavioral outcomes named in the claim. However, the claim is too broad regarding hostility: S4, S5, and S9 report that algorithmic ranking or exposure to hostile political content changed affective polarization, partisan feelings, or political hostility. The sources address attitudes and online/platform outcomes, not offline hostility specifically, so they do not establish that offline hostility changes—but they do show algorithm-related effects on hostility-related attitudes.
- Supporting text: S9: “the duration of these effects and their real impact on voting or other forms of citizen participation are unclear.” S10: the chronological feed “did not significantly alter levels of issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, or other key attitudes” during the study period. By contrast, S4 reports that increasing hostile content exposure caused a “cooling” toward the opposing party, and S5 reports that algorithmic reranking shifted partisan feelings.

#### F6: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Different conclusions arise because studies test different links in the causal chain and use different counterfactuals.
- Sources: S9, S10, S1, S7, S2, S8
- Rationale: The saved sources collectively support both parts of the claim. They describe studies testing different mechanisms or links—such as algorithmic ranking, exposure to like-minded or hostile content, user behavior, attitudes, polarization, and belief accuracy—and explicitly show that experiments use different comparison conditions. In particular, S10 contrasts the default algorithmic feed with a reverse-chronological feed, while S1 contrasts algorithmically selected with randomly selected arguments; S9 further contrasts changing feed ranking with changing exposure to hostile and antidemocratic content. These differing interventions and counterfactuals are presented alongside different conclusions.
- Supporting text: S10 says prior studies often treated social media as a bundle, while its experiment isolated algorithmic ranking using a chronological-feed counterfactual. S1 says researchers separated exposure to like-minded content from effects caused by algorithms, comparing algorithmically selected and randomly selected arguments. S9 reports that a study changing chronological ordering versus algorithmic curation found no significant polarization effect, whereas another study changing exposure to hostile, antidemocratic content found a causal effect. S8 likewise states that effects depend on the specific political variable and system examined.
