# Research Report

## Research Question

Can carbon capture and storage make a significant contribution to reducing global CO₂ emissions? Evaluate its demonstrated effectiveness, costs, scalability, energy requirements, and major arguments for and against large-scale deployment.

## Summary

The supplied evidence supports a qualified conclusion: CCS can make a significant contribution in selected applications, particularly residual process emissions and some hard-to-abate industries, and CCS-based carbon removal may be important in net-zero pathways. However, its demonstrated global contribution remains very small, project performance is not uniform, costs and energy requirements can be substantial, and the evidence does not establish that gigatonne-scale deployment is feasible, broadly cost-effective, or reliably net-negative. CCS therefore appears better supported as a targeted complement to other mitigation options than as a general substitute for rapid emissions reduction.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

CCS is technically demonstrated in multiple commercial and storage applications, but its present global emissions contribution is small.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Multiple supplied sources consistently distinguish established operations from the limited scale of current global mitigation. Counts differ because project definitions differ.

**Evidence**

- Commercial-scale capture has operated in natural-gas processing, coal gasification, ethanol, fertilizer, refinery hydrogen, and coal-fired power; Sleipner had injected more than 16.5 million tonnes by January 2017. [S4]
- The supplied evidence reports less than 0.1% of annual global CO₂ emissions currently captured and stored, while other inventories report 18 dedicated storage projects storing over 80 million tonnes or broader inventories of 41 operating commercial projects. [S1] [S7] [S8]
- Other inventories report more than 50 operational facilities or approximately 20 MtCO₂ captured annually in the United States, illustrating both growth and the small current contribution. [S13] [S14]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

Some facilities have reported capture rates above 90%, and several storage projects show high technical performance, but gross capture cannot be treated as permanently stored or as net lifecycle emissions reduction.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The evidence demonstrates successful projects and identifies project-specific limitations. It does not provide a consistent, independently verified fleet-wide lifecycle account.

**Evidence**

- Capture rates above 90% have been reported for some power and industrial facilities; Sleipner, the Alberta Carbon Trunk Line, and Quest are described as consistently achieving high technical performance. [S4] [S8]
- More than three decades of dedicated storage experience and over 80 million tonnes stored across 18 projects are reported without cases of leakage to the surface causing environmental harm; however, In Salah suspended injection over concerns involving seal integrity, fractures, and migration outside the lease. [S7] [S9]
- The evidence explicitly distinguishes CO₂ captured from CO₂ avoided, with energy used to operate CCS and fossil-based energy supply potentially generating additional emissions. [S10]
- Potential release pathways, groundwater and ecosystem hazards, induced seismicity, and the need for monitoring and remediation limit any claim of demonstrated universal permanence, even though the supplied evidence does not establish actual harmful leakage in the cited dedicated projects. [S6]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

CCS may be especially relevant to residual process emissions and selected hard-to-abate industrial applications, but its suitability is context-dependent and must be assessed against alternatives.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The current wording is qualified and verified: it does not imply that all emissions in these sectors are unavoidable or that CCS is always the best option.

**Evidence**

- Cement, lime, some chemical production, and waste incineration are identified as involving unavoidable or residual process emissions; cement, steel, chemicals, and other heavy industries are described as hard to decarbonize in some contexts. [S5] [S8] [S10] [S11]
- The sources present CCS as potentially valuable in hard-to-abate sectors, legacy retrofits, low-carbon hydrogen, and other applications where alternatives may be limited, immature, or costly. [S1] [S11] [S12] [S14]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

CCS costs and energy requirements vary greatly by application and CO₂ stream, and the supplied evidence characterizes many applications as expensive and dependent on project conditions or policy support.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The supplied sources consistently support strong application and project-stage variation. They do not provide a harmonized total-system comparison against major alternatives.

**Evidence**

- A broad indicative range of 2.5–8 GJ per tonne of CO₂ for energy demand and $50–600 per tonne for cost is reported, with recurring cost overruns in reviewed cases. [S1]
- Indicative capture costs are reported at approximately CAD 27–48/tCO₂ for concentrated streams and CAD 50–150/tCO₂ for diluted streams; transport and storage are additional. [S10]
- Capture is commonly the largest cost component, while transport costs depend on flow rate, distance, and transport mode. Many reported figures are engineering- or FEED-level estimates rather than validated final project costs. [S10] [S13]
- CCUS is reported as economically noncompetitive in most US applications under prevailing conditions without incentives or legal requirements, while some hard-to-abate applications and low-carbon hydrogen pathways are presented as potentially cost-effective. [S4] [S11] [S12] [S14]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

Global CCS deployment would need to expand by orders of magnitude relative to current operations, and the evidence does not establish that the required build-out rates and infrastructure are feasible.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The gap between current deployment and modeled requirements is well supported. Feasible global build rates, injection limits, geological capacity, workforce, materials, and permitting requirements remain unquantified in the supplied evidence.

**Evidence**

- Climate pathways cited in the ledger require several billion tonnes of storage annually by 2050 or 1 GtCO₂ annually by 2030, while current deployment is reported as less than 0.1% of global annual emissions and approximately 20 MtCO₂ annually in the United States. [S1] [S8] [S12] [S14]
- The EU is reported to potentially require 300–640 MtCO₂ per year by 2050, including at least 150 Mt/year of atmospheric or biogenic CO₂. [S5]
- The sources identify the need for substantial expansion of capture facilities, transport networks, storage sites, permitting, and coordination between geographically separated sources and sinks. [S4] [S8] [S11] [S13]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

CCS-based carbon removal could be important for residual or legacy emissions, but current evidence does not establish that direct-air-capture storage or bioenergy with CCS is already scalable or cost-effective.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The ledger contains meaningful supporting and opposing evidence. The conclusion is limited to potential pathway importance, not present commercial scalability or affordability.

**Evidence**

- The sources identify direct-air capture with storage and bioenergy with CCS as potential ways to remove atmospheric CO₂ and address residual or historic emissions; modeled pathways include substantial future removal requirements. [S3] [S5] [S8] [S11] [S12]
- The same evidence emphasizes that climate-oriented capture, transport, and storage remain relatively new at the needed scale and can be costly, with no demonstrated broad scalability or cost-effectiveness. [S1] [S8] [S10]

### Finding 7

**Claim**

The strongest case for large-scale CCS is targeted deployment alongside other mitigation measures; the strongest case against broad deployment is its cost, energy burden, slow historical scale-up, dependence on support, and risk of overreliance before net lifecycle performance is consistently demonstrated.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The ledger supports both sides but lacks a comprehensive empirical comparison of CCS with electrification, renewables, efficiency, material substitution, and other alternatives across regions and sectors.

**Evidence**

- Supportive sources describe roles in residual process emissions, hard-to-abate industry, firm or backup power, synthetic fuels, retrofits, and carbon removal, and characterize CCS as complementary to electrification and renewable energy. [S3] [S5] [S11] [S12] [S14]
- Opposing evidence identifies slow deployment, high and uncertain costs, limited historical global impact, project complexity, reliance on public support, and unresolved environmental, monitoring, permanence, infrastructure, and opportunity-cost concerns. [S1] [S6] [S8] [S10] [S11] [S14]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- The evidence supports CCS-based carbon removal as potentially important in modeled net-zero pathways, while other evidence stresses that its present cost-effectiveness, durability at scale, and scalability are unproven. This is a substantive uncertainty rather than a basis for choosing one side. [S3] [S5] [S8] [S10] [S11] [S12]
- Some projects report high technical performance and no observed harmful surface leakage in cited dedicated-storage projects, while In Salah experienced concerns about seal integrity and migration, and other evidence identifies potential release and environmental hazards. The supplied evidence does not establish uniform fleet-wide permanence. [S6] [S7] [S8] [S9]
- Reported project counts and stored quantities are not directly comparable because inventories differ in treatment of dedicated storage, EOR, broader CCUS facilities, and projects not primarily operated for climate mitigation. [S7] [S8]
- CCS is described as potentially cost-effective in selected hard-to-abate applications but not economically competitive in most US applications under prevailing conditions; no consistent cross-region comparison with alternatives is supplied. [S4] [S10] [S11] [S12] [S14]

## Remaining Gaps

- G1: No consistent, independently verified full-lifecycle accounting is supplied for operating projects, including capture, compression, transport, injection, monitoring, leakage, and post-closure stewardship.
- G2 and G7: Comparable regional, application-specific total-system costs—including financing, transport, storage, and avoided-emissions accounting—are unavailable, as is a robust comparison with electrification, renewables, efficiency, and other alternatives.
- G3 and G8: The ledger does not quantify global storage capacity, feasible annual injection rates, infrastructure, materials, workforce, permitting, or realistic build rates through 2050.
- G4 and G9: Sector-specific energy, electricity, fuel, water, induced-emissions, and net-emissions results under different energy supplies are not provided.
- G5: Empirical cross-jurisdiction evidence on environmental and public-health effects, leakage liability, monitoring, infrastructure lock-in, and opportunity costs is insufficient.
- G6: No harmonized independent inventory reconciles project counts, stored quantities, performance, EOR, and climate-purpose boundaries.

## Conclusion

On the supplied evidence, CCS can make a significant contribution to global CO₂ reduction in the sense of being an important targeted complement—especially for residual process emissions, selected hard-to-abate industries, some retrofits, and potentially carbon removal. Its broad global significance is not yet demonstrated: current deployment is a very small share of emissions, costs and energy requirements are often high, whole-system lifecycle reductions are incompletely documented, and the infrastructure and build-out needed for modeled gigatonne-scale pathways remain unproven. The evidence therefore supports neither dismissing CCS entirely nor treating it as a general solution. A defensible deployment strategy would prioritize applications with limited alternatives and strong monitoring and lifecycle accounting, while preserving rapid development of other mitigation options. This assessment concerns the current evidence base and pathways toward 2030–2050; it is geographically and application-dependent, with major unresolved uncertainties in costs, alternatives, storage capacity, build rates, and net lifecycle emissions.
