#### F2: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Observational and quasi-experimental evidence does not yield a uniform productivity effect: some pandemic-period single-firm studies reported short-run declines, while the BLS analysis found statistically significant positive associations between increased remote work and total-factor-productivity growth across 61 private-sector industries.
- Sources: S4, S6, S7, S9
- Rationale: The saved sources support the claim’s central factual content. S4 states that pandemic-period single-firm case studies found short-run productivity declines, while the BLS analysis across 61 private-sector industries found a positive relationship between increases in remote work and TFP growth. S9 further specifies that the relationships for 2019–21 and 2019–22 were statistically significant. S6 and S7 also describe conflicting study results and the positive BLS association. The sources do not explicitly use the label “quasi-experimental,” but they substantiate the stated pattern of mixed evidence.
- Supporting text: S4: “a couple of single-firm case studies conducted during the pandemic find that employee productivity declined in the short run” and “TFP growth ... is positively associated with the rise in the percentage of remote workers across 61 industries.” S9: the association was “statistically significant” in both periods.

#### F3: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Company- and aggregate-industry-level evidence is conflicting: one analysis summarized in the supplied sources reportedly found little relationship between an industry's capacity to work remotely and aggregate labor productivity, while the BLS analysis found positive pandemic-period TFP associations.
- Sources: S4, S7, S8, S9
- Rationale: S4 directly reports both components of the claim: Fernald et al. (2024) found little relationship between an industry's ability to work entirely remotely and labor productivity across 43 private-sector industries, while the BLS analysis found positive associations between increased remote work and TFP growth across 61 industries during 2019–21 and 2019–22. S9 independently confirms the positive BLS associations. S7 and S8 repeat the BLS finding but are not needed for support.
- Supporting text: S4: Fernald et al. #### F4: SUPPORTED

The supplied evidence includes calls completed, emails, lines of code, performance ratings, self-reported productivity, monitored activity, hours worked, and aggregate industry productivity; these outcomes are not interchangeable.

The conclusion that these outcomes are not interchangeable is directly supported by the sources’ distinction among individual-task proxies, survey measures, monitored activity, and aggregate productivity.

#### F5: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED

- Claim: Work type and work design appear to moderate effects. Remote work is associated in the supplied sources with stronger individual focus or deep-work outcomes, whereas office work is associated with faster collaboration, spontaneous problem-solving, knowledge sharing, mentoring, and innovation; hybrid arrangements may balance these functions.
- Sources: S1, S2, S4, S6, S7
- Rationale: The supplied sources support the main moderation pattern: outcomes depend on task type and collaboration requirements; remote work is linked to stronger individual focus/deep work, office work to faster spontaneous collaboration and knowledge sharing, and hybrid work to balancing focus and collaboration. The cautious wording “appear” and “may” is appropriate.
- Supporting text: S2 states that remote work improves “individual focus productivity,” especially for “deep concentration,” while “office environments improve collaboration and spontaneous problem-solving”; it also says hybrid setups “balance focus time and collaboration.” It further identifies office benefits including “faster collaboration and knowledge sharing,” “mentoring junior employees,” “creative problem solving,” and contributions to “innovation.” S1 reports a median “+10% for individual focused tasks” versus “-4% for collaborative tasks,” and S4 says effects depend on “the types of tasks,” technology, home environment, motivation, and management.

#### F6: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Productivity should be distinguished from organizational outcomes. The supplied experimental summaries report higher satisfaction and lower attrition under remote or hybrid arrangements even when measured productivity was unchanged or when productivity gains coexisted with social-isolation concerns.
- Sources: S1, S6, S7, S8, S4, S5
- Rationale: The sources support the distinction between measured productivity and broader organizational outcomes. They describe hybrid experiments with unchanged or statistically indistinguishable performance alongside higher satisfaction and substantially lower attrition or resignation. They also describe fully remote productivity gains accompanied by higher satisfaction and lower attrition, while noting social isolation or loneliness as a concern. S4 further distinguishes individual productivity measures from aggregate productivity and links remote work with higher satisfaction and lower turnover.
- Supporting text: S1 reports that Trip.com hybrid workers had no measurable productivity difference while showing higher job satisfaction and 35% lower attrition; its Ctrip experiment found a 13.5% performance gain, higher satisfaction, 50% lower attrition, and loneliness/social isolation concerns. S6 and S8 likewise summarize unchanged hybrid performance with lower resignations, while S4 states that remote work raised job satisfaction and lowered turnover.
