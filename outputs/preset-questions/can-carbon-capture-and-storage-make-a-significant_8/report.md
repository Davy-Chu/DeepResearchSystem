# Research Report

## Research Question

Can carbon capture and storage make a significant contribution to reducing global CO₂ emissions? Evaluate its demonstrated effectiveness, costs, scalability, energy requirements, and major arguments for and against large-scale deployment.

## Summary

The accumulated evidence supports a conditional answer. Carbon capture and storage (CCS) is technically feasible and has operated successfully at some facilities, but its current delivered contribution is very small relative to global emissions. Its strongest justification is selective deployment for difficult industrial process emissions, some existing assets, and durable carbon-removal pathways such as BECCS or direct-air capture with permanent geological storage. High costs, significant energy and infrastructure requirements, uneven project performance, storage and liability concerns, and competition from other mitigation options weigh against indiscriminate large-scale deployment.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

CCS can make a significant contribution in selected applications, but the evidence does not support treating it as the dominant economy-wide emissions-reduction strategy.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The selective-role conclusion is consistent across strategic, critical, and project-focused sources. The evidence does not establish a precise maximum global contribution.

**Evidence**

- The IEA identifies roles for CCUS in emissions from existing energy assets, hard-to-abate sectors, low-carbon hydrogen, and atmospheric carbon removal. The EU CCUS strategy similarly emphasizes cement, steel, chemicals, waste, and removal pathways. [S2] [S5]
- Scenario-related evidence indicates that renewables, electrification, fuel switching, and efficiency provide most modeled emissions reductions, although the precise 4.9% CCUS figure cited from the IEA is not verified in the retrieved primary WEO material. [S7] [S12]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

CCS has demonstrated technical feasibility at individual facilities, but not consistently reliable, climate-relevant performance across the global project fleet.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The evidence establishes both successful projects and significant limitations, while also making clear that standardized fleet-wide performance data are lacking.

**Evidence**

- Sleipner, Quest, and the Alberta Carbon Trunk Line are reported to have achieved high technical performance, demonstrating that capture, transport, and geological storage can operate at substantial scale. [S26]
- Other projects have experienced suboptimal capture, cost overruns, operational difficulties, or storage-integrity concerns. At In Salah, injection was suspended over concerns about seal integrity and CO₂ movement, although no leakage was reported during operation. [S1] [S17] [S26]
- The retrieved sources do not provide a standardized, independently verified global measure of actual annual capture and permanent storage, fleet-wide availability, lifecycle emissions, or net abatement. [S9] [S13] [S16] [S20]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

Current delivered CCS capacity is very small relative to global emissions, despite rapid growth in project pipelines.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The reported operating totals differ by date and definition, but all indicate that delivered capacity is small and far below announced or developmental capacity.

**Evidence**

- One estimate places operational capacity at approximately 51 MtCO₂ per year against 37,600 MtCO₂ per year of energy and industrial-process emissions, or about 0.16%. [S9]
- Other sources report approximately 63.9 Mtpa of operating commercial capture capacity, while the Global CCS Institute reports 513 Mtpa across operating and developmental projects. [S16] [S20]
- The IEA project database includes commissioned, under-construction, and planned projects, so pipeline capacity is not equivalent to captured or permanently stored CO₂. [S13]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

CCS costs are high, uncertain, and strongly dependent on the CO₂ source, system boundary, infrastructure, financing, and policy support.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Multiple sources, including a government research service, identify cost and policy dependence as central constraints. Exact cost figures are not directly comparable across technologies or system boundaries.

**Evidence**

- The reviewed evidence gives an indicative cost range of $50–600 per tonne of CO₂ and cites recurring project cost overruns. [S1]
- An EU estimate gives end-to-end disposal costs of approximately €105–280 per tonne of CO₂. [S7]
- US incentives of up to $85 per tonne for some permanent-storage projects and $180 per tonne for direct-air-capture storage illustrate the importance of public support rather than demonstrating unsubsidized competitiveness. [S24] [S25]
- The Congressional Research Service reports that construction and operating costs would need to decline for widespread deployment. [S25]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

CCS requires substantial energy and infrastructure inputs, which can reduce its net emissions benefit.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The energy burden and infrastructure requirements are supported, but standardized lifecycle measurements across sectors and projects are unavailable.

**Evidence**

- The critical review estimates energy demand of approximately 2.5–8 GJ per tonne of CO₂ and warns that this can reduce net emissions benefits. [S1]
- Large-scale deployment requires coordinated capture, transport, storage, monitoring, and sometimes cross-border infrastructure, especially where sources are distant from storage sites. [S5] [S26]
- Net climate performance depends on the energy source, capture rate, transport, storage permanence, and whether CO₂ is used for enhanced oil recovery or dedicated storage. [S1] [S25] [S26]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

The strongest argument for CCS is its potential to address unavoidable industrial process emissions and provide storage for durable carbon removals.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

These roles are consistently identified by strategic and project-focused sources, although their aggregate global potential is not quantified.

**Evidence**

- Cement and lime process emissions are identified as difficult to eliminate through fuel switching or electrification. [S2] [S5] [S15]
- CCUS may address residual emissions from existing assets, support low-carbon hydrogen, and provide geological storage for BECCS or direct-air-capture removals. [S2] [S5]
- High-performing projects show that the underlying capture, transport, and storage technologies are achievable. [S26]

### Finding 7

**Claim**

The strongest arguments against broad CCS deployment are high cost, energy use, uneven operating performance, storage and liability uncertainties, possible fossil-fuel lock-in, and diversion of investment from alternatives.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The principal objections are supported by multiple strategic, project-focused, critical, and government sources and align with the gap between pipeline growth and delivered climate impact.

**Evidence**

- Critical reviews identify high cost, energy demand, project underperformance, geological uncertainty, long-term liability, and opportunity costs. [S1]
- The Congressional Research Service records concerns that CCS may prolong fossil-fuel combustion and that long-term underground storage remains uncertain. [S25]
- Scenario evidence assigns most modeled reductions to alternatives such as renewables, electrification, fuel switching, and efficiency, although the exact CCUS share is not independently verified. [S7] [S12]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Strategic and industry sources describe CCS as important, essential, or capable of reaching very large scale, while critical and scenario-based sources view it as a limited and conditional contributor. The disagreement largely concerns the benchmark: hard-to-abate residual emissions versus economy-wide mitigation. [S1] [S2] [S5] [S7] [S20]
- Several high-performing projects demonstrate technical feasibility, but this does not resolve evidence of cost overruns, uneven operation, and limited delivered global capacity. These positions are therefore conditional rather than mutually exclusive. [S1] [S26]
- Reported operating capacity ranges from approximately 51 to 63.9 Mtpa, while the broader 513 Mtpa figure includes operating and developmental projects. Differences likely reflect dates, thresholds, and whether sources count capture capacity, storage capacity, commercial facilities, or planned projects. [S9] [S16] [S20]
- Historical CCS experience includes enhanced-oil-recovery utilization, whereas climate-focused assessments emphasize dedicated permanent storage and durable removals. These pathways are not equivalent in terms of net atmospheric removal. [S25] [S26]
- The cited 4.9% CCUS contribution to emissions reductions is reported by a secondary interpretation, but the retrieved primary IEA World Energy Outlook page does not contain or define that figure. [S7] [S12]

## Remaining Gaps

- The independently verified current annual quantity of CO₂ actually captured and permanently stored, rather than nameplate capacity, remains unresolved.
- Standardized measured capture rates, availability, energy penalties, lifecycle emissions, and net-abatement results for representative operating projects were not retrieved.
- The share of current and planned capacity involving dedicated permanent storage, CO₂-EOR, fossil-fuel processing, BECCS, or direct-air capture remains unclear.
- The proportion of announced capacity that will reach operation given cost, permitting, transport, storage-characterization, public-acceptance, and liability constraints remains uncertain.
- The evidence does not provide a consistent sector-specific lifecycle comparison between CCS and electrification, efficiency, renewable hydrogen, or other alternatives.
- The primary IEA World Energy Outlook 2025 text retrieved here does not substantiate or define the cited 4.9% contribution figure.

## Conclusion

CCS can make a meaningful contribution to global CO₂ reduction, but its likely value is targeted rather than universal. It is technically demonstrated in some projects and particularly relevant where process emissions are difficult to eliminate or where permanent geological storage is needed for carbon removal. However, current operating capacity is only roughly 0.16% of the global energy and industrial-process emissions estimate used in the retrieved evidence, and much larger pipeline figures combine operating, planned, and developmental projects. Costs, energy requirements, infrastructure needs, uneven performance, storage governance, and policy dependence make broad reliance on CCS risky. The evidence therefore supports a portfolio approach: prioritize direct emissions avoidance, efficiency, electrification, and renewable energy where practical, while deploying CCS selectively where alternatives are limited and requiring transparent measurement of capture, transport, storage permanence, lifecycle emissions, and net climate benefit.
