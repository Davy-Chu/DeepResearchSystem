# Research Report

## Research Question

Can carbon capture and storage make a significant contribution to reducing global CO₂ emissions? Evaluate its demonstrated effectiveness, costs, scalability, energy requirements, and major arguments for and against large-scale deployment.

## Summary

The ledger supports a qualified conclusion: CCS has demonstrated technical operation in several industrial settings and could make a significant future contribution, particularly for some process emissions and engineered carbon removal. However, its demonstrated aggregate contribution remains very small—below 0.1% of annual global CO₂ emissions according to the strongest aggregate claim—and the evidence does not establish that projected gigatonne-scale deployment is technically, economically, energetically, or institutionally achievable. CCS should therefore be treated as a potentially important, application-specific complement to emissions reduction, not as a demonstrated substitute for broader decarbonization.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

CCS has been demonstrated in multiple commercial applications, but demonstrated global emissions reductions remain very small relative to the scale required for meaningful global mitigation.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The aggregate scale claim is directly supported and broadly corroborated, while the conflicting evidence concerns the distinction between operational/nameplate capacity and verified durable net reductions rather than directly overturning the aggregate conclusion.

**Evidence**

- A 2025 review reports that CCS captures and stores less than 0.1% of annual global CO₂ emissions despite more than $40 billion in investment. [S1]
- Operational capacity is reported at roughly 51–64 MtCO₂ per year, but these figures are capacity measures rather than consistent verification of actual capture, transport, injection, and durable net storage. [S8] [S9] [S10]
- Commercial facilities operate across natural-gas processing, fertilizer, ethanol, coal gasification, refinery hydrogen, and at least one coal-power project; Sleipner is reported to have injected approximately 0.85 MtCO₂ annually and more than 16.5 Mt cumulatively by January 2017. [S4] [S2]
- The evidence conflicts over what counts as successful CCS: commercial operation demonstrates technical viability, while the review questions durability, capture performance, scale, and emissions-offset claims. [S1] [S4]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

CCS costs are highly application- and design-dependent; the supplied evidence indicates substantial costs but does not support a robust universal comparison with alternative mitigation options.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The ledger consistently supports wide cost variation and significant cost uncertainty. It does not resolve system boundaries or provide directly comparable costs for renewables, electrification, efficiency, or non-CCS industrial pathways.

**Evidence**

- The supplied review gives an indicative CCS cost range of $50–$600 per tonne of CO₂ and states that energy demand ranges from 2.5–8 GJ per tonne. [S1]
- Capture is described as typically the largest component of CCS costs; compression, pumping, pipelines, and shipping add separate costs, and estimates are characterized as uncertain and mainly useful for relative comparison. [S11]
- An industrial-emitter capture cost of approximately $60–$120 per tonne is reported, while pipeline transport may account for 21–25% of total CCUS project costs, depending on distance, terrain, and throughput. [S12]
- Cost barriers, cost overruns, inflation, and limited final-project benchmarking are identified as obstacles to economies of scale and investment. [S1] [S3] [S11]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

CCS has expansion potential and some infrastructure models are operating, but the evidence does not establish global scalability at gigatonne scale.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The existence of a growing project base and specific infrastructure examples is well supported, but the evidence remains insufficient to infer that planned capacity will be delivered or that storage and institutional conditions permit global gigatonne-scale deployment.

**Evidence**

- More than 620 CCS projects, including more than 50 operating facilities, have been identified; reported operating capture capacity is about 64.2 MtCO₂ per year across 46 facilities in one inventory. [S10] [S11]
- The global pipeline is reported to include more than 700 projects and over 500 MtCO₂ per year of capacity in development, while facilities under construction could raise capture capacity above 100 MtCO₂ per year. [S6] [S9]
- Northern Lights demonstrates an operating cross-border transport and storage model, with Phase 1 capacity of 1.5 MtCO₂ per year and a planned expansion to 5 MtCO₂ per year by 2028. [S12]
- Barriers include high costs, policy dependence, permitting delays, right-of-way challenges, coordination problems between emitters and transport/storage providers, and the need for shared cross-border infrastructure. [S2] [S3] [S5] [S9] [S11] [S12]
- The ledger does not quantify geological storage by theoretical, technically accessible, permitted, and practically developable categories, nor does it establish global supply-chain or public-acceptance limits. [S10] [S11] [S12]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

The supplied evidence indicates a material CCS energy requirement, but it does not determine the lifecycle or net climate effect under different energy sources and project designs.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The aggregate energy range is directly supplied, but the open evidence gaps prevent a reliable conclusion about net climate benefits across fuels, electricity sources, capture configurations, and storage outcomes.

**Evidence**

- The review gives an aggregate energy-demand range of 2.5–8 GJ per tonne of CO₂. [S1]
- The ledger identifies capture, compression, transport, and storage as separate cost and process components, but does not provide component-level energy penalties. [S11]
- Energy needs are identified as a constraint for engineered removals, alongside cost, measurement, and permanence concerns. [S14]
- The evidence does not quantify residual lifecycle emissions or specify the conditions under which CCS benefits become limited or negative. [S1] [S14]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

The strongest case for large-scale CCS is application-specific: it may address intrinsic process emissions in sectors such as cement, lime, chemicals, steel, and waste incineration, and it is proposed for BECCS and DACCS. The strongest objections concern cost, delayed alternatives, fossil-fuel dependence, permanence, and the large gap between modeled roles and demonstrated removal.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The sectoral rationale and modeled future roles are supported, but the ledger also documents substantial counterevidence and does not resolve the comparative performance of applications or the feasibility of modeled deployment.

**Evidence**

- The EU CCUS vision identifies cement, steel, chemical production, and waste incineration as hard-to-abate sectors and distinguishes unavoidable process emissions in cement, lime, and some chemicals. [S5]
- Supportive sources cite modeled contributions of approximately 13% of required reductions by 2060 or 14% of reductions needed by 2050, and identify CCS as potentially important for industrial decarbonization. [S2] [S4] [S11]
- DACS and BECCS are presented as routes to permanent atmospheric carbon removal; one EU assessment gives a modeled requirement of at least 150 MtCO₂ per year of atmospheric or biogenic capture and storage by 2050. [S5] [S14]
- The opposing evidence states that reliance on CCS may delay more scalable alternatives and prolong fossil-fuel dependence, while current carbon-removal activity is only in the tens of millions of tonnes per year against an estimated 7–9 GtCO₂ per year removal requirement by 2050. [S1] [S14]
- The evidence does not provide a systematic application-by-application comparison of fossil-power CCS, blue hydrogen, cement CCS, BECCS, and DACCS for net emissions, permanence, environmental effects, lock-in, opportunity costs, or demonstrated scalability. [S1] [S5] [S14]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Commercial operation and growing planned capacity support technical viability and future potential, while evidence on actual durable net storage remains incomplete and aggregate demonstrated deployment remains below the scale needed for major global reductions. [S1] [S4] [S8] [S9] [S10] [S11]
- CCS is presented as potentially contributing 13–14% of modeled future reductions, but current deployment is below 1% of global emissions and current carbon removal is far below modeled 2050 requirements. [S2] [S4] [S8] [S9] [S14]
- Infrastructure examples and a large project pipeline indicate possible momentum, but cost, permitting, coordination, policy, storage, supply-chain, and public-acceptance constraints are not quantified sufficiently to establish global scalability. [S3] [S5] [S6] [S9] [S10] [S11] [S12]
- The aggregate energy-demand range is available, but component-level penalties, energy sources, residual emissions, and net lifecycle outcomes remain unresolved. [S1] [S11] [S14]

## Remaining Gaps

- G1: No consistent, independently verified global inventory distinguishes nameplate capacity from actual captured, transported, injected, durably stored, and net-reduced CO₂.
- G2/G6: Cost system boundaries, sector-specific whole-system costs, and comparable costs for alternative mitigation options remain unresolved.
- G3/G7: Geological storage availability and practical global infrastructure, permitting, supply-chain, and public-acceptance limits are not quantified.
- G4/G8: Component-level energy penalties and lifecycle residual emissions under different fuel and electricity sources are not established.
- G5/G9: CCS applications are not systematically compared for net emissions, permanence, environmental impacts, lock-in, opportunity costs, and demonstrated scalability.

## Conclusion

On the supplied evidence, CCS can potentially make a significant contribution to global CO₂ reduction, but that contribution is prospective and conditional rather than demonstrated. Its clearest potential role is targeted deployment for difficult industrial process emissions and possibly carefully governed carbon-dioxide removal. Current operational capacity and verified aggregate reductions remain very small, costs vary widely and lack consistent comparison with alternatives, energy and lifecycle effects are insufficiently resolved, and global scalability faces major infrastructure and institutional barriers. Large-scale CCS may therefore be valuable as a complement to rapid direct emissions reductions, but the ledger does not justify treating it as a proven route to large global reductions or as a basis for delaying alternatives.
