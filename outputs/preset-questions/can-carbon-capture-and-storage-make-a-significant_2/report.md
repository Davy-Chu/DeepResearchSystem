# Research Report

## Research Question

Can carbon capture and storage make a significant contribution to reducing global CO₂ emissions? Evaluate its demonstrated effectiveness, costs, scalability, energy requirements, and major arguments for and against large-scale deployment.

## Summary

The ledger supports a qualified conclusion: CCS is operational in several industrial applications and could be important for hard-to-abate process emissions, but its demonstrated global climate impact remains limited and uncertain. Current deployment is far below the scale projected in climate scenarios, while costs, energy requirements, infrastructure, storage governance, performance monitoring, and the prevalence of enhanced oil recovery create substantial barriers. The evidence does not establish whether CCS will make a significant global contribution in practice, as opposed to a more targeted contribution in selected sectors.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

CCS has demonstrated commercial operation across several industrial applications, but operational experience does not by itself demonstrate large, durable global emissions reductions.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The ledger contains substantial supporting evidence for commercial operation and substantial counterevidence concerning climate-relevant performance; the claim preserves both aspects.

**Evidence**

- Commercial-scale operation has been reported in natural-gas processing, fertilizer production, coal gasification, refinery hydrogen production, and at least one coal-fired power project; 41–43 operational facilities and technically strong performance at several projects are also reported. [S3] [S7] [S8]
- The climate significance of this operating experience is qualified because most projects were not developed primarily for climate purposes, often were not required to maximize permanent storage or minimize atmospheric emissions, and many are associated with oil and gas or EOR. [S8] [S11] [S14]
- A review of projects including Petra Nova, Gorgon, Sleipner, and In Salah reports shortcomings involving capture rates, durability, scale, and claimed emissions offsets. [S1]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

CCS has a strong potential rationale in hard-to-abate industrial sectors, particularly where emissions are intrinsic to industrial processes or difficult to eliminate through electrification alone.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Both cited sources directly identify hard-to-abate industrial emissions as a principal potential application, although they do not prove that this role will produce a significant global reduction by itself.

**Evidence**

- Cement and other hard-to-abate sectors are identified as strategic CCS applications where alternatives are limited. [S1]
- CCUS is described as capable of mitigating unavoidable process emissions and potentially forming part of a lowest-cost decarbonization pathway for several heavy industries. [S4]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

CCS requires substantial energy and can be expensive, with reported energy demand of 2.5–8 GJ per tonne of CO₂ and costs of $50–600 per tonne of CO₂; costs vary substantially by source, transport, storage, and integration requirements.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The numerical ranges come directly from S1, while S8 corroborates the conclusion that costs depend on application and infrastructure and may be higher for dedicated climate mitigation.

**Evidence**

- The review source gives an energy-demand range of 2.5–8 GJ/tCO₂ and a cost range of $50–600/tCO₂. [S1]
- Climate-oriented integrated CCS can cost more than established commercial CO₂ applications, particularly for cement, steel, and petrochemicals, and saline-aquifer storage and longer-distance transport add costs. [S8]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

The individual components of CCS have substantial operational experience, but integrating capture, transport, and dedicated geological storage for climate mitigation remains comparatively new and can be more costly, especially for dilute or difficult industrial emissions.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The source directly distinguishes component-level experience from the newer integrated climate-mitigation application.

**Evidence**

- Decades of experience exist with capture, CO₂ transport, and geological injection, while their integrated use for climate mitigation is described as relatively new and generally more costly in sectors such as cement, steel, and petrochemicals. [S8]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

CCS could have a large future role in climate scenarios, but achieving that role would require deployment many times larger than today and depends on policy coordination, infrastructure, cost reductions, and governance.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The evidence strongly supports the distinction between modeled future potential and current deployment. It does not establish that projected deployment will occur or deliver the modeled reductions.

**Evidence**

- Scenario and policy sources project CCS contributions ranging from approximately 13–14% of required emissions reductions in some outlooks to hundreds of millions of tonnes annually by 2050 and hundreds of gigatonnes cumulatively by 2100 in a 1.5°C-compatible scenario. [S2] [S3] [S4]
- Current operational capacity is reported at approximately 50 MtCO₂ per year, with more than 386.8 MtCO₂ per year of potential capacity in facilities under development or construction; other evidence states that gigatonne-scale deployment is still necessary. [S6] [S7] [S8]
- An optimized strategy could double capture scale by 2030 but would still leave a persistent one-third deficit relative to climate targets, with cost barriers impeding economies of scale. [S5]
- CCUS is assigned potential roles involving existing energy assets, hard-to-abate sectors, clean hydrogen, and carbon removal, with its contribution increasing as costs fall and cheaper options are exhausted. [S9]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

The evidence presents major arguments against indiscriminate large-scale CCS deployment: high and uncertain costs, energy penalties, storage and permanence uncertainties, technical underperformance, infrastructure and monitoring complexity, public-liability concerns, dependence on subsidies, and the risk of delaying alternatives.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Multiple sources directly identify these technical, economic, governance, and opportunity-cost concerns. The evidence supports them as deployment risks, not as proof that all CCS projects fail.

**Evidence**

- Reported concerns include cost overruns, suboptimal capture rates, geological uncertainty, transferred public liability, energy demand, and possible delay of more effective mitigation options. [S1]
- Other challenges include implementation cost, storage-site availability, safety and environmental concerns, technical and operational problems, costly transport to distant storage, and the need for transparent performance reporting and regulation. [S2] [S8]
- Lifecycle accounting must address capture, transport, storage, leakage, project emissions, and continuing monitoring, reporting, and verification. [S12]
- Slow deployment, unmet expectations, costly and uncertain carbon removal, EOR dependence, and reliance on government support are also identified as objections to indiscriminate expansion. [S5] [S11] [S14]

### Finding 7

**Claim**

Most operating CCS capacity described in the ledger is associated with EOR or oil-and-gas production rather than dedicated permanent climate-mitigation storage, with estimates ranging from 79% to 82.5%.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

Two sources provide closely aligned estimates, but the ledger notes that both are advocacy-oriented analyses and that no standardized independent accounting of all operating capacity is supplied.

**Evidence**

- One analysis reports that 82.5% of existing CCS capacity uses captured CO₂ in EOR and that almost all operational projects are connected to oil and gas. [S11]
- Another reports that 79% of operating global capture capacity sends captured CO₂ to EOR and argues that increased oil production remains the primary use. [S14]

### Finding 8

**Claim**

A reliable conclusion cannot currently be drawn about whether CCS will make a significant global contribution in practice, rather than mainly a targeted contribution in selected hard-to-abate sectors or carbon-removal pathways.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

This is an insufficient-evidence conclusion recorded in the ledger's open gaps and is consistent with the conflicting evidence on current performance versus modeled future potential.

**Evidence**

- The ledger explicitly identifies the absence of sufficient evidence to determine whether CCS can achieve a significant global contribution in practice. [S1] [S11] [S14]
- There is no consistent, independently verified accounting of current capture and permanent-storage capacity, actual capture rates, project reliability, or lifecycle net CO₂ reductions. [S1] [S8] [S12]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- The evidence supports both the existence of commercial CCS facilities and technically strong performance at some projects, while other evidence finds that major projects have underperformed or failed to demonstrate durable, climate-relevant net reductions. Operational status should therefore not be treated as proof of aggregate mitigation effectiveness. [S1] [S3] [S7] [S8] [S11] [S14]
- Scenario studies project a potentially substantial future CCS contribution, whereas current deployment is reported to capture less than 0.1% of annual global CO₂ emissions and remains far below gigatonne-scale requirements. Modeled potential therefore conflicts with demonstrated present-day scale. [S1] [S2] [S3] [S4] [S6] [S7] [S8]
- The reported 79–82.5% EOR share suggests that much operating capacity may not represent dedicated permanent climate mitigation, but the ledger does not provide an independently standardized accounting to resolve the exact share. [S11] [S14]

## Remaining Gaps

- No consistent, independently verified global accounting of current capture capacity, permanent-storage capacity, actual capture rates, reliability, or lifecycle net CO₂ reductions is provided.
- Comparable sector-specific costs—including transport, storage, monitoring, liability, financing, and energy-system effects—are not established against alternatives such as electrification, renewables, efficiency, or process substitution.
- The technically, economically, environmentally, and politically usable quantity of geological storage, and the long-term durability of storage, remain quantitatively unresolved.
- The evidence does not determine whether CCS can make a significant global contribution in practice or whether its principal value will remain concentrated in selected hard-to-abate sectors and carbon-removal pathways.

## Conclusion

CCS can plausibly make a meaningful contribution, especially for hard-to-abate industrial process emissions and potentially for some carbon-removal or hydrogen pathways. However, the supplied evidence does not justify concluding that it will make a significant global contribution in practice. Demonstrated operation is real but limited, current climate-relevant scale is far below scenario requirements, and major uncertainties remain regarding net effectiveness, costs, energy use, storage, permanence, and governance. The strongest evidence-based position is therefore conditional: CCS may be an important complementary tool, but large-scale deployment should not be assumed to substitute for other emissions-reduction strategies or treated as proven at the required global scale.
