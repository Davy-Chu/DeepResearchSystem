# Research Report

## Research Question

Can carbon capture and storage make a significant contribution to reducing global CO₂ emissions?

## Summary

This report evaluates the potential of carbon capture and storage (CCS) to significantly reduce global CO₂ emissions by assessing its demonstrated effectiveness, costs, scalability, energy requirements, and major arguments for and against its large-scale deployment.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) has demonstrated effectiveness in reducing CO₂ emissions in certain industries.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Multiple successful projects have been documented, demonstrating the capability of CCS in reducing emissions.

**Evidence**

- CCS projects have successfully captured millions of tons of CO₂ in industries such as oil and gas, cement, and power generation.
- Case studies indicate that CCS can capture up to 90% of CO₂ emissions from targeted facilities at scale.

### Finding 2

**Claim**

The costs associated with CCS deployment are currently high but decreasing over time due to advancements in technology.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

While costs are high, trends show potential for reductions as technologies mature.

**Evidence**

- The average cost for capturing CO₂ is currently estimated at $50-$100 per ton, depending on the technology and sector.
- Recent innovations have shown potential for reducing costs to below $30 per ton in certain applications.

### Finding 3

**Claim**

CCS scalability is hindered by several factors, including infrastructure development and regulatory environments.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

Limitations in existing infrastructure and regulatory frameworks limit the current scalability of CCS.

**Evidence**

- Only a limited number of CCS facilities are currently operational, largely due to high initial investment and lack of policy incentives.
- Challenges such as transportation of captured CO₂ and the availability of suitable geological storage sites pose significant hurdles.

### Finding 4

**Claim**

The energy requirements for CCS can be substantial, which may offset the reductions in CO₂ emissions in some scenarios.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The energy intensity of CCS processes needs careful consideration in evaluating net emissions reductions.

**Evidence**

- Estimates suggest that up to 30% of a facility's energy output may be needed to capture and store CO₂, impacting overall efficiency.
- Lifecycle assessments show that the net CO₂ reduction can be affected by the energy source used for capture processes.

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Some critics argue that reliance on CCS may divert investment from renewable energy technologies, delaying the transition to sustainable energy.
- There are concerns about the long-term efficacy and safety of geological storage of captured CO₂, with potential risks of leakage.

## Remaining Gaps

- More extensive long-term data on the performance and sustainability of CCS systems are needed.
- Research is necessary to improve the economic viability of CCS technologies and their integration into existing infrastructures.

## Conclusion

While carbon capture and storage presents a promising method for reducing CO₂ emissions, particularly from industrial sources, there are significant challenges including high costs, energy requirements, and scalability. Continued research and investment in CCS technology, supportive policies, and complementary investments in renewable energy sources are essential to realize its full potential in global CO₂ reduction efforts.
