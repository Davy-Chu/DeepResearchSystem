# Research Report

## Research Question

Can carbon capture and storage make a significant contribution to reducing global CO₂ emissions? Evaluate its demonstrated effectiveness, costs, scalability, energy requirements, and major arguments for and against large-scale deployment.

## Summary

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) is technically demonstrated and could make a significant contribution in absolute terms, but it is not currently operating at anything close to global scale. Present operational capacity captures roughly 50–64 MtCO₂ per year, less than 1% of global emissions. The evidence supports a targeted role—especially in cement, chemicals, steel, selected existing assets, low-carbon hydrogen, and durable carbon removal—rather than universal deployment or continued reliance on fossil-fuel power with CCS. Costs and energy requirements are substantial and highly site-specific, while storage availability, permitting, transport infrastructure, permanence, and project performance remain important constraints. CCS should therefore complement, not substitute for, direct emissions reductions, electrification, renewable energy, efficiency, and fuel switching.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Current CCS deployment has demonstrated technical operation but delivers only a very small share of global emissions reductions.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Multiple institutional and project sources agree that CCS operates at commercial-project scale but remains far below one percent of global emissions.

**Evidence**

- Operational CCS capacity is reported at approximately 50–51 MtCO₂ per year, compared with about 37,600 MtCO₂ per year of energy and industrial-process emissions—roughly 0.16%. WRI similarly reports approximately 50 MtCO₂ per year, or around 0.1% of global emissions. [S4] [S11] [S24]
- Petra Nova demonstrated commercial-scale post-combustion capture, transport and injection, capturing approximately 3.54 million metric tonnes during its three-year demonstration period. [S18]
- The IEA states that historical CCUS deployment has been slow and has had only limited impact on global CO₂ emissions. [S9]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

The demonstrated effectiveness of CCS is mixed and depends strongly on the metric and system boundary used.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The evidence directly establishes technical operation and monitoring, while also documenting materially different results under different boundaries. It does not support treating a high capture rate at one process unit as proof of whole-system, long-term climate effectiveness.

**Evidence**

- Petra Nova’s DOE report describes a design target of at least 90% capture from a 240-MW-equivalent flue-gas slipstream, while a CCS Knowledge Centre cites 92.4% capture efficiency for the processed slipstream. [S17] [S18]
- IEEFA estimates substantially lower performance using facility-level emissions data—approximately 65–70% capture, or 55–58% when emissions from the gas-fired cogeneration facility are included—and reports that the project was taken offline in 2020. [S16]
- University of Texas monitoring materials document capture, transport, injection, mass-balance accounting, pressure monitoring, geophysical logging, groundwater characterization and soil-gas monitoring. They report 3.61 million short tons sequestered out of 3.64 million short tons captured under the project accounting method. [S19]
- A broader project review identifies some projects, including Sleipner, the Alberta Carbon Trunk Line and Quest, as having consistently achieved high technical performance, while also documenting operational challenges elsewhere. [S20]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

CCS could become significant in absolute terms only through orders-of-magnitude expansion.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The current baseline and peer-reviewed scale-up estimates clearly show that meaningful global contribution requires very rapid expansion.

**Evidence**

- A peer-reviewed feasibility study estimates a maximum global storage rate of 16 GtCO₂ per year by 2050 under highly favorable conditions, but proposes 5–6 GtCO₂ per year as a more feasible benchmark. [S22]
- Current capacity of approximately 50–51 MtCO₂ per year is far below even the 5–6 GtCO₂ feasible benchmark, implying growth of roughly two orders of magnitude. [S4] [S11] [S24]
- Historical IEA and IPCC pathways cited by Carbon Brief envisaged approximately 2 GtCO₂ per year by 2030 and 7–10 GtCO₂ per year by 2050, illustrating the scale assumed in some climate pathways; these are older scenario figures rather than current forecasts. [S13]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

Geological storage is potentially large, but usable capacity is finite, geographically uneven and constrained by risk, permitting and infrastructure.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The peer-reviewed feasibility analysis provides strong evidence for geographic and scale-up constraints, while the prudent-capacity estimate and regional examples are not a complete global assessment.

**Evidence**

- A study summarized by Mongabay reports 11,800 GtCO₂ of mapped geological capacity but approximately 1,460 GtCO₂ of prudent usable capacity after excluding areas associated with water contamination, induced earthquakes, leakage risks and proximity to populated areas. [S21]
- The feasibility study finds that maximum deployment depends heavily on particular regions, especially the United States, and that some assumptions for China, Indonesia and South Korea are difficult to realize. [S22]
- Geoscience Australia distinguishes demonstrated resources from sub-commercial resources requiring further exploration and appraisal, showing that geological potential is not equivalent to commercially available storage. [S24]
- The European assessment identifies lack of domestic storage, long development timelines, permitting delays and cross-border transport constraints as practical bottlenecks in several countries. [S25]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

CCS has substantial and highly variable costs and energy requirements.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The evidence supports material energy and financial penalties, but the ranges are heterogeneous and cannot be treated as a universal cost or energy-per-tonne value.

**Evidence**

- The critical review gives indicative energy requirements of 2.5–8 GJ per tonne of CO₂ and costs of approximately $50–600 per tonne, emphasizing variation by source, technology, transport and storage conditions. [S1]
- A cited EU estimate places end-to-end capture, transport and storage costs at approximately €105–280 per tonne of CO₂, compared with an EU ETS average of about €75 per tonne in 2025 under the cited assumptions. [S7]
- Carbon Brief explains that capture, compression and injection consume energy that otherwise could serve the electricity grid or industrial production. [S13]
- Petra Nova cost approximately $1 billion to build and received substantial public and concessional financial support; IEEFA reports technical and financial problems and significant impairment charges. [S16] [S18]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

The strongest case for CCS is targeted deployment in hard-to-abate sectors and selected carbon-removal pathways.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The sectoral rationale is consistent across the IEA, WRI, project-track-record reviews and geological-storage analyses, although the comparative economics vary by location and technology.

**Evidence**

- The IEA identifies four strategic roles: retrofitting existing energy and industrial assets, addressing hard-to-abate sectors, enabling low-carbon hydrogen, and supporting atmospheric removal through bioenergy or direct air capture. [S5] [S9]
- Sources identify cement, steel, chemicals, aluminium, natural-gas processing and methane-based hydrogen as potential applications where alternatives may be limited. [S1] [S2] [S20] [S24]
- WRI distinguishes source capture from atmospheric carbon removal and explains that BECCS can provide removal because the biomass carbon was previously drawn from the atmosphere. [S4]
- The storage-feasibility evidence recommends prioritizing carbon-intensive applications that are difficult to replace with renewable alternatives and notes opportunity costs when storage is allocated to applications with alternatives. [S21]

### Finding 7

**Claim**

The principal arguments against broad CCS deployment are high cost, energy use, infrastructure and storage constraints, uncertain net climate performance, and the risk of delaying direct emissions reductions.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Multiple sources document these concerns directly. Their aggregate scale and frequency remain insufficiently quantified in the retrieved material.

**Evidence**

- The critical review documents cost overruns, suboptimal capture rates, geological uncertainties, durability concerns and possible transfer of liability to the public. [S1]
- WRI records objections involving moral hazard, continued fossil-fuel use, mixed project success, high costs and potentially disproportionate impacts on vulnerable communities. [S4]
- Many projects in the historical record were developed for commercial enhanced oil recovery rather than to maximize climate benefits; this complicates claims about permanent emissions reduction. [S13] [S20] [S24]
- Regional evidence shows that storage development, permitting and cross-border transport can lag behind capture ambitions, especially for carbon-removal projects. [S25]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Reported global operational capacity varies from approximately 51 MtCO₂ per year to approximately 64 Mtpa. The discrepancy likely reflects different dates, project populations, definitions of capture versus storage capability, and treatment of utilization projects, but the retrieved sources do not resolve it. [S11] [S12] [S14] [S24]
- Petra Nova is described both as meeting or exceeding a process-level capture target and as a commercial and financial failure. These accounts use different definitions of success: capture efficiency in the processed slipstream versus whole-facility emissions, uptime, economics, lifecycle emissions and long-term climate benefit. [S16] [S17] [S18] [S19]
- The IEA presents CCS as strategically important in selected net-zero pathways, while an IEEFA interpretation of the IEA’s 2025 outlook assigns CCUS less than 5% of modeled emissions reductions. These positions can coexist if CCS is important for particular sectors but modest in total global mitigation. [S5] [S7] [S9]
- Industry sources emphasize rapid growth in projects and infrastructure, whereas other sources emphasize that planned or nominal capacity is not equivalent to CO₂ actually captured and permanently stored. [S4] [S8] [S14] [S24]
- Storage estimates differ because theoretical geological capacity, prudent risk-adjusted capacity, technically feasible annual injection rates and permitted operating capacity are different quantities. [S21] [S22] [S23] [S24]
- The evidence does not establish decades-long retention, comprehensive lifecycle emissions, or the full climate effect of enhanced-oil-recovery projects, including additional oil and upstream emissions. [S16] [S18] [S19]

## Remaining Gaps

- A standardized, independently audited global dataset separating nominal capacity, actual capture, permanent geological storage and CO₂ utilization is still needed.
- Comparable facility-level data on uptime, energy consumption, capture rates, whole-system emissions and lifecycle climate performance are not available in the retrieved material.
- The full methods and uncertainties behind the risk-adjusted 1,460 GtCO₂ storage estimate have not been retrieved or compared systematically with other storage assessments.
- The amount of permitted and monitored storage and transport infrastructure that can operate at 5–6 GtCO₂ per year remains uncertain.
- The retrieved sources do not provide robust, sector-by-sector comparisons between CCS and electrification, efficiency, renewable hydrogen or alternative industrial processes.
- Long-term leakage rates, post-closure monitoring and liability arrangements are not quantified consistently across storage settings.
- The proportion of projected CCS deployment that would represent fossil-source abatement rather than durable carbon removal remains unresolved.

## Conclusion

Yes—CCS can make a significant contribution to reducing global CO₂ emissions, but only conditionally and mainly as a targeted complement to direct emissions cuts. Individual projects demonstrate that capture, transport, injection and monitoring can work, yet current delivered capacity remains below 1% of global emissions and the broader record is mixed. Scaling to several gigatonnes per year would require orders-of-magnitude growth, major investment, energy inputs, transport networks, permitted storage and robust monitoring. High costs, energy penalties, uneven geology, uncertain long-term and lifecycle performance, and the possibility of prolonging fossil-fuel use argue against treating CCS as a universal solution. The evidence most strongly supports prioritizing well-regulated CCS for genuinely hard-to-abate industrial emissions and carefully verified durable removals, while relying primarily on renewables, electrification, efficiency, fuel switching and other direct measures wherever they are available.
