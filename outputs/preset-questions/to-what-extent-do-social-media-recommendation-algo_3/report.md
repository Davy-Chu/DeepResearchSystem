## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

In two panel quasi-experiments, exposure to like-minded political arguments increased attitude and affective polarization more than exposure to opposing arguments; algorithmic selection did not generally amplify these exposure effects, although it produced a small additional attitude-polarization effect for one topic.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

S1 directly reports experimental comparisons and explicitly separates the effect of like-minded exposure from the incremental effect of algorithmic selection.

**Evidence**

- The study compared algorithmically selected with randomly selected arguments that were either attitude-congruent or attitude-incongruent. Like-minded exposure increased attitude and affective polarization more strongly than opposing exposure, while algorithmic selection generally did not amplify the effects and had a small direct effect for one topic. [S1]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

A browser-extension experiment on X that reranked, rather than removed, posts to increase or decrease exposure to antidemocratic attitudes and partisan animosity changed affective polarization after one week: reduced exposure made participants feel about 2.11 points warmer toward the opposing party, while increased exposure made them about 2.48 points cooler on a 100-point scale.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

S4 and S5 consistently report a randomized reranking experiment and quantitative affective-polarization effects, but the supplied materials are reports of the study rather than the primary article and do not provide its full methods or uncertainty estimates.

**Evidence**

- A report on the Science study states that 1,256 U.S. participants were assigned to increased or decreased exposure to antidemocratic and partisan-animosity content for one week, producing opposing-party feeling changes of 2.11 and 2.48 points. [S4]
- The institutional news report describes the browser-extension reranking experiment, its more-than-1,200 participants, the one-week intervention, and approximately two-point shifts in feelings toward the opposing party. [S5]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

The X reranking experiment altered political attitudes without changing the underlying set of followed posts: it changed the order and prominence of content, and the reported effects were not necessarily consciously perceived by users.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

Both supplied reports describe the intervention and its low perceptibility, but the primary study is not included and the materials do not establish whether all relevant political-attitude pathways operated solely through exposure order.

**Evidence**

- The experiment is described as reranking posts within users' existing feeds without removing content; participants reportedly could not notice the reranking, while exposure to partisan-animosity content was increased or decreased. [S5]
- The report states that the content and users remained the same and only ranking changed, and that 74% of participants reported noticing no impact on their experience. [S4]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

The available overview characterizes algorithmic effects on polarization as non-straightforward and argues that algorithms often reinforce pre-existing social drivers rather than independently determining polarization; feedback between user behavior and algorithmic curation makes causal attribution difficult.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The overview’s reinforcement account is now supplemented by a large randomized platform intervention showing substantial exposure effects without detectable attitude effects, while the X experiment continues to show that causal downstream effects can occur under some conditions. The best-supported conclusion is conditional and outcome- and design-dependent.

**Evidence**

- The review states that algorithms create feedback loops with social drivers, that evidence concerning polarization is far from straightforward, and that existing evidence suggests algorithms mostly reinforce existing social drivers. [S3]
- The study notes that prior research often could not separate effects of exposure to like-minded content from effects caused by algorithms themselves, and finds stronger effects for like-minded exposure than for algorithmic selection. [S1]
- The randomized Meta experiments found large changes in exposure and activity but no significant changes in polarization or key attitudes, supporting a non-straightforward and conditional account of algorithmic political effects. [S6]
- The project summary states that algorithms strongly shaped on-platform experiences while changing them did not alter political attitudes, reinforcing the distinction between algorithmic exposure and downstream polarization. [S8]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

The supplied evidence does not establish that recommendation algorithms change political participation, voting, or other political behavior; the reported downstream outcomes concern attitudes, affective polarization, and partisan feelings.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The new experiment adds measured on-platform activity outcomes but still does not establish effects on political participation, voting, sharing as political action, or offline behavior. The claim should therefore distinguish generic platform activity from political behavior.

**Evidence**

- The Meta experiment changed time spent and on-platform activity, but the reported downstream political outcomes were attitudes, knowledge, and polarization; it provides no evidence about voting, participation, or other political behavior. [S6]
- The summary lists null effects on political attitudes and polarization and does not report effects on voting or political participation. [S8]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

In a randomized 2020 U.S. election experiment on Facebook and Instagram, replacing personalized algorithmic feeds with reverse-chronological feeds substantially changed users’ on-platform experience and exposure: it reduced time spent and activity, increased political and untrustworthy content on both platforms, increased exposure to moderate friends and ideologically mixed sources on Facebook, and decreased uncivil or slur-containing content on Facebook. Despite these exposure and engagement changes over three months, it did not significantly change issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, or other key political attitudes.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

S6 provides a detailed primary-study summary of randomized experiments and distinguishes exposure, activity, and downstream attitude outcomes; S8 independently summarizes the same project and findings. The conclusion remains bounded to Facebook and Instagram during the 2020 U.S. election and the study period.

**Evidence**

- The study directly reports random assignment to chronological versus default algorithmic feeds, substantial changes in exposure and activity, and null effects on several political-attitude and polarization outcomes during the three-month intervention. [S6]
- The university summary reports that changing fundamental parts of Facebook and Instagram’s algorithms substantially changed content exposure but did not significantly affect political attitudes, polarization, ideological extremity, or candidate evaluations. [S8]

### Finding 7

**Claim**

The Meta 2020 election experiments provide evidence that algorithmic feed ranking can substantially alter exposure and on-platform engagement without producing detectable short-run changes in measured political attitudes or polarization, illustrating that exposure effects do not automatically translate into downstream belief effects.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The Meta evidence directly supports a separation between substantial exposure changes and null measured attitude effects, while the X experiment preserves a genuine cross-platform and intervention-specific conflict rather than eliminating it.

**Evidence**

- The randomized replacement of algorithmic ranking changed content exposure and time/activity while leaving issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, and other key attitudes statistically unchanged over three months. [S6]
- The project summary explicitly contrasts the algorithm’s large effect on what people see with the absence of significant effects on political attitudes and polarization. [S8]

### Finding 8

**Claim**

Differences in conclusions may reflect differences in the intervention and counterfactual: the Meta experiments replaced the entire default feed-ranking system with chronological ordering for three months, whereas the X experiment reranked particular types of content for one week; these designs alter different aspects of users’ information environments and may produce different downstream effects.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The sources directly establish differences in intervention type and duration, but they do not by themselves prove that these differences caused the divergent downstream results.

**Evidence**

- The Meta study identifies chronological feeds as the counterfactual to personalized ranking and emphasizes that total feed-system effects require a well-understood alternative; its intervention lasted three months. [S6]
- The X study report describes a one-week intervention that increased or decreased exposure to antidemocratic and partisan-animosity content through reranking. [S4]
- The institutional report likewise distinguishes reranking within existing feeds from broader feed replacement and reports the one-week duration. [S5]

### Finding 9

**Claim**

Evidence from the broader digital-media literature suggests that political effects vary by outcome and political system: participation and information consumption can be beneficial associations, while declining trust, populism, and polarization are more pronounced in established democracies; this broader pattern is relevant context but does not isolate recommendation algorithms.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

S7 is a large systematic review, but it concerns digital media broadly rather than recommendation algorithms specifically, so its relevance to the algorithm question is contextual and indirect.

**Evidence**

- A systematic review of 496 causal and correlational articles reports outcome- and system-dependent associations, with polarization-related associations more pronounced in established democracies. [S7]
