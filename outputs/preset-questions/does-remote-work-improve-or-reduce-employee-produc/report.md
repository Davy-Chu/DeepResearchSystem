# Research Report

## Research Question

Does remote work improve or reduce employee productivity? Compare evidence from controlled experiments, observational studies, and company-level data, and explain why different studies reach conflicting conclusions.

## Summary

The evidence does not support a universal productivity effect. Controlled experiments find positive or neutral effects in the settings studied: a mostly remote Ctrip call-center treatment increased measured performance by 13%, while a Trip.com hybrid experiment found no measurable performance penalty. Observational and survey studies often report stable or improved productivity, but these results are more vulnerable to selection, self-reporting, and adaptation effects. Industry-level evidence is mixed, ranging from a positive association with total-factor-productivity growth to little relationship with labor-productivity growth. Differences in tasks, work arrangements, worker selection, management quality, adaptation, and productivity measures explain much of the apparent conflict.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Remote work does not have a uniform effect on employee productivity; outcomes depend on the task, arrangement, worker population, and organizational context.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

This conditional interpretation is directly supported by the official BLS synthesis and corroborated by another review.

**Evidence**

- The BLS review states that effects depend on task type, technology, the home environment, worker motivation, and management practices, and reports positive individual-level findings alongside negative short-run case studies and near-zero aggregate results. [S3]
- A separate synthesis reports advantages for remote focus work but advantages for offices in collaboration and spontaneous problem-solving, concluding that leadership and work design may matter more than location alone. [S2]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

The strongest evidence from the Ctrip randomized experiment shows a positive productivity effect for a selected group of call-center employees working mostly remotely.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The result and sample definition are reported in the peer-reviewed publication record and an NBER institutional summary, including the randomized assignment.

**Evidence**

- The nine-month experiment randomly assigned 249 eligible volunteers from Ctrip’s call center to home or office work. Home workers’ measured performance rose 13%; about 9 percentage points came from more minutes worked per shift and 4 percentage points from more calls per minute, with no measurable difference in call quality. [S13] [S14]
- Ctrip was a 16,000-employee firm, but the randomized treatment sample was 249 eligible volunteers, not the entire workforce. [S11] [S13] [S14]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

The Ctrip experiment found divergent effects on productivity, retention, and career advancement: attrition fell, while promotion rates conditional on performance were lower for home workers.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The outcome pattern is consistently reported by the NBER and Stanford publication summaries; the visibility mechanism is explicitly presented as a possibility rather than a proven causal explanation.

**Evidence**

- Home workers’ attrition was approximately half that of office workers, but their promotion rate conditional on performance was almost 50% lower; reduced visibility to office-based managers was offered as a possible explanation. [S13] [S14]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

The post-experiment Ctrip productivity increase of approximately 22% reflects selection and learning, not a second randomized causal estimate.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The pattern is directly reported, but worker choice after the experiment eliminates random assignment, so the 22% figure cannot be interpreted as a clean causal effect.

**Evidence**

- After Ctrip expanded the work-from-home option and allowed employees to choose their location, more than half of the experimental participants switched arrangements and the reported productivity gain nearly doubled to 22%; weaker performers at home were reported to be among those returning to the office. [S13] [S14]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

The Trip.com randomized hybrid experiment found that working from home two days per week preserved measured performance and promotion prospects while reducing resignations by about one-third.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The result is consistently reported by an institutional Stanford source and an independent news account, and the underlying design is described as randomized; the retrieved material is nevertheless a summary rather than the full paper.

**Evidence**

- Stanford’s account of the experiment involving more than 1,600 employees reports that hybrid workers were as productive and as likely to be promoted as fully office-based peers, while resignations fell by 33%. [S9]
- An independent report describes the same 1,612-person experiment and reports equal performance across functions, with non-manager attrition falling from 7.2% to 2.4%. [S8]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

Observational and survey evidence generally reports stable or improving productivity, but it is weaker for causal inference than the experiments.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The sources consistently document positive perceptions and improvement over time, but survey responses and observational comparisons are vulnerable to selection, reporting bias, changing conditions, and adaptation.

**Evidence**

- Repeated surveys found that 70% of small-business owners initially perceived a productivity dip in early 2020, but the median owner reported a positive effect by 2021 after investments in technology, training, task redesign, and remote-management improvements. [S5]
- A Great Place To Work analysis of more than 800,000 employees reported stable or improved productivity after transition to remote work, using employee survey measures of effort and adaptability rather than direct output measures. [S4]
- Commercial summaries report that many remote workers perceive higher productivity and easier focus at home, but these figures are largely self-reported or based on proprietary analyses. [S6] [S7]

### Finding 7

**Claim**

Company- and industry-level data do not show that remote work necessarily reduces aggregate productivity, but they do not establish that remote work caused the observed outcomes.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The BLS evidence is large-scale and includes both positive and near-zero results, but industry associations and selected-company comparisons cannot identify a definitive causal effect.

**Evidence**

- BLS reports that, across 61 private-sector industries, growth in total-factor productivity from 2019–21 and 2019–22 was positively associated with increases in the share of remote workers after accounting for pre-pandemic productivity trends. [S3]
- The same BLS review cites a separate analysis across 43 industries that found little relationship between an industry’s ability to work entirely remotely and labor-productivity growth. [S3]
- A selected sample of high-performing Fortune 100 companies largely supported remote or hybrid work and reported substantially higher productivity, but the comparison does not separate the effects of work location from company culture and selection. [S4]

### Finding 8

**Claim**

Different studies reach conflicting conclusions partly because they measure different dimensions and levels of productivity.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The heterogeneity of measures and analytical levels is explicitly documented across the retrieved sources and provides a direct explanation for apparent disagreement.

**Evidence**

- The sources use outcomes including calls handled, minutes worked, call quality, performance reviews, promotions, lines of code, revenue, survey responses, labor productivity, and total-factor productivity. [S1] [S3] [S4] [S5] [S6] [S8] [S9] [S13] [S14]
- The evidence distinguishes individual focused-task output from collaboration, innovation, mentoring, learning, retention, and aggregate industry productivity; these are not interchangeable outcomes. [S1] [S2] [S3] [S9]

### Finding 9

**Claim**

Differences between fully remote and hybrid arrangements are central to interpreting the evidence.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The distinction is directly stated in the experimental accounts and is consistent with the reported mechanisms, although the long-run effects on collaboration and innovation remain incompletely measured.

**Evidence**

- The Ctrip experiment examined a mostly remote schedule for selected call-center volunteers, whereas the Trip.com experiment examined two remote days per week with three office days; the latter found equal measured performance and lower attrition. [S9] [S13] [S14]
- Stanford’s account distinguishes hybrid work from fully remote studies and notes that fully remote difficulties may arise in arrangements that are poorly managed or that provide fewer opportunities for office-based interaction. [S9]
- Other summaries identify collaboration, mentoring, brainstorming, knowledge sharing, and cross-team interaction as areas that may benefit from physical proximity. [S2] [S3]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- The Ctrip result is positive, while other reported evidence finds output reductions of 10–20% for some fully remote roles, particularly highly collaborative roles or newly hired workers. These findings concern different populations, tasks, and arrangements rather than establishing a universal contradiction. [S1] [S9] [S13] [S14]
- Industry-level evidence is mixed: BLS reports a positive association between increased remote work and total-factor-productivity growth across 61 industries, while citing another 43-industry analysis that found little relationship with labor-productivity growth. [S3]
- Survey and company reports often indicate stable or improved productivity, whereas some pandemic-era single-firm case studies found short-run declines. Early disruption, technological investment, task redesign, and management learning may account for part of the difference, but the retrieved evidence does not isolate their individual contributions. [S3] [S5]
- Some commercial sources make broad positive claims and report large self-reported effects, but they provide insufficient methodological detail to determine sample selection, survey wording, independence of estimates, or causal validity. [S2] [S6] [S7]
- One commercial summary described the Ctrip study as involving 16,000 call-center employees, while the primary-source records identify 16,000 as the firm’s total workforce and 249 as the randomized eligible sample. The primary-source interpretation resolves this apparent discrepancy. [S7] [S11] [S13] [S14]
- The retrieved material does not establish the long-run effects of remote work on innovation, mentoring, skill acquisition, collaboration quality, or team-level performance across occupations. [S1] [S2] [S3] [S9]

## Remaining Gaps

- The full primary Trip.com paper and complete statistical tables were not retrieved, so confidence intervals, detailed compliance, and subgroup estimates cannot be independently assessed here.
- The Ctrip participants were volunteers with at least six months of tenure, broadband access, and an independent home workspace; their representativeness of other workers, newly hired employees, and other occupations is uncertain.
- The available evidence does not provide harmonized effect sizes comparing controlled experiments, observational studies, and company-level data using common productivity definitions.
- The evidence does not determine whether fully remote work has different long-run effects from hybrid work on innovation, mentoring, learning, and team productivity.

## Conclusion

Remote work can improve productivity in some settings and reduce it in others, but the best-supported conclusion is conditional rather than categorical. Controlled evidence shows a positive effect for selected Ctrip call-center workers and a neutral productivity effect for Trip.com employees on a two-days-remote hybrid schedule. Observational and survey evidence is often positive but less causally reliable, while industry-level findings range from positive associations to no clear relationship. The apparent conflicts are largely explained by differences in task content, fully remote versus hybrid schedules, worker selection, management and technological adaptation, time horizon, and whether studies measure individual output, collaboration, retention, perceived productivity, or aggregate productivity. For the evidence retrieved here, hybrid work has the clearest support as a way to preserve measured individual performance while improving retention; claims about universal gains from fully remote work remain unsupported.
