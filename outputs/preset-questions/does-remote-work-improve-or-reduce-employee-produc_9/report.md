# Research Report

## Research Question

Does remote work improve or reduce employee productivity? Compare evidence from controlled experiments, observational studies, and company-level data, and explain why different studies reach conflicting conclusions.

## Summary

The evidence does not support a single productivity effect for “remote work.” Controlled experiments find positive effects in highly measurable, relatively independent call-center work and no measurable performance loss under a hybrid schedule for professional employees. An observational study of more than 10,000 IT professionals finds that fully remote work increased hours but not output, reducing output per hour by roughly 8%–19%. Company- and industry-level evidence is mixed, ranging from positive associations with total-factor-productivity growth to little relationship with labor productivity. The conflicts largely reflect differences in work arrangement, task coordination, outcome measurement, worker and firm characteristics, and the ability—or inability—to isolate remote work from other changes.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Controlled experiments suggest that remote work can improve individual productivity in highly measurable, relatively independent jobs, but hybrid work appears mainly to preserve performance rather than increase it.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The hybrid result is supported by the primary Nature article. The call-center result is consistently reported across sources, although the original Ctrip article itself was not retrieved.

**Evidence**

- The reported 2015 randomized Ctrip experiment assigned call-center workers to remote or office conditions and found that remote workers completed about 13.5% more calls, with gains attributed to more minutes worked and more calls per minute. [S1] [S5] [S7]
- The 2024 Nature randomized experiment at Trip.com assigned 1,612 professional employees to a hybrid schedule or five office days and found no statistically significant difference in performance reviews, promotions, code output, or marketing revenue. [S8] [S1] [S5]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

The strongest observational evidence in the retrieved material indicates that fully remote work can reduce productivity per hour in coordination-intensive IT work, even when employees work more hours.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The result is documented by institutional and research repositories and linked to a peer-reviewed publication. The exact decline is presented as 8%–19% in one source and approximately 20% in another, but the direction and broad mechanism agree.

**Evidence**

- Gibbs, Mengel, and Siemroth use personnel and analytics data from more than 10,000 professionals at an Indian IT-services company. Hours worked increased by roughly 30%, average output did not significantly change, and output per hour declined by 8%–19%. [S10] [S12] [S13]
- The reported decline was associated with more coordination and meeting time, less uninterrupted work, reduced networking, and fewer coaching and one-to-one meetings with supervisors. [S10] [S12] [S13]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

Total effort, total output, and productivity per hour can move in different directions.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The sources directly describe the different productivity definitions and their decompositions.

**Evidence**

- In the IT-services study, employees worked substantially more hours without a significant increase in average output, causing output per hour to fall. [S10] [S12] [S13]
- In the Ctrip experiment, higher total call output was attributed partly to more minutes worked and partly to greater output per minute. [S1] [S5]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

Company- and industry-level data do not yield a uniform conclusion.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

These results are reported by a government source and a policy review, but the retrieved material does not contain the full methods or underlying reports for all aggregate analyses.

**Evidence**

- The BLS analysis reports that growth in total-factor productivity across 61 private-sector industries from 2019–21 and 2019–22 was positively associated with increases in the share of remote workers, even after accounting for pre-pandemic productivity trends. [S5]
- The same BLS review reports that another analysis across 43 private-sector industries found little relationship between labor productivity and the ability of workers in an industry to work entirely remotely. [S5]
- A Bruegel policy review concludes that remote work helped maintain substantial productivity in many sectors during the pandemic, while emphasizing that feasibility varies by task and sector. [S14]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

Remote work may produce organizational benefits even when it does not increase direct measured output.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The retention result for Trip.com is supported by the primary Nature article; the broader retention pattern is also reported by the BLS review.

**Evidence**

- The reported Ctrip experiment found higher job satisfaction and substantially lower attrition among remote workers. [S1] [S5]
- The Trip.com hybrid experiment found no performance damage and a roughly one-third reduction in resignations. [S8] [S1] [S5]
- The BLS review notes that lower turnover associated with remote work can reduce hiring costs. [S5]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

Different studies conflict because they examine different treatments, populations, tasks, and outcomes rather than one identical version of remote work.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The differences in study design, work setting, and measurement are explicit in the retrieved sources and provide a well-supported explanation for the apparently conflicting findings.

**Evidence**

- The evidence compares a repetitive call-center experiment, a hybrid experiment involving engineering, marketing, and finance employees, and a fully remote observational study of skilled IT professionals. [S7] [S8] [S12]
- The retrieved reviews distinguish focused individual work from collaborative work and hybrid from fully remote arrangements; coordination-intensive work is more exposed to meeting, networking, and knowledge-sharing costs. [S1] [S4] [S10] [S12] [S14]
- The studies use different measures, including calls, calls per minute, performance reviews, promotions, code, revenue, hours, output per hour, total-factor productivity, and labor productivity. [S1] [S5] [S10] [S12]

### Finding 7

**Claim**

Observational and aggregate studies cannot cleanly attribute all observed productivity changes to remote work alone.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The non-random designs and concurrent changes are directly documented or follow from the study descriptions.

**Evidence**

- The IT study compares productivity before and during the pandemic rather than randomly assigning employees to remote work. [S10] [S12] [S13]
- The survey evidence documents simultaneous adjustments including technology investment, training, changes in task assignments, and improved remote management. [S2]
- The research state identifies pandemic shocks, worker selection, industry composition, changed tasks, and technology investment as potential explanations that are difficult to separate from remote work in observational data. [S2] [S5] [S12]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- The Ctrip randomized experiment reports higher call-center output, while the Gibbs et al. observational study reports lower output per hour among IT professionals. These are not estimates of the same treatment: the jobs, degree of collaboration, work arrangements, and outcome definitions differ. [S1] [S7] [S12]
- The Trip.com randomized hybrid experiment finds no performance damage, whereas the IT-services study finds lower hourly productivity under fully remote work. The contrast may reflect hybrid versus fully remote work as well as differences in coordination demands. [S8] [S12] [S14]
- The Gibbs et al. decline is reported as 8%–19% in one abstract and approximately 20% in other summaries. This is a minor numerical inconsistency; the sources agree on the direction of the finding. [S10] [S12] [S13]
- The BLS-reviewed industry evidence is mixed: one analysis reports a positive association with total-factor-productivity growth, while another finds little relationship with labor productivity. These aggregate measures operate at a different level from individual output and may reflect sector composition and other concurrent changes. [S5]
- Claims about longer-run innovation, mentoring, networking, onboarding, and career development remain less certain than the short-run output findings. A secondary source reports more siloed Microsoft networks and associations with innovation and career outcomes, but the retrieved material does not establish a causal effect. [S7]

## Remaining Gaps

- How much of the IT-services productivity decline was caused specifically by remote work rather than pandemic conditions, changed demand, management adjustments, or other simultaneous changes.
- How well findings from call centers and one IT-services company generalize to software development, creative work, professional services, managers, and other collaborative knowledge jobs.
- Whether coordination and communication costs diminish as firms and employees adapt over longer periods.
- How remote and hybrid work affect long-run innovation, mentoring, onboarding, networking, and promotion outcomes.
- How best to compare output per hour, total output, quality, innovation, retention, and firm-level productivity without treating them as interchangeable measures.

## Conclusion

Remote work neither universally improves nor universally reduces productivity. The most defensible synthesis is conditional: fully remote work can raise output in measurable, relatively independent jobs such as the reported call-center setting; hybrid work can preserve professional employees’ measured performance while improving retention; and fully remote work can lower output per hour in coordination-intensive IT work by increasing meetings and reducing uninterrupted work, even as hours rise. Company- and industry-level results are mixed because they capture broader organizational and sector effects and cannot always separate remote work from technology, management, selection, and pandemic changes. Thus, policy decisions should distinguish hybrid from fully remote arrangements, match location flexibility to task requirements, and evaluate productivity using role-specific objective outcomes rather than hours, self-reported productivity, or digital activity alone.
