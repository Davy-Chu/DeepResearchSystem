# Can Carbon Capture and Storage Make a Significant Contribution to Reducing Global CO₂ Emissions?

## Executive conclusion

Yes—but only as one component of a broader mitigation strategy, not as a substitute for rapidly reducing fossil-fuel use and improving energy efficiency.

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) is technically demonstrated. CO₂ has been separated, transported, injected underground, and monitored safely for decades, and some industrial facilities capture CO₂ at large scale. However, global deployment remains very small relative to emissions. Existing facilities capture roughly **45–50 million tonnes of CO₂ per year**, compared with approximately **37 billion tonnes of energy- and industrial-process CO₂ emissions annually**. This is around **0.1% of annual global emissions**.

The strongest case for CCS is in:

- cement and lime production;
- some chemical and hydrogen processes;
- natural-gas processing and other facilities where CO₂ is already concentrated;
- removal of residual emissions through **direct air capture (DAC)** and possibly **bioenergy with CCS (BECCS)**;
- industrial clusters where shared transport and storage infrastructure can reduce costs.

The weakest case is using CCS to justify continued large-scale unabated coal and gas power generation, particularly where renewable electricity, storage, transmission, and efficiency are cheaper and easier to deploy.

CCS could plausibly reach **hundreds of millions of tonnes per year by 2030** and potentially **several billion tonnes per year by 2050** under highly supportive policies and infrastructure investment. But it faces major constraints: high costs, energy penalties, limited project delivery, the need for extensive CO₂ transport and storage networks, uncertain economics, public opposition in some regions, and the risk that it delays direct emissions reductions.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore:

> CCS can make a significant contribution—especially in hard-to-abate industry and carbon removal—but it cannot carry the main burden of global decarbonization and should not be treated as an excuse to postpone emissions cuts.

## 1. What carbon capture and storage involves

CCS usually consists of four stages:

1. **Capture**  
   CO₂ is separated from an industrial flue gas, process stream, or the atmosphere.

2. **Compression and conditioning**  
   The CO₂ is compressed into a dense phase suitable for transport and injection.

3. **Transport**  
   CO₂ is moved by pipeline, ship, truck, or rail to a storage site.

4. **Storage and monitoring**  
   It is injected into deep geological formations, such as saline aquifers or depleted oil and gas reservoirs, and monitored to verify containment.

CCS should be distinguished from **carbon capture and utilization (CCU)**. Many uses of captured CO₂—such as synthetic fuels, beverages, or building materials—do not necessarily provide permanent storage. If the carbon is released later, utilization may only delay emissions rather than eliminate them.

CCS is also distinct from **carbon dioxide removal (CDR)**:

- Capturing CO₂ from a fossil-fuel or industrial process prevents part of an emission from entering the atmosphere.
- Capturing CO₂ from biomass or directly from air and permanently storing it removes CO₂ that is already, or would otherwise be, in the atmosphere.

## 2. Demonstrated effectiveness

### 2.1 Capture performance

Capture technologies are commercially mature in some applications and less mature in others.

#### High-purity and process CO₂

The most successful applications capture CO₂ that is already relatively concentrated. Examples include:

- natural-gas processing;
- hydrogen production;
- ammonia and fertilizer production;
- ethanol fermentation;
- certain chemical processes.

In these settings, capture can be comparatively inexpensive and energy requirements are lower because CO₂ separation is already partly built into the process.

#### Post-combustion capture

Capturing CO₂ from coal- or gas-fired power-plant exhaust is more difficult because CO₂ is diluted in a large volume of nitrogen and other gases. Amine-based systems can achieve high capture rates, commonly around **85–95% of the CO₂ in the treated exhaust stream**, depending on design and operating conditions.

That figure is not the same as a 95% reduction in lifecycle emissions. Residual emissions arise from:

- uncaptured flue-gas CO₂;
- additional fuel burned to supply capture energy;
- upstream methane leakage;
- extraction, processing, and transport emissions;
- construction and operation of the capture and storage system;
- occasional venting or equipment downtime.

A fossil power plant with CCS may therefore achieve a substantial, but not absolute, lifecycle reduction. Under favorable conditions, reductions of roughly **70–90%** relative to an unabated plant are possible; under unfavorable fuel-supply and operational conditions, the reduction may be considerably lower.

### 2.2 Operational evidence

Important projects have demonstrated the basic chain:

- **Sleipner** in Norway has injected approximately 1 million tonnes of CO₂ per year into a saline formation since 1996.
- **Snøhvit**, also in Norway, stores CO₂ separated from natural gas.
- **Quest** in Canada has captured and stored roughly 1 million tonnes annually from hydrogen production since 2015.
- **Boundary Dam 3** in Canada demonstrated coal-power capture, although its operational history illustrates the technical and economic difficulties of retrofitting power plants.
- **Petra Nova** in the United States captured CO₂ from a coal plant but ceased operation in 2020, largely because of economic conditions and the collapse in oil prices that undermined its enhanced-oil-recovery revenue model. It later restarted for a period.
- Several U.S. ethanol plants and natural-gas-processing facilities capture and store CO₂.
- The **Northern Lights** project in Norway is intended to provide shared CO₂ shipping and storage services for industrial emitters.

These projects demonstrate that capture and geological storage work. They do not yet demonstrate that CCS is broadly economical or easily replicable across the global economy.

### 2.3 Storage permanence

Deep geological storage is potentially very durable. Properly selected formations can retain CO₂ for centuries to millennia through:

- structural trapping beneath impermeable caprock;
- residual trapping in pore spaces;
- dissolution into formation water;
- mineralization over longer periods.

The main risks are not that all stored CO₂ will suddenly escape, but that:

- poorly selected sites may leak;
- old wells may provide leakage pathways;
- pressure management may be inadequate;
- monitoring may be incomplete;
- stored CO₂ may migrate beyond the intended storage complex.

Most scientific assessments conclude that well-designed storage can achieve very high retention rates. The challenge is establishing robust site characterization, monitoring, liability rules, and long-term stewardship.

## 3. How much could CCS contribute?

### 3.1 Current scale

The current contribution is small. Depending on how facilities are counted and whether nominal, nameplate, or actually captured volumes are used, the global operational capture capacity is approximately **45–50 MtCO₂ per year**. Some industry inventories report higher nominal capacity when facilities under construction or temporarily offline are included.

Against roughly **37 GtCO₂ per year** from energy and industrial processes, current CCS captures about **one part in 800** of annual emissions.

This gap matters. CCS is not currently operating at a scale that materially changes global atmospheric emissions.

### 3.2 Project pipeline

The number of announced CCS projects has grown rapidly, especially in:

- the United States;
- Canada;
- Norway;
- the United Kingdom;
- the Netherlands;
- Denmark;
- Australia;
- the Middle East;
- China.

Industry trackers report a pipeline potentially approaching **hundreds of millions of tonnes per year by 2030**, with announced projects in some inventories exceeding 400 MtCO₂ annually. However, announced capacity is not the same as delivered capacity. Projects may be delayed or canceled because of:

- uncertain subsidies;
- permitting difficulties;
- lack of transport and storage access;
- high capital costs;
- uncertain CO₂ supply;
- public opposition;
- technical problems;
- weak demand for low-carbon products.

The historical record shows a substantial difference between proposed and operating projects.

### 3.3 Long-term scenarios

Most major climate scenarios include CCS at significant scale, particularly for industry and carbon removal. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, International Energy Agency, International Renewable Energy Agency, and major integrated assessment models generally envisage:

- hundreds of millions of tonnes per year by 2030;
- roughly **1–7 GtCO₂ per year by 2050**, depending on the pathway;
- substantial use of CCS in cement, chemicals, hydrogen, and carbon removal.

The range is very wide because it reflects different assumptions about:

- how rapidly fossil fuels decline;
- the cost of renewable electricity;
- industrial demand;
- availability of sustainable biomass;
- DAC costs;
- policy support;
- the amount of residual emissions allowed in net-zero systems.

At the lower end, CCS mainly serves industrial applications. At the higher end, it is deployed broadly for industry, hydrogen, power, and removal. The upper end should not be interpreted as an established forecast; it is a demanding infrastructure and policy scenario.

## 4. Costs

Costs vary more than for almost any other climate technology because CO₂ concentration, facility size, energy prices, financing, transport distance, and storage geology differ substantially.

### 4.1 Capture costs by application

Indicative ranges are approximately:

| Application | Approximate capture cost |
|---|---:|
| Natural-gas processing or other high-purity streams | **$10–40 per tonne CO₂** |
| Ethanol fermentation | **$15–30/tCO₂** before transport and storage |
| Hydrogen, ammonia, and some chemical processes | **$20–80/tCO₂** |
| Cement and lime | **$50–120+/tCO₂** |
| Steel, depending on process | **$40–100+/tCO₂** |
| Gas-fired power | **$50–120+/tCO₂** |
| Coal-fired power retrofit | **$60–150+/tCO₂** |
| Direct air capture, current or early commercial systems | **roughly $250–600+/tCO₂** |

These are broad estimates, not universal prices. First-of-a-kind projects can be much more expensive. Costs may decline with standardization, larger equipment, improved solvents, modular designs, and experience.

Transport and storage may add approximately **$10–30/tCO₂** in favorable industrial clusters, but isolated projects, offshore shipping, or difficult geology can cost substantially more.

### 4.2 Cost of electricity with CCS

CCS increases both capital and operating costs for power plants.

The cost of electricity depends on fuel prices, financing, utilization, and capture design. In many markets:

- new gas or coal plants with CCS are more expensive than new wind or solar generation;
- gas CCS may provide firm power, but its economics depend heavily on gas prices and the cost of alternatives;
- retrofitting old coal plants is usually particularly difficult;
- the business case can improve where existing plants have long remaining lives, reliable storage access, and strong carbon prices.

The cost comparison must also account for system value. Dispatchable low-carbon generation can complement variable renewable electricity, but this does not automatically make CCS power the least-cost option.

### 4.3 Direct air capture

DAC is substantially more expensive because atmospheric CO₂ is only about 0.04% of air. Large quantities of air must be moved through contactors, and the capture material must be regenerated.

Current DAC energy requirements and costs vary by technology, but representative estimates include:

- roughly **5–10 GJ of heat per tonne of CO₂**;
- approximately **0.5–1.5 GJ of electricity per tonne**;
- current costs commonly estimated at **$250–600/tCO₂**, with some early projects potentially higher.

Future costs could fall, but claims of very low costs remain uncertain until demonstrated at large scale. DAC is most defensible for residual emissions and durable removals, not as a near-term replacement for preventing emissions at the source.

## 5. Energy requirements and efficiency penalties

CCS does not capture CO₂ for free. Separation, compression, transport, injection, and monitoring all require energy.

### 5.1 Power plants

For a coal or gas plant, the capture system usually requires steam and electricity. The “energy penalty” can mean:

- a reduction in net electrical output of roughly **10–30%**, depending on the plant and capture system;
- increased fuel consumption per unit of electricity delivered;
- additional cooling-water demand in some configurations;
- lower plant efficiency.

A power plant may need to burn significantly more fuel to deliver the same net electricity after capture. This increases exposure to upstream emissions and fuel costs.

### 5.2 Industrial facilities

Energy penalties are often lower in facilities where CO₂ is already concentrated. However, cement illustrates the difficulty of complete decarbonization: most cement emissions arise not only from fuel combustion but also from the chemical calcination of limestone. Electrification and renewable energy can reduce combustion emissions, but they do not eliminate process CO₂. CCS is therefore one of the few options capable of addressing the majority of cement-process emissions.

### 5.3 Direct air capture

DAC has especially large energy requirements because air contains little CO₂. If DAC were deployed at several billion tonnes per year, energy demand could become very large—potentially comparable to a substantial fraction of today’s global energy system, depending on technology and efficiency.

This does not make DAC impossible, but it means it must use genuinely low-carbon energy. If DAC is powered by fossil energy without effective capture, its climate benefit can be sharply reduced or eliminated.

### 5.4 Hydrogen and fossil fuels

“Blue hydrogen” produced from natural gas with CCS can have lower emissions than unabated hydrogen, but its performance depends on:

- capture rate;
- methane leakage;
- emissions from gas extraction and processing;
- whether upstream and downstream emissions are included;
- how the energy penalty is supplied.

A high capture rate at the hydrogen plant does not guarantee low lifecycle emissions if methane leakage is substantial.

## 6. Scalability

### 6.1 Geological storage capacity

The theoretical geological storage resource is very large. Global saline aquifers and depleted hydrocarbon reservoirs could, in principle, store many thousands of gigatonnes of CO₂—far more than likely requirements this century.

But theoretical capacity is not the same as usable capacity. Practical deployment is limited by:

- site characterization;
- injectivity;
- pressure management;
- pore-space ownership;
- permitting;
- distance from emitters;
- pipeline and shipping access;
- monitoring and liability;
- public acceptance.

The decisive constraint is likely to be the development rate of qualified storage sites and transport systems, not the total amount of underground space.

### 6.2 Transport infrastructure

Large-scale CCS requires a network analogous to natural-gas infrastructure, but with different engineering and regulatory requirements.

A mature system may need:

- dense-phase CO₂ pipelines;
- liquefaction and shipping terminals;
- intermediate storage;
- injection wells;
- measurement and verification systems;
- shared hubs serving many emitters.

Clustered systems are attractive because a single trunk pipeline and storage complex can serve multiple cement plants, refineries, chemical facilities, and waste-to-energy plants. This lowers unit costs and reduces the risk faced by individual emitters.

### 6.3 Materials and supply chains

CCS does not appear to require globally scarce quantities of lithium, cobalt, or rare earth metals on the scale associated with some electrification technologies. Nevertheless, rapid deployment would require expansion of:

- compressors and pumps;
- specialized steel and pipelines;
- solvents and sorbents;
- drilling capacity;
- engineering and construction labor;
- monitoring equipment;
- ports and ships.

The supply-chain challenge is serious but probably manageable if investment is sustained.

### 6.4 Biomass constraints for BECCS

BECCS can generate “negative emissions” if sustainably sourced biomass is burned or processed and the resulting CO₂ is stored. Its potential is limited by:

- land availability;
- food production;
- water demand;
- biodiversity;
- fertilizer use;
- transport emissions;
- uncertain carbon accounting;
- the risk of indirect land-use change.

Large-scale BECCS should not be assumed to provide unlimited removals. Sustainable biomass may support a meaningful but constrained contribution.

### 6.5 Public acceptance and local impacts

CCS projects can face opposition related to:

- pipeline safety;
- induced seismicity;
- perceived leakage risks;
- land acquisition;
- environmental justice;
- distrust of fossil-fuel companies;
- concern that CCS prolongs fossil extraction;
- unclear responsibility for future storage-site management.

Projects are more likely to gain acceptance where communities receive tangible benefits, monitoring is transparent, risks are independently assessed, and the project is part of a credible emissions-reduction strategy.

## 7. Major arguments in favor of large-scale CCS

### 7.1 It addresses process emissions that are difficult to eliminate

Some emissions cannot be easily removed through renewable electricity or efficiency alone. The most important example is cement:

- limestone is heated to make clinker;
- calcination releases CO₂ chemically;
- even a fully renewable kiln still produces process emissions.

CCS can potentially capture most of these emissions. Similar arguments apply to lime, some chemical processes, and certain waste-treatment facilities.

### 7.2 It can provide carbon removal

Net-zero pathways will likely contain residual emissions from agriculture, aviation, shipping, industrial processes, and other difficult sectors. Durable removals may be needed to balance those emissions.

CCS enables removal through:

- DAC with geological storage;
- BECCS;
- potentially some forms of carbon-negative industrial processing.

Without a scalable removal option, net-zero targets could depend on unrealistic assumptions that every source of emissions can be eliminated.

### 7.3 It can reduce emissions from existing industrial assets

Many industrial facilities have long lifetimes and are expensive to replace. Retrofitting capture may reduce emissions while allowing continued use of existing plants and infrastructure.

This is particularly relevant in regions where:

- industrial assets are relatively new;
- alternative low-carbon production technologies are not yet commercial;
- local energy systems cannot rapidly electrify all industrial heat;
- captured CO₂ can be transported to nearby storage.

### 7.4 It can support industrial competitiveness

Shared CCS hubs may help decarbonize industrial regions while preserving employment and production. Carbon management infrastructure could support low-carbon products such as:

- lower-emissions cement;
- low-carbon chemicals;
- hydrogen;
- synthetic fuels;
- captured-carbon building materials.

This may be important where governments seek to prevent “carbon leakage,” in which production shifts to jurisdictions with weaker climate policies.

### 7.5 It improves the robustness of climate strategies

Climate policy involves uncertainty about technology costs, energy demand, and public acceptance. CCS provides an additional mitigation option. Maintaining several pathways can reduce the risk that a single technology fails to scale.

## 8. Major arguments against or limitations of large-scale CCS

### 8.1 It is currently small and has a mixed project record

The existence of technically successful projects does not establish rapid, low-cost scalability. Some major projects have:

- captured less CO₂ than planned;
- experienced downtime;
- faced cost overruns;
- relied on enhanced oil recovery;
- been canceled before construction;
- operated below nameplate capacity.

A frequent criticism is that CCS has been “promised at scale” for decades but remains tiny compared with global emissions. This criticism is substantially justified.

### 8.2 It is often more expensive than avoiding emissions

For power generation, new renewables, efficiency, storage, and grid investment frequently offer lower-cost emissions reductions than adding CCS to new or existing fossil plants.

This is not universally true, especially for cement and some industrial processes, but it means CCS should be targeted where its comparative advantage is strongest.

### 8.3 Energy penalties can undermine climate benefits

Because CCS consumes energy, it increases fuel use and upstream emissions. This is particularly concerning for:

- coal plants;
- gas plants supplied by high-methane-leakage systems;
- DAC powered by carbon-intensive electricity;
- hydrogen systems using poorly controlled methane supply chains.

The relevant metric is lifecycle emissions per tonne of product or per unit of useful energy—not the capture rate at the smokestack.

### 8.4 It can prolong fossil-fuel dependence

CCS may be used politically to argue that coal, oil, and gas can continue at near-current levels. Critics warn that:

- fossil-fuel companies may favor CCS because it preserves their business model;
- public funds may subsidize uneconomic plants;
- CCS can divert investment from renewables, efficiency, electrification, and public transit;
- continued fossil infrastructure can create “lock-in.”

This risk is greatest when CCS is applied to power generation without strict emissions standards and when captured CO₂ is used for enhanced oil recovery.

### 8.5 Capture rates are not 100%

Even well-designed systems emit some CO₂. Claims that CCS creates “zero-emission” fossil energy are misleading unless they include:

- residual stack emissions;
- energy-penalty emissions;
- methane leakage;
- upstream fuel production;
- construction;
- transport;
- long-term storage performance.

### 8.6 Storage and transport carry real risks

CO₂ is not flammable, but dense clouds can displace oxygen and create a risk of asphyxiation after a major pipeline rupture. Underground injection can also cause pressure-related problems or induced seismicity, although these risks can generally be managed through site selection and operating limits.

Storage needs independent verification and long-term institutional responsibility. Poor governance could turn a technically manageable risk into a public-trust failure.

### 8.7 Carbon removal may create moral hazard

If policymakers assume future DAC or BECCS will remove billions of tonnes of CO₂, they may weaken near-term emissions policy. This is dangerous because removals are expensive, energy-intensive, land-constrained, and not guaranteed at the scale assumed in some scenarios.

Removals should therefore compensate for genuinely residual emissions, not justify continued avoidable emissions.

### 8.8 Utilization is often not permanent

Captured CO₂ used in fuels, plastics, or enhanced oil recovery may eventually return to the atmosphere. Climate claims should distinguish:

- permanent geological storage;
- long-lived mineral or building-material storage;
- temporary utilization;
- enhanced oil recovery with uncertain net benefits.

## 9. Where CCS is most and least appropriate

### Stronger applications

1. **Cement and lime**  
   One of the clearest cases because calcination generates unavoidable process emissions.

2. **High-purity industrial streams**  
   Natural-gas processing, ethanol fermentation, ammonia, and some chemical processes can offer relatively low-cost capture.

3. **Industrial clusters**  
   Shared networks reduce transport and storage costs.

4. **Waste-to-energy with biogenic content**  
   Can provide net removals if the biomass fraction and accounting are credible.

5. **Hydrogen and chemicals**  
   Potentially useful where methane leakage is low and capture rates are high, although electrified alternatives should be compared.

6. **DAC and BECCS for residual emissions**  
   Potentially important in net-zero systems, but currently expensive and resource-intensive.

### Weaker applications

1. **New coal power plants in regions with abundant renewable alternatives**
2. **Retrofits of old, inefficient fossil plants**
3. **Projects dependent on enhanced oil recovery to remain profitable**
4. **DAC powered by high-carbon energy**
5. **Blue hydrogen with high methane leakage or low capture rates**
6. **Projects with no verified storage site or transport plan**

## 10. Policy conditions for responsible deployment

If governments support CCS, several safeguards are important.

### 10.1 Require lifecycle emissions accounting

Regulation should count:

- uncaptured CO₂;
- energy-penalty emissions;
- methane leakage;
- upstream and downstream fuel emissions;
- transport;
- storage performance.

A facility should qualify as “low carbon” only if its total lifecycle emissions meet a stringent threshold.

### 10.2 Prioritize hard-to-abate sectors

Public incentives should be strongest where alternatives are limited, rather than indiscriminately subsidizing fossil power.

### 10.3 Use performance standards rather than unlimited subsidies

Policies should reward verified tonnes permanently stored or avoided, not merely installed capture capacity. Contracts should include:

- minimum capture rates;
- availability requirements;
- monitoring;
- penalties for underperformance;
- clear liability rules.

### 10.4 Establish independent storage regulation

Storage sites need:

- rigorous characterization;
- baseline monitoring;
- injection plans;
- leakage detection;
- financial assurance;
- post-closure monitoring;
- transparent reporting.

### 10.5 Build common infrastructure carefully

Shared hubs can reduce costs, but governments should avoid creating stranded pipelines or storage networks based solely on speculative projects. Infrastructure planning should be coordinated with industrial decarbonization strategies.

### 10.6 Separate removals from avoided emissions

A tonne captured from a fossil facility is not equivalent to a tonne removed from the atmosphere. Carbon markets and national inventories should prevent double counting and distinguish permanent removal from emissions avoidance.

### 10.7 Do not delay direct mitigation

CCS policy should be accompanied by:

- renewable electricity deployment;
- electrification;
- efficiency standards;
- methane reduction;
- coal phaseout;
- reduced oil and gas demand;
- industrial material efficiency;
- public investment in alternatives.

## 11. Overall assessment

### Demonstrated effectiveness

**Technically demonstrated, commercially uneven.** Capture and storage work, especially for concentrated CO₂ streams. Power-sector CCS and DAC are less mature and have a more difficult economic record.

### Cost

**Highly variable.** CCS is relatively competitive for some high-purity industrial sources but generally expensive for dilute flue gas and currently very expensive for DAC. Power-sector CCS often struggles against renewable alternatives.

### Scalability

**Potentially large but not yet proven.** Geological storage is abundant, but transport, permitting, financing, project execution, and public acceptance could constrain deployment. Announced capacity substantially exceeds operating capacity.

### Energy requirements

**Significant.** CCS consumes energy and reduces plant efficiency. DAC is particularly energy-intensive. Climate benefits depend on using low-carbon energy and controlling upstream emissions.

### Climate contribution

**Potentially significant in specific sectors and in carbon removal, but insufficient as a central global strategy.** Even several gigatonnes per year would represent an important contribution, especially for cement and residual emissions, but global mitigation still requires the vast majority of avoidable emissions to be prevented directly.

## Final judgment

Carbon capture and storage should be treated as a **targeted decarbonization and carbon-removal technology**, not a general-purpose license to continue emitting.

A credible global strategy would:

1. reduce fossil-fuel demand and emissions as rapidly as possible;
2. deploy CCS where process emissions are difficult to eliminate;
3. develop shared transport and storage infrastructure;
4. use strict lifecycle accounting;
5. reserve DAC and BECCS mainly for residual emissions;
6. require permanent, independently verified storage;
7. avoid allowing CCS subsidies to delay cheaper and more reliable mitigation options.

Under those conditions, CCS can make a significant contribution—potentially reducing several gigatonnes of CO₂ annually by mid-century. Without those conditions, it risks remaining an expensive niche technology or becoming a mechanism for prolonging fossil-fuel dependence.
