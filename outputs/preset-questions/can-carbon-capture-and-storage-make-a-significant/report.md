# Research Report

## Research Question

Can carbon capture and storage make a significant contribution to reducing global CO₂ emissions? Evaluate its demonstrated effectiveness, costs, scalability, energy requirements, and major arguments for and against large-scale deployment.

## Summary

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) can make a significant contribution to global emissions reduction, but only if deployed selectively and expanded dramatically. Its strongest case is in hard-to-abate industries with process emissions—such as cement, chemicals, steel and waste—and in some carbon-removal pathways such as BECCS and direct air capture with permanent geological storage. Current demonstrated deployment remains very small relative to global emissions and modeled net-zero requirements. Some facilities have achieved high technical performance, but the overall record is uneven, and planned or nominal capacity should not be confused with verified, permanently stored CO₂. High and uncertain costs, energy requirements, infrastructure needs, storage liability and the risk of delaying direct emissions reductions argue against treating CCS as a universal solution.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Current CCS deployment makes only a small demonstrated contribution to global CO₂ reduction, despite recent growth in projects and planned capacity.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Recent IEA operational data and the convergence of WRI and review estimates establish a clear gap between current activity and modeled requirements.

**Evidence**

- The IEA reports just over 50 MtCO₂ per year of capture and storage capacity in operation in the first quarter of 2025, and says current trends remain insufficient for a pathway to net-zero emissions by mid-century. [S7]
- WRI and a peer-reviewed review estimate that current CCUS/CCS activity represents around 0.1% or less of annual global CO₂ emissions—approximately 50 MtCO₂—while 1.5°C scenarios indicate roughly 1 Gt by 2030 and several billion tonnes by 2050. [S1] [S4]
- The IEA projects around 430 MtCO₂/year of capture capacity and 670 MtCO₂/year of storage capacity by 2030 based on the current project pipeline, but this is substantially larger than currently operating capacity and is not equivalent to demonstrated reductions. [S7]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

High capture rates and reliable storage have been demonstrated at some facilities, but performance across the wider project population is heterogeneous and facility capture rates do not by themselves establish equivalent net climate benefits.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The sources demonstrate both successful projects and significant shortcomings, but the supplied material does not provide a comprehensive, independently verified project-by-project dataset of net capture, leakage, downtime and lifecycle emissions.

**Evidence**

- C2ES states that capture technologies can capture more than 90% of CO₂ emissions from power and industrial facilities. [S3]
- CATF’s review identifies Sleipner, the Alberta Carbon Trunk Line and Quest as projects that consistently achieved high technical performance, while also documenting operational and technical challenges in other projects and applications. [S10]
- The critical review reports recurring suboptimal capture rates, cost overruns, geological uncertainty and shortcomings in some projects’ durability, scale or emissions-offset claims. [S1]
- WRI notes that geological storage and some concrete applications may be permanent, while CO₂ used to produce synthetic fuels is re-emitted when those fuels are burned. [S4]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

CCS has its strongest strategic rationale in hard-to-abate industrial sectors and selected carbon-removal applications, rather than as a general justification for continued fossil-fuel use.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Multiple sources converge on sectoral targeting and clearly distinguish point-source emissions control from atmospheric carbon removal.

**Evidence**

- The sources identify cement, lime, chemicals, steel and waste incineration as sectors where process emissions or high-temperature requirements may not be fully eliminated by electrification or alternative fuels. [S1] [S5] [S7]
- The critical review recommends targeted deployment in hard-to-abate sectors such as cement and in legacy industrial retrofits where alternatives are limited. [S1]
- The EU CCUS vision identifies BECCS and direct air capture with geological storage as potential means of achieving durable atmospheric carbon removal for residual and historical emissions. [S5]
- WRI distinguishes source capture from carbon dioxide removal and states that emissions reductions should provide the vast majority of mitigation, with removals addressing a smaller residual share. [S4]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

Large-scale deployment is technically and institutionally possible in principle, but would require expansion by orders of magnitude in capture, transport, storage, monitoring and permitting infrastructure.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The sources establish the scale gap and identify existing technical building blocks, but do not resolve the practical limits of storage capacity, permitting, infrastructure, workforce or public acceptance.

**Evidence**

- The IEA reports that 60% of the 2030 pipeline is at advanced stages or under construction, but operational growth in 2024 was marginal because the eight new projects were relatively small. [S7]
- The EU working-group report estimates that the EU may need to capture, use or store 300–640 MtCO₂ annually by 2050, including at least 150 Mt/year of atmospheric or biogenic-origin CO₂ stored for removal. [S5]
- The same report cites a global median requirement of 665 Gt of captured and stored CO₂ cumulatively by 2100 in a 1.5°C-compatible scenario. [S5]
- CATF reports existing experience with CO₂ capture, pipelines and geological injection, but notes that integrating these components for climate mitigation at broader scale is newer and more costly, particularly for diverse industrial sources and saline-aquifer storage. [S10]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

Costs and energy requirements are major constraints and vary substantially according to the CO₂ source, capture technology and application.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

A quantitative range is available, but the retrieved material does not provide comparable lifecycle cost and energy estimates by sector or explain the range’s methodology and boundaries.

**Evidence**

- The critical review gives an indicative energy requirement of 2.5–8 GJ per tonne of CO₂ and a cost range of approximately $50–600 per tonne of CO₂. [S1]
- The review also associates CCS projects with high costs and cost overruns, while WRI identifies stubbornly high costs and insufficient financial support as barriers to deployment. [S1] [S4]
- CATF notes that climate-oriented capture from sectors such as cement, steel and petrochemicals is generally more difficult and costly than capture from historically favored high-purity sources; transport and saline-aquifer storage add further costs and complexity. [S10]
- U.S. policy incentives described by C2ES include $85 per tonne for industrial and power-sector saline storage and $180 per tonne for direct-air-capture saline storage, illustrating the scale of support used to improve project economics. [S3]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

The principal arguments for large-scale CCS are its potential to address unavoidable industrial emissions, provide durable removals, support low-emissions energy and use established industrial capabilities.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The strategic rationale is supported, but claims about future system-wide contributions are model-based or advocacy-positioned rather than demonstrated by current deployment.

**Evidence**

- The IEA describes CCUS as potentially important to a low-emissions, secure and affordable energy system and reports renewed activity around hubs and networks. [S7]
- The EU report argues that CCS can address process emissions and high-temperature industrial emissions, and may support some low-carbon hydrogen and dispatchable power applications. [S5]
- CATF reports decades of experience with capture technologies, CO₂ pipelines and underground injection, providing a technical base for further deployment. [S10]
- C2ES reports that modeled pathways assign CCS a potentially material role, including an estimate of 14% of global emissions reductions needed by 2050. [S3]

### Finding 7

**Claim**

The principal arguments against indiscriminate large-scale deployment are uncertain costs and performance, energy use, infrastructure and liability challenges, opportunity costs, possible community impacts and the risk of prolonging fossil-fuel dependence.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

These concerns recur across the sources, but the material does not quantify their full lifecycle, social, health, opportunity-cost or long-term liability implications.

**Evidence**

- The critical review identifies energy demand, cost overruns, geological uncertainty, public liability transfer and the risk that reliance on CCS delays more effective solutions. [S1]
- WRI reports objections that CCUS can create moral hazard, act as a temporary fix instead of reducing fossil-fuel use, impose high costs and produce disproportionate impacts on vulnerable communities. [S4]
- CATF notes that many existing projects were motivated by commercial purposes such as enhanced oil recovery rather than maximizing climate benefits, and that saline storage and long-distance transport introduce additional complexity. [S10]
- The EU report identifies the need for cross-border coordination and regulatory action to develop transport and storage networks. [S5]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Reported CCS capacity differs substantially. The Global CCS Institute-linked source reports 513 MtCO₂/year of total capture capacity and a 54% year-on-year increase in operational projects, whereas the IEA reports just over 50 MtCO₂/year of operational capture and storage capacity. The retrieved material does not resolve whether the difference reflects nominal, total, announced or actually operating-and-stored capacity. [S6] [S7] [S9]
- The sources differ in strategic emphasis. C2ES presents CCS as capable of contributing materially to global reductions and as especially important for industrial decarbonization, while the EU working-group report concludes that “no CCUS, no net zero.” WRI and the critical review accept a role for CCS but caution against broad reliance and displacement of direct emissions reductions. [S1] [S3] [S4] [S5]
- Evidence of high performance at selected projects conflicts with broader claims of an uneven record. These positions are compatible if high technical performance is understood as demonstrated at some facilities rather than established across all projects or as proof of whole-system net emissions reductions. [S1] [S10]
- The sources do not provide sufficient project-level evidence to determine independently verified net reductions after accounting for energy penalties, upstream emissions, transport, compression, downtime, leakage and storage duration. [S1] [S7] [S10]

## Remaining Gaps

- A harmonized, independently verified measure of operating capture and permanent storage, excluding short-lived utilization and applications whose climate benefit is uncertain.
- Comparable lifecycle costs and energy requirements across cement, steel, hydrogen, power, natural-gas processing, direct air capture and BECCS, including comparison with non-CCS alternatives.
- Project-level data on capture rates, downtime, transport emissions, upstream emissions, leakage and long-term storage performance.
- A robust assessment of whether geological storage characterization, permitting, transport infrastructure, monitoring and long-term liability systems can expand at the rates required by modeled pathways.
- A clearer separation between CCS for point-source emissions reduction and CCS-linked carbon removal, together with analysis of how deployment needs change under faster electrification and renewable expansion.

## Conclusion

CCS can make a significant contribution to reducing global CO₂ emissions, but the evidence supports a conditional—not universal—role. Its current contribution is small, at roughly 50 MtCO₂ per year of operating capture and storage according to the IEA, and remains far below modeled requirements. Selected projects show that high technical performance and durable geological storage are achievable, yet the broader record is mixed and much existing experience is concentrated in relatively favorable, high-purity sources. Expansion into cement, steel, chemicals, waste, power and removal applications is more difficult, energy-intensive and costly. The most defensible deployment strategy is to prioritize genuinely hard-to-abate emissions and durable removal, require transparent performance and lifecycle accounting, and avoid allowing CCS to substitute broadly for renewables, electrification and direct fossil-fuel reductions. Under those conditions CCS could be an important part of global mitigation; current evidence does not support treating it as a standalone solution or assuming that announced capacity will automatically deliver equivalent climate benefits.
