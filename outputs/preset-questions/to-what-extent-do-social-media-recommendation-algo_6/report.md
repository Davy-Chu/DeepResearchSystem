# Social-media recommendation algorithms and political polarization

## Executive summary

Social-media recommendation algorithms clearly affect **what users encounter**: they rank, filter, and repeat some political content while reducing exposure to other material. The strongest evidence shows that ranking systems can increase the visibility of ideologically congenial content, emotionally charged material, partisan misinformation, and posts from highly engaged political actors. However, evidence that these changes **substantially alter political beliefs or political behavior is much weaker and more mixed**.

The best recent causal studies—especially large randomized interventions conducted with Meta and Twitter/X—often find that changing users’ feeds changes exposure and engagement but produces **little or no measurable short-term change in political attitudes, polarization, or voting-related outcomes**. Other experiments find effects, including increased polarization when people are exposed to opposing partisan content, greater ideological extremity after algorithmic amplification of emotional content, and changes in political knowledge or participation.

The literature reaches different conclusions because studies examine different outcomes, platforms, time periods, algorithmic interventions, user populations, and definitions of polarization. A feed can change the information environment without changing beliefs because users ignore much of what they see, interpret it through prior identities, encounter countervailing information elsewhere, or have already formed strong views. Conversely, effects may appear only for particular groups—highly partisan users, politically attentive users, or people exposed repeatedly over long periods.

The most defensible conclusion is:

> Recommendation algorithms are an important cause of unequal political exposure and may amplify social division, but they are not yet shown to be a dominant, general-purpose cause of mass political polarization. Their effects on beliefs and behavior are real in some settings, but usually smaller, more conditional, and less uniform than public debate often implies.

---

## 1. Clarifying the causal question

“Political polarization” can mean several different things:

1. **Ideological polarization**: people move toward more extreme policy positions.
2. **Affective polarization**: people increasingly dislike or distrust members of the opposing party.
3. **Perceptual polarization**: people develop more distorted beliefs about the opposing side.
4. **Epistemic polarization**: groups inhabit increasingly different factual information environments.
5. **Behavioral polarization**: changes in voting, protest, political donations, harassment, or partisan participation.

Recommendation systems can affect these outcomes through several stages:

\[
\text{Algorithmic ranking}
\rightarrow \text{exposure}
\rightarrow \text{attention and engagement}
\rightarrow \text{beliefs and emotions}
\rightarrow \text{political behavior}
\]

Evidence is strongest for the first link and substantially weaker for the last two. It is therefore important not to treat evidence that algorithms change exposure as evidence that they change political beliefs.

---

# 2. Evidence that algorithms change what users see

## 2.1 Ranking systems substantially alter exposure

Most major platforms do not show posts chronologically. They rank content according to predicted engagement, relevance, prior interactions, and other signals. This means the algorithm determines not only what is available but what is prominent, repeated, and encountered first.

Large-scale studies using user-level data have found that ranking systems can produce measurable changes in the political composition of users’ feeds.

### Facebook and Instagram

A major collaboration between Meta and independent academic researchers during the 2020 U.S. election compared users exposed to Facebook’s standard algorithmic feed with users assigned to a chronological feed.

The associated studies found that:

- the algorithmic feed and chronological feed produced substantially different patterns of exposure;
- changing the feed reduced exposure to some politically congenial content and changed the amount of political material users saw;
- the intervention affected engagement and the composition of information flows;
- nevertheless, it generally did not produce detectable changes in several measures of political attitudes over the study period.

These results are important because they directly establish that algorithms matter for information exposure even when downstream persuasion effects are absent.

A related study of Facebook found that users tend to see more content from politically like-minded sources than from opposing sources. But the amount of ideological segregation attributable specifically to Facebook’s ranking algorithm was often smaller than the segregation produced by users’ own choices—whom they follow, friend, click, and engage with.

### Twitter/X

Twitter’s 2021–2022 algorithmic-ranking research compared the standard “For You” or home timeline with a chronological feed. The results indicated that the algorithmic feed:

- amplified some political content more than a chronological feed;
- favored right-leaning political content in several countries, particularly in the United States;
- amplified posts from political actors and content that generated strong engagement;
- did not necessarily produce a uniform ideological “echo chamber.”

The study by Huszár and colleagues found that Twitter’s ranking algorithm amplified right-leaning political content relative to the reverse-chronological alternative in the countries examined. This is evidence of differential exposure, not by itself evidence that users became more conservative or more polarized.

### News-feed experimentation

The Facebook “emotional contagion” experiment showed that reducing the presence of emotionally positive or negative posts affected the emotional tone of users’ own posts. Although that study was not specifically about political polarization, it demonstrated that feed-ranking changes can influence users’ expressive behavior.

Other work has shown that engagement-based ranking tends to elevate:

- moral-emotional language;
- anger and outrage;
- identity-based political content;
- sensational or misleading claims;
- posts that provoke rapid reactions.

Such content can be more polarizing than neutral or informational material, but amplification is not equivalent to persuasion. The system may increase the visibility of outrage without changing users’ underlying views.

---

## 2.2 User choice often matters as much as, or more than, the algorithm

A recurring finding is that political segregation emerges from both:

1. **algorithmic selection**, and
2. **human selection**.

Users choose whom to follow, friend, mute, block, click, and share. They also spend more time with content that confirms their political identities. Algorithms then learn from those choices and reinforce them.

Bakshy, Messing, and Adamic’s influential study of Facebook users found substantial ideological segregation in cross-cutting news exposure. But it estimated that individual choices contributed more to the observed segregation than algorithmic ranking did, although ranking still had a measurable effect.

This finding complicates the simple narrative that “the algorithm creates the bubble.” A more accurate model is:

> Users’ preferences shape the data the algorithm learns from; the algorithm then sorts and amplifies those preferences, potentially making them more persistent and visible.

The algorithm may therefore be a **multiplier or accelerator** rather than the original source of polarization.

---

## 2.3 Algorithms can change the quality and emotional character of exposure

Even where algorithms do not dramatically change users’ left-right ideological balance, they can change the *type* of political material users see.

Engagement-based systems tend to reward content that is:

- emotionally arousing;
- socially threatening;
- morally judgmental;
- identity-relevant;
- novel or surprising;
- easy to react to.

Research by Brady and colleagues, for example, found that moral-emotional language increased the diffusion of political messages, especially within users’ own ideological communities. Work by Robertson and others similarly found that social-media environments reward emotional and partisan content.

This may contribute to affective polarization by repeatedly exposing users to portrayals of the opposing group as immoral, dangerous, or stupid—even if it does not move policy preferences.

# 3. Evidence that algorithms change political beliefs

The evidence here is considerably less consistent.

## 3.1 Large randomized feed interventions often find little short-term attitude change

The strongest recent evidence comes from randomized experiments around the 2020 U.S. election involving Facebook and Instagram users.

Researchers altered important parts of participants’ social-media experience, including:

- switching from an algorithmically ranked feed to a chronological feed;
- reducing political content;
- changing exposure to reshared content;
- modifying the amount of platform use.

The interventions changed what users saw and how they interacted with the platforms. Yet the studies generally found:

- little or no effect on political knowledge;
- little or no effect on issue positions;
- little or no effect on affective polarization;
- little or no effect on reported political participation or voting-related outcomes over the study period.

These results appeared in a set of papers published in *Science* in 2023, including work by Guess and colleagues, Nyhan and colleagues, and others.

The findings do not show that algorithms never affect beliefs. They show that even fairly substantial short-term changes in Facebook and Instagram feeds did not cause broad changes in major political attitudes among the studied users.

Several interpretations are possible:

- users were exposed to changed content for only a limited period;
- many users had already formed stable views;
- users obtained political information from television, news sites, friends, and other platforms;
- altered feeds changed content exposure but not actual attention;
- the relevant psychological effects may accumulate over years rather than weeks;
- the interventions did not eliminate algorithmic personalization altogether.

## 3.2 Some experiments find polarization effects from opposing-content exposure

A well-known experiment by Bail and colleagues exposed Twitter users to automated accounts that posted content from the opposing political side. Rather than reducing polarization, exposure to opposing views increased political polarization among some participants, particularly Republicans in the study.

This result is important because it shows that more ideological diversity does not automatically produce moderation. Cross-cutting exposure can:

- provoke threat responses;
- reinforce group identity;
- make opponents seem more extreme;
- increase motivated reasoning;
- encourage defensive counter-arguing.

However, the study did not directly reproduce a platform’s complete recommendation algorithm. It tested a particular form of repeated exposure to selected opposing content. It therefore demonstrates a possible mechanism, not the average effect of all algorithmic recommendation.

Other studies similarly find that exposure to hostile or out-group political content can increase anger and affective polarization, especially when the content is perceived as threatening or contemptuous.

## 3.3 Engagement-based ranking may affect attitudes indirectly

Some experiments and observational studies suggest that algorithmic amplification of emotional or partisan content can influence:

- emotional reactions;
- perceptions of group norms;
- willingness to share political content;
- hostility toward opposing groups;
- support for political violence or anti-democratic claims in vulnerable subgroups.

The evidence is strongest for intermediate psychological outcomes, such as anger, moral outrage, perceived threat, and group identification. Evidence that these effects translate into durable changes in policy preferences or voting behavior is more limited.

In other words, algorithms may be more effective at changing **how people feel and communicate** than at changing **what they believe about policy**.

---

# 4. Evidence that algorithms change political behavior

Political behavior includes voting, turnout, donating, sharing, protesting, harassment, and political violence. Evidence varies sharply across these outcomes.

## 4.1 Engagement and sharing

There is strong evidence that recommendation systems influence:

- which political posts users click;
- what they share;
- how often they comment;
- which political accounts gain reach;
- the spread of misinformation and outrage.

This is partly because ranking affects the probability that content is seen, and partly because users respond to incentives created by visibility and engagement.

A post that is emotionally provocative is more likely to be rewarded with distribution. Political actors may therefore adapt their communication to the system, producing more inflammatory content even if ordinary users’ beliefs remain stable.

## 4.2 Voting and participation

Evidence that recommendations substantially alter turnout or vote choice is much weaker.

Some platform-based interventions have increased civic participation—for example, Facebook’s 2010 “I Voted” message was associated with increased turnout among some users. But that type of intervention is a direct mobilization message, not an ordinary recommendation algorithm.

The 2020 Meta feed experiments found little evidence that short-term changes in algorithmic ranking significantly changed broad political participation outcomes. This suggests that ordinary feed curation may have less effect on voting than direct mobilization, campaign contact, or offline political factors.

## 4.3 Political violence and anti-democratic behavior

Platforms may contribute to radicalization by facilitating:

- repeated exposure to conspiratorial narratives;
- recruitment into extremist communities;
- social reinforcement;
- normalization of dehumanizing language;
- coordination and mobilization.

Research on YouTube, Facebook groups, and extremist networks documents pathways of escalation. But establishing the independent causal effect of recommendation algorithms is difficult because users often actively seek extremist content, move across platforms, and interact with offline organizations.

The existence of a recommendation pathway does not establish that the algorithm caused an individual to become extremist. It may instead make already interested individuals more efficiently connected to like-minded networks.

# 5. Why the literature reaches different conclusions

## 5.1 Different causal estimands

Studies are often asking different questions:

- Does ranking change content exposure?
- Does it change exposure to ideologically congenial content?
- Does it alter immediate emotional reactions?
- Does it shift policy attitudes?
- Does it affect affective polarization?
- Does it change voting?

A positive answer to the first question and a negative answer to the last are perfectly compatible.

## 5.2 Different algorithmic interventions

“Changing the algorithm” can mean very different things:

- replacing ranking with chronological order;
- reducing political content;
- changing recommendations;
- suppressing reshares;
- altering engagement-based ranking;
- introducing opposing political content;
- recommending content from outside a user’s network.

An intervention may change exposure dramatically without changing the psychological mechanisms that matter for beliefs. Another may deliver highly provocative content to a targeted group and produce stronger effects.

## 5.3 Short-term experiments versus long-term social processes

Most randomized experiments last days or weeks. Polarization may develop through cumulative exposure over months or years.

Long-term effects could operate through:

- repeated identity reinforcement;
- gradual normalization of extreme claims;
- changes in perceived social norms;
- migration into more isolated communities;
- political actor adaptation;
- cumulative distrust of institutions.

Short experiments are excellent for identifying immediate causal effects but may underestimate slow-moving effects. Conversely, long-term observational studies may capture cumulative effects but cannot easily separate algorithms from self-selection and broader political trends.

## 5.4 Selection effects and reverse causation

People with strong political views are more likely to:

- seek partisan content;
- follow ideologically similar accounts;
- engage with outrage;
- join political groups;
- believe misinformation consistent with their identities.

Thus, a correlation between algorithmic exposure and polarization may reflect the fact that polarized users generate the behavior from which the algorithm learns.

This is a central identification problem:

\[
\text{Polarized users} \rightarrow \text{polarized choices}
\rightarrow \text{algorithmic recommendations}
\]

rather than:

\[
\text{algorithmic recommendations} \rightarrow \text{polarized users}
\]

Both processes can occur simultaneously.

## 5.5 Exposure is not attention

A feed may display content without users reading or believing it. Researchers increasingly distinguish:

- **potential exposure**: content appeared on screen;
- **actual exposure**: users noticed or consumed it;
- **engagement**: users clicked, liked, commented, or shared;
- **internalization**: users accepted the claim;
- **behavioral consequence**: users acted differently.

Many studies measure only the first or second stage. The gap between exposure and belief is especially important for political content, where users actively interpret information through partisan identities.

## 5.6 Effects differ across users

Average treatment effects can conceal important subgroup effects. Recommendation systems may have stronger effects on:

- politically inattentive users;
- younger users;
- highly engaged users;
- people with weak prior opinions;
- users isolated from alternative news;
- people susceptible to social identity or threat cues.

At the same time, highly partisan users may be more responsive to reinforcing content but less persuadable on policy issues. An intervention could therefore increase affective hostility without changing ideological positions.

## 5.7 Platforms differ

Findings from Facebook cannot automatically be generalized to:

- Twitter/X, where public political discussion is central;
- YouTube, where recommendations can produce long viewing sequences;
- TikTok, where entertainment and politics are mixed;
- Instagram, where visual identity and influencers matter;
- encrypted messaging platforms, where recommendations are less central.

The social context, user base, ranking objective, and content format all affect the consequences.

## 5.8 Political context matters

The same algorithm may have different effects during:

- ordinary periods;
- elections;
- war or crisis;
- protests;
- public-health emergencies;
- moments of institutional distrust.

Political elites, journalists, activists, and ordinary users also respond strategically to ranking changes. Effects may be larger when salient events provide emotionally charged content for the system to amplify.

## 5.9 Measurement of polarization is inconsistent

Studies use different measures:

- feeling thermometers;
- ideological self-placement;
- policy scales;
- partisan stereotypes;
- support for compromise;
- willingness to interact with opponents;
- emotional language;
- news diversity;
- exposure to opposing views.

An algorithm could increase outrage while leaving policy positions unchanged. Depending on the measure, the same intervention might appear to increase polarization, have no effect, or even reduce it.

# 6. Major mechanisms

## 6.1 Selective exposure and reinforcement

Algorithms learn that users prefer familiar political material and supply more of it. Repeated confirmation can strengthen confidence and reduce exposure to corrective information.

The effect is likely strongest when:

- users have strong partisan identities;
- content is socially reinforced by friends;
- alternative sources are hard to find;
- the platform is a major source of news.

## 6.2 Engagement optimization

Systems optimized for clicks, watch time, comments, or shares may favor content that provokes arousal. Anger and moral outrage are especially effective at generating interaction.

This can produce **expressive polarization**: more hostile language and more visible conflict, even without large changes in private beliefs.

## 6.3 Social proof and perceived norms

High-ranking content appears popular or socially approved. Users may infer that:

- extreme views are common;
- opponents are more threatening than they really are;
- one’s own group is under attack;
- political hostility is expected.

These perceived norms can affect speech and behavior independently of persuasion.

## 6.4 Network formation and group isolation

Recommendations can help users find politically similar communities. Once formed, these communities provide:

- identity;
- emotional support;
- repeated narratives;
- social rewards;
- sanctions against dissent.

The important causal effect may not be a single recommendation but the gradual construction of a social environment.

## 6.5 Elite adaptation

Political campaigns, influencers, and media organizations learn what the algorithm rewards. They may produce more:

- sensational headlines;
- moral condemnation;
- identity-based appeals;
- misleading but engaging claims.

Thus, algorithms can affect polarization indirectly by changing the political supply side.

---

# 7. What the strongest evidence supports

## High confidence

Recommendation algorithms:

- change the ordering and visibility of political content;
- amplify some emotional, moralized, and partisan content;
- create different information environments for different users;
- interact with user choices to produce ideological segregation;
- affect engagement, sharing, and expressive behavior.

## Moderate confidence

Recommendation algorithms can:

- increase affective hostility under some conditions;
- reinforce existing partisan identities;
- intensify anger and perceived threat;
- help sustain extremist or conspiratorial communities;
- shape political communication by rewarding provocative content.

## Low or mixed confidence

There is limited and inconsistent evidence that ordinary recommendation systems generally:

- cause large shifts in policy ideology;
- make the average user substantially more politically extreme;
- determine vote choice;
- produce broad changes in turnout;
- are the primary cause of national-level polarization.

The evidence does not justify either extreme claim that algorithms are harmless or that they single-handedly created contemporary polarization.

---

# 8. Overall assessment

The contribution of recommendation algorithms is best understood as **conditional amplification**.

They do not create political identities from nothing. Polarization is also driven by:

- partisan realignment;
- elite rhetoric;
- economic and racial conflict;
- institutional distrust;
- partisan media;
- geographic and social sorting;
- offline organizations;
- major political events.

But algorithms can make these divisions more visible, emotionally intense, socially reinforced, and rapidly transmissible. They may be especially consequential for affective polarization, political communication, and the formation of high-conflict communities.

The most plausible causal model is therefore:

> Algorithms usually have modest average effects on deeply held political beliefs, but potentially larger effects on exposure, emotional reactions, social norms, political expression, and the development of highly engaged polarized subcommunities.

That distinction explains why large experiments can find little change in average attitudes while platform audits and network studies find substantial changes in what people see and share.
