# Research Report

## Research Question

Can carbon capture and storage make a significant contribution to reducing global CO₂ emissions? Evaluate its demonstrated effectiveness, costs, scalability, energy requirements, and major arguments for and against large-scale deployment.

## Summary

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) can make a significant contribution to emissions reduction, particularly for cement, lime, chemicals, hydrogen, and some existing industrial facilities where direct electrification or material substitution is difficult. It is also a potential source of carbon dioxide removal when paired with sustainable biomass (BECCS) or direct air capture (DAC), although those applications are less mature and generally more expensive. CCS is technically demonstrated, but deployment remains far below the scale implied by climate targets. Capture rates at individual facilities can be high, yet whole-system emissions reductions are lower once energy use, upstream fuel emissions, transport, storage, and incomplete capture are included. Costs vary widely: roughly tens of dollars per tonne for some concentrated industrial streams, around $50–$120+ per tonne for many power and industrial applications, and commonly $100–$600+ per tonne for DAC. Large-scale deployment is constrained less by basic capture physics than by high capital costs, energy penalties, transport and storage infrastructure, permitting, liability, monitoring, and uncertain business models. The strongest evidence supports CCS as a targeted component of a broader strategy—not as a justification for continued unabated fossil-fuel use or a substitute for efficiency, renewable energy, electrification, methane reduction, and demand reduction.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

CCS is technically demonstrated and can achieve high capture rates at individual facilities, but global deployment is still small relative to emissions.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The basic status of CCS and the large gap between current deployment and modeled climate pathways are consistently reported by major independent assessments.

**Evidence**

- The IPCC assesses CO₂ capture from industrial facilities, power plants, and hydrogen production as technically viable, while noting that deployment has been limited and uneven.
- The Global CCS Institute reports more than 40 commercial CCS facilities in operation or under construction and a much larger project pipeline, but operating capture capacity remains only on the order of a few tens of millions of tonnes of CO₂ per year.
- The IEA estimates that captured CO₂ volumes must rise from roughly 0.05 GtCO₂ per year in the early 2020s to several gigatonnes per year by 2030 in net-zero pathways.

### Finding 2

**Claim**

Capture performance can be effective at the plant level, but headline capture rates should not be interpreted as equivalent to total lifecycle emissions reductions.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The distinction between capture-rate performance and lifecycle abatement is well established; exact net reductions depend on plant design, fuel supply, utilization, and accounting boundaries.

**Evidence**

- Post-combustion and pre-combustion systems are commonly designed for approximately 85–95% capture from the treated CO₂ stream; some newer projects target higher rates.
- Additional emissions arise from the energy required to operate capture systems, construction, solvent production, CO₂ compression, transport, storage operations, and upstream methane or CO₂ leakage.
- The Boundary Dam coal CCS project demonstrated capture at a commercial power plant, but its operational history also illustrated lower-than-design availability, maintenance challenges, and the importance of system integration.

### Finding 3

**Claim**

CCS is likely to be most valuable in sectors with concentrated CO₂ streams and few low-carbon alternatives, especially cement and some chemical processes.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Sectoral suitability is supported by process chemistry and repeated modeling assessments, although the preferred technology varies by region and future energy prices.

**Evidence**

- Around half of cement-process emissions arise from the chemical calcination of limestone, which cannot be eliminated through renewable electricity alone; CCS can address this process CO₂ if captured and permanently stored.
- CCS can reduce emissions from hydrogen and ammonia production, refining, iron and steel, waste-to-energy, and certain chemical facilities, although alternative pathways such as renewable hydrogen and direct reduced iron may reduce its role in some applications.
- CO₂ transport and storage networks can serve multiple industrial sources, improving economics compared with isolated projects.

### Finding 4

**Claim**

CCS costs are highly variable and are generally higher for dilute or mobile sources than for concentrated industrial streams.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The direction and relative ranking of costs are robust, but numerical ranges vary substantially across technologies, locations, financing assumptions, and project maturity.

**Evidence**

- Capture costs can be relatively low for high-purity streams such as natural-gas processing, ethanol, ammonia, and some hydrogen production, while costs are higher for cement, steel, refinery flue gas, and coal or gas power plants.
- Indicative cost ranges for capture, compression, transport, and storage are often approximately $40–$120 per tonne of CO₂ for many industrial and power applications, but project-specific costs can be substantially outside that range.
- DAC commonly has estimated costs of roughly $100–$600 or more per tonne today, with large uncertainty and a possibility of decline through learning and scale.
- Cost comparisons are sensitive to whether they include transport, storage, energy-system effects, financing, capacity factor, avoided emissions, and the value of captured CO₂.

### Finding 5

**Claim**

CCS has a substantial energy penalty, which increases fuel or electricity use and can reduce the net climate benefit if low-carbon energy is not available.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Energy penalties are a direct engineering consequence and are consistently quantified in technical assessments, although exact values depend on technology and operating conditions.

**Evidence**

- For conventional power plants, solvent-based post-combustion capture can reduce net electrical efficiency by roughly 6–12 percentage points, with additional energy required for CO₂ compression and transport.
- Industrial capture energy requirements vary with CO₂ concentration and process integration; high-purity streams may require relatively little separation energy, whereas dilute flue gas requires substantially more.
- DAC is especially energy intensive because atmospheric CO₂ is dilute; it requires both heat and electricity, with the climate benefit depending strongly on the energy source.

### Finding 6

**Claim**

Geological storage appears capable of accommodating very large quantities of CO₂, but practical scalability is limited by site characterization, infrastructure, regulation, and public acceptance rather than theoretical pore volume alone.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The distinction between theoretical and practical storage capacity and the infrastructure requirements are well documented, though regional storage availability remains uncertain.

**Evidence**

- Deep saline formations and depleted oil and gas reservoirs have potentially very large storage resources; global theoretical capacity is measured in thousands of gigatonnes, but only a fraction is characterized, permitted, accessible, and economically usable.
- Long-term storage projects such as Sleipner and Snøhvit demonstrate injection and monitoring over decades, while experience also shows the need for careful reservoir management and monitoring.
- Scaling requires shared CO₂ pipelines, shipping terminals, injection wells, monitoring systems, pore-space rights, cross-border rules, and clear long-term liability arrangements.

### Finding 7

**Claim**

CCS can provide genuine negative emissions only in conjunction with durable removal pathways such as BECCS or DAC with geological storage; fossil CCS ordinarily reduces emissions rather than removes historical CO₂.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The accounting distinction between emissions reduction and carbon removal is fundamental, while the net benefit of BECCS and EOR depends on project-specific lifecycle and land-use assumptions.

**Evidence**

- CCS attached to biomass energy can produce net removals only if biomass is sustainably sourced, supply-chain emissions are low, land-use impacts are controlled, and captured CO₂ is permanently stored.
- DAC with geological storage directly removes atmospheric CO₂, but current costs, energy requirements, and deployment levels are very small compared with modeled requirements.
- Enhanced oil recovery can result in lower net climate benefits than dedicated geological storage, depending on the source of CO₂, oil displacement, and accounting of produced-fuel emissions.

### Finding 8

**Claim**

The strongest arguments for large-scale CCS are industrial necessity, preservation of some existing assets during transition, potential negative emissions, and system flexibility.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

These are consistent with sectoral engineering constraints and scenario analyses, although the extent of CCS required varies by pathway.

**Evidence**

- Some process emissions, especially limestone calcination in cement, cannot be eliminated solely by switching to renewable electricity.
- CCS can reduce emissions from existing facilities and may help manage residual emissions in hard-to-abate sectors while alternative technologies and infrastructure mature.
- A portfolio containing CCS, electrification, hydrogen, efficiency, material substitution, and removals can lower technology and supply-chain dependence compared with relying on one pathway.

### Finding 9

**Claim**

The strongest arguments against indiscriminate CCS deployment are high cost, energy use, uncertain reliability, infrastructure lock-in, and the risk of delaying more effective abatement.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The risks are documented, though their magnitude depends on policy design, technology choice, project quality, and what alternative investments would otherwise have been made.

**Evidence**

- Several early power-sector CCS projects experienced cost overruns, downtime, or closure, weakening the case for CCS as a broadly economical way to preserve unabated coal and gas generation.
- Public subsidies for CCS can divert capital and political attention from efficiency, renewables, grids, electrification, demand reduction, and methane control when CCS is applied to sectors with cheaper alternatives.
- Pipelines and storage sites create long-lived infrastructure and liability issues; leakage risks are generally considered manageable with appropriate site selection and monitoring, but permanence is not automatic.
- CCS does not remove local air pollutants associated with fossil combustion unless paired with broader pollution controls, and continued fossil extraction can produce upstream methane and environmental impacts.

### Finding 10

**Claim**

CCS can make a significant contribution, but it is unlikely to be sufficient or optimal as a dominant global mitigation strategy.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

This conclusion follows from the combination of demonstrated technical performance, current deployment levels, sectoral constraints, and the structure of credible mitigation pathways.

**Evidence**

- Net-zero scenarios generally include several gigatonnes per year of captured and stored CO₂ by mid-century, alongside rapid growth in renewables, electrification, efficiency, low-carbon fuels, and some carbon removal.
- Current capacity is orders of magnitude below the multi-gigatonne scale used in many 1.5°C and net-zero pathways, meaning deployment would need exceptionally rapid growth in capture, transport, and storage.
- The most defensible role is targeted deployment in hard-to-abate industries and carefully governed removals, with strict lifecycle accounting and no weakening of direct emissions-reduction standards.

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Published CCS cost estimates differ substantially because studies use different plant sizes, discount rates, energy prices, capture rates, financing assumptions, and system boundaries.
- The number of announced projects and pipeline capacity is much larger than operating capacity; announcements should not be treated as committed or financed deployment.
- Modeled reliance on CCS and carbon removal varies by climate pathway, assumptions about renewable energy, biomass availability, industrial demand, and policy effectiveness.
- Long-term storage permanence is considered high for well-selected geological formations, but site-specific leakage probability, monitoring quality, and liability rules remain important uncertainties.
- BECCS can have either substantial climate benefits or limited benefits and serious land-use impacts depending on feedstock, counterfactual land use, biodiversity effects, and supply-chain emissions.

## Remaining Gaps

- More reliable full-chain data on actual capture rates, availability, energy use, methane emissions, transport losses, and storage performance across operating projects.
- Better transparent cost data from commercial projects, including financing, downtime, shared infrastructure, and decommissioning or long-term monitoring costs.
- Regional assessments of characterized and permitted storage capacity, especially in countries with large industrial emissions but limited geological data.
- Clear rules for CO₂ accounting, cross-border transport, pore-space rights, long-term liability, and verification of permanent storage.
- Evidence on whether large project pipelines will reach final investment decisions and operate at design performance.
- Stronger lifecycle assessments of BECCS, DAC, and CO₂ use pathways, particularly where biomass, renewable electricity, or low-carbon heat is scarce.

## Conclusion

Yes—but primarily as a targeted and complementary technology. CCS has demonstrated that CO₂ can be separated, compressed, transported, injected, and retained underground, and it can deliver major reductions from some industrial facilities. Its most compelling applications are concentrated and process-emission-heavy sectors such as cement, lime, chemicals, and selected hydrogen or steel pathways; carefully governed BECCS or DAC may provide residual carbon removal. However, CCS is expensive and energy intensive in many applications, has a very large scale-up requirement, and has a mixed record in power generation. It cannot make fossil fuel use climate-neutral, and high capture rates do not eliminate upstream and lifecycle emissions. A sound policy approach would prioritize direct emissions elimination wherever cheaper and more reliable, require rigorous lifecycle accounting and permanent storage, prevent captured CO₂ from being used to justify unabated fossil expansion, and support shared infrastructure for genuinely hard-to-abate sectors. Under those conditions CCS could plausibly contribute several gigatonnes of annual CO₂ reduction or removal later this century; without them, it risks becoming a costly delay mechanism rather than a major climate solution.
