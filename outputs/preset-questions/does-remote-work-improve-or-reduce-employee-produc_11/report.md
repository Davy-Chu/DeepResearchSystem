# Research Report

## Research Question

Does remote work improve or reduce employee productivity? Compare evidence from controlled experiments, observational studies, and company-level data, and explain why different studies reach conflicting conclusions.

## Summary

The accumulated evidence supports neither a universal productivity gain nor a universal productivity loss. Effects depend on whether work is fully remote or hybrid, the task’s need for concentration versus coordination, the workers and firms studied, the outcome measured, and the time allowed for adaptation. Controlled experiments generally find that well-designed hybrid work preserves measured individual performance and can improve retention and well-being. A fully remote call-center experiment found higher output, but other evidence reports lower output for some collaborative or newly hired roles. Company-level Italian evidence indicates that forced, economy-wide adoption initially reduced firm productivity, with losses diminishing over time and varying by firm size, ICT investment, and worker qualifications.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Hybrid work generally preserves measured individual performance and may improve retention, satisfaction, and communication.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Two distinct field experiments provide causal evidence, although they concern specific employers, occupations, countries, and relatively limited time periods.

**Evidence**

- In a randomized Trip.com study, employees working from home two days per week showed no significant differences from office workers in reported performance, promotions, coding, revenue, or hours, while resignations fell by about one-third and satisfaction and work-life balance improved. [S1] [S7] [S8]
- A BRAC field experiment randomized office attendance among administrative workers and found that an intermediate hybrid schedule improved manager performance ratings, email communication, information conveyed, work-life balance, and isolation outcomes. [S9]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

Fully remote work can increase individual output in some settings, but its effects are more context-dependent than those of the studied hybrid arrangements.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The positive call-center result is repeatedly reported, but the retrieved material does not provide the primary experiment in full; the negative fully remote estimates are also available mainly through summaries.

**Evidence**

- The reported Ctrip randomized experiment found that mostly home-based call-center workers completed about 13.5% more calls than office-based workers, with gains attributed to more minutes worked and greater efficiency per minute. [S1]
- The accumulated evidence also reports lower output for some fully remote roles, particularly work requiring substantial collaboration or involving newly hired workers. [S1] [S9]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

Observational and survey evidence often reports stable or improving perceived productivity, but it does not by itself establish that remote work caused those outcomes.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The HBS source provides relevant longitudinal survey evidence, but perceptions are subject to reporting and selection issues; the BLS result is presented only as an association in the available material.

**Evidence**

- A Harvard Business School working paper reports that 70% of surveyed small-business owners initially perceived a productivity decline during the early pandemic transition, while the median owner reported a positive effect by early 2021. [S4]
- The same study attributes later improvement partly to technology investment, training, task redesign, and better remote-management practices, while noting continuing monitoring and skill-learning difficulties. [S4]
- A reported BLS analysis found a positive association between remote-work adoption and total-factor-productivity growth, but the retrieved material does not provide sufficient identification details to establish causality. [S2]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

Company-level evidence indicates short-run productivity losses during forced pandemic adoption, followed by recovery or heterogeneous effects as firms adapt.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The result is consistently stated across records of the study and uses a potentially informative firm-level design, but the retrieved excerpts omit effect sizes, confidence intervals, productivity definitions, first-stage strength, and full robustness checks.

**Evidence**

- The Italian firm-level study, using administrative data covering remote workers and a fibre-broadband instrumental-variable strategy, reports a large negative productivity effect during the pandemic but no statistically significant longer-term average effect. [S12] [S15] [S16] [S17]
- The study reports that larger firms and firms with prior ICT investment mitigated losses, while firms employing highly qualified workers showed suggestive longer-term productivity gains. [S12] [S15] [S16] [S17]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

The apparent disagreement among studies is mainly explained by differences in treatment, tasks, populations, outcomes, and adaptation stage.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The sources directly document variation in study design, setting, measurement, and timing, offering a coherent explanation for the conflicting results.

**Evidence**

- The sources distinguish focused individual work from collaboration, onboarding, mentoring, knowledge sharing, and creative problem-solving, which need not respond similarly to remote work. [S1] [S3] [S9]
- The experiments measure calls, performance ratings, coding, promotions, communication, retention, satisfaction, and isolation, whereas the Italian study measures firm-level productivity; these outcomes can move in different directions. [S1] [S7] [S9] [S12]
- Pandemic-era forced transitions occurred while firms were still developing technology, training, task design, and remote-management practices; later arrangements benefited from adaptation and organizational complements. [S4] [S12] [S15] [S16] [S17]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

Remote work can produce organizational benefits even when measured individual output is unchanged, while individual productivity measures may omit longer-run coordination and learning effects.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

Retention and well-being effects are directly reported, but the long-run effect on broader organizational productivity remains unresolved.

**Evidence**

- The Trip.com experiment found equivalent measured performance alongside substantially lower attrition and improved satisfaction and work-life balance. [S7] [S8]
- The BRAC experiment measured communication and isolation, while the retrieved evidence does not establish long-run effects on innovation, mentoring, onboarding, or knowledge accumulation. [S9]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- The fully remote Ctrip call-center experiment reported higher call output, while other reported evidence finds 10–20% lower output for some fully remote, collaborative, or newly hired roles. The studies may differ in occupation, worker selection, and task interdependence rather than directly contradicting one another. [S1]
- The Italian firm-level study reports pandemic-era productivity losses, contrasting with positive or neutral results from selected-worker experiments. The difference may reflect economy-wide forced adoption, firm-level adjustment costs, and different units of analysis. [S1] [S7] [S9] [S12] [S15] [S16]
- Business-owner perceptions shifted from predominantly negative early in the pandemic to positive after roughly a year. This suggests adaptation over time, but survey perceptions cannot be treated as equivalent to measured productivity. [S4]
- The BRAC experiment found improved communication and manager ratings under intermediate hybrid work, whereas Trip.com found no significant changes in several performance measures. The schedules, settings, samples, and outcome definitions differ. [S7] [S9]
- S6 reports inconsistent Ctrip sample sizes and effect estimates and presents a broad positive conclusion despite acknowledging negative and null findings. Its figures should not be used without verification against primary studies. [S6] [S7] [S9]
- The Italian study’s fibre-broadband instrument may help address selection into remote work, but the retrieved material does not show whether it fully separates WFH adoption from broader digital capability and broadband-related productivity effects. [S12] [S15] [S16] [S17]

## Remaining Gaps

- Exact effect sizes, confidence intervals, treatment compliance, and complete outcome tables are unavailable for several controlled experiments.
- The Italian firm-level study’s precise productivity measure, estimates, time horizons, and identification diagnostics remain unavailable.
- Direct evidence is limited on company-level revenue, value added, total-factor productivity, innovation, onboarding, mentoring, and long-run knowledge accumulation.
- It remains unclear how experimental individual-output effects translate into aggregate firm outcomes.
- Generalizability across countries, occupations, worker seniority, home conditions, management practices, and voluntary versus imposed remote work is uncertain.
- The post-pandemic firm-level records S23–S27 may represent versions of the same Boeri et al. research; their empirical direction and magnitude are not reported.

## Conclusion

Remote work neither simply improves nor reduces productivity. The strongest supported interpretation is conditional: hybrid work, particularly arrangements combining focused remote days with planned in-person coordination, can maintain individual performance while improving retention and worker well-being. Fully remote work can raise output for some independent, measurable tasks, but may reduce performance or coordination in collaborative, onboarding-intensive, or newly formed teams. Company-level evidence adds an important qualification: forced pandemic adoption initially imposed productivity costs, and outcomes improved as firms developed suitable ICT, management practices, task designs, and skills. Therefore, conflicting studies are usually measuring different versions of “remote work” and “productivity,” at different organizational levels and stages of adaptation. The available evidence supports context-specific work-design decisions, not a universal remote-work or return-to-office rule.
