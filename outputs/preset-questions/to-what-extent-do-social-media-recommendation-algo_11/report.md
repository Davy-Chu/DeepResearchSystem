# Research Report

## Research Question

To what extent do social-media recommendation algorithms contribute to political polarization? Separate evidence that algorithms change what users see from evidence that they actually change political beliefs or behavior, and explain why the literature reaches different conclusions.

## Summary

The accumulated evidence supports a conditional, not universal, role for recommendation algorithms. Randomized studies clearly show that ranking systems alter the political content users encounter and the visibility of political actors. Evidence that this exposure changes political attitudes is mixed: a recent randomized X experiment found shifts in selected conservative policy opinions and political following behavior, while randomized Facebook/Instagram and other experiments found substantial exposure changes but little or no change in polarization-related attitudes. Evidence concerning consequential political behavior such as voting, turnout, donations, or offline activism remains sparse and is not established for ordinary feed recommendation. The literature reaches different conclusions because studies examine different platforms, algorithms, content environments, durations, user populations, and outcomes—and often fail to distinguish algorithmic ranking from users’ prior preferences, network composition, and exposure to like-minded content.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

Recommendation algorithms clearly change what users see and whose political content receives visibility.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

These findings come from randomized platform experiments with direct measures of feed content, visibility, or exposure.

**Evidence**

- A randomized experiment involving nearly 2 million Twitter/X accounts found that algorithmic personalization amplified mainstream right-wing political content more than mainstream left-wing content in six of seven countries, and favored right-leaning U.S. news sources. [S6] [S8] [S9] [S10]
- In a randomized Facebook and Instagram election experiment, replacing personalized ranking with chronological feeds changed exposure to political and untrustworthy content, uncivil or slur-containing content, and content from moderate or ideologically mixed sources. [S11]
- A randomized X field experiment found that the algorithm promoted conservative content and demoted posts from traditional media outlets. [S16] [S19]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

Algorithms can change some political beliefs or issue attitudes, but the effect is selective and context-dependent rather than a general increase in polarization.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The evidence includes randomized experiments, but the results differ across platforms, interventions, and outcomes. The strongest supported conclusion is that effects occur under some conditions and for some attitudes, not that algorithms uniformly polarize users.

**Evidence**

- A randomized seven-week X experiment found that assigning users to the algorithmic feed shifted opinions toward more conservative policy priorities and altered views on several political issues. [S16] [S19]
- The same study found no significant change in affective polarization or self-reported partisanship, indicating that issue-attitude change did not necessarily become broader partisan hostility or identity change. [S16]
- A separate X reranking experiment reported that reducing exposure to partisan animosity and antidemocratic content increased warmth toward the opposing party by 2.11 points on a 100-point scale, while increasing such exposure reduced warmth by 2.48 points after one week. [S1]
- Other experiments found that like-minded content increased attitude and affective polarization, but algorithmic selection generally did not amplify those effects; only a small direct effect appeared for one topic. [S4]
- The Facebook/Instagram experiment found no significant change in issue polarization, affective polarization, political knowledge, or other key attitudes over three months despite substantial changes in exposure and platform activity. [S11]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

Algorithms change on-platform behavior, but evidence that ordinary recommendation feeds change consequential political behavior is limited.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Engagement, time spent, and following were directly measured. The lack of demonstrated downstream political effects is a limitation of the retrieved evidence, not proof that such effects never occur.

**Evidence**

- Moving users from algorithmic to chronological Facebook and Instagram feeds substantially reduced time spent on the platforms and user activity. [S11]
- The X algorithmic-feed assignment increased engagement and led users to follow more conservative political activist accounts. [S16] [S19] [S20]
- Users who later returned from algorithmic to chronological feeds did not show comparable reversals in views or following behavior, suggesting persistence in some on-platform effects. [S16] [S19] [S20]
- The retrieved feed studies do not establish effects on voting, turnout, political donations, offline activism, or offline hostility. [S11] [S16] [S6] [S8] [S9] [S10]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

Evidence of effects on voting behavior exists for targeted political advertising, but it should not be conflated with recommendation-algorithm effects.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The outcome is a concrete behavioral measure, but the retrieved source is a university news report rather than the primary study and the intervention is mechanistically distinct from feed recommendation.

**Evidence**

- A report on a study of more than 10,000 U.S. participants states that exposure to Facebook vote-suppressing advertisements was associated with a 1.9-percentage-point reduction in actual turnout, using voter records. [S14]
- The intervention involved microtargeted vote-suppression advertisements rather than randomized ranking of ordinary social-media feeds, so it addresses targeted advertising and delivery, not recommendation ranking itself. [S14]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

Like-minded exposure and preexisting social dynamics may be more important than algorithmic selection alone in producing polarization.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Multiple sources explicitly distinguish algorithmic ranking from content exposure, self-selection, networks, and other social-media mechanisms.

**Evidence**

- Experiments that separately manipulated content alignment and selection method found stronger polarization after exposure to like-minded arguments than after exposure to opposing arguments, while algorithmic selection generally added little. [S4]
- A review describes algorithms as operating in feedback loops with existing social drivers, including users’ preferences and broader social conditions, rather than independently generating polarization. [S15]
- The Facebook/Instagram study notes that social-media effects can reflect network composition, user choice, resharing, and other platform features in addition to ranking algorithms. [S11]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

The apparent disagreement in the literature reflects different estimands and experimental environments rather than one settled universal effect.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The sources directly document differences in platforms, interventions, content, duration, populations, and outcome measures, providing a strong explanation for divergent conclusions.

**Evidence**

- The X studies manipulated or observed feeds that promoted particular conservative, partisan, or antidemocratic content, whereas the Facebook/Instagram study replaced personalized ranking with chronology without targeting a specific political-content category. [S1] [S11] [S16]
- The studies measure different outcomes: visibility, exposure, engagement, following, issue opinions, affective polarization, partisanship, political knowledge, or real-world behavior. [S4] [S11] [S16]
- The X experiment found an asymmetry: turning the algorithm on produced opinion and following effects, while switching it off produced no comparable reversal, plausibly because users had already followed accounts promoted during initial exposure. [S16] [S19] [S20]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- The X field experiment reports shifts toward more conservative issue opinions, while the Facebook/Instagram experiment found no detectable changes in issue or affective polarization after three months. This is a cross-platform and cross-intervention conflict, not necessarily a direct replication failure. [S16] [S11]
- The X reranking study reports a direct affective-attitude effect, whereas another experiment found that like-minded exposure mattered more than whether selection was algorithmic. The findings may differ because the studies manipulated different content and used different online environments. [S1] [S4]
- The X study found changes in selected issue opinions but no significant change in affective polarization or self-reported partisanship. Thus evidence for belief or preference change is stronger than evidence for generalized partisan polarization. [S16]
- An earlier secondary account gave the X reranking study a sample of 1,256 participants, while the later primary-source materials report 4,965 users. The primary-source figures should be preferred, and the relationship between the samples is unresolved in the accumulated material. [S1] [S16] [S19] [S20]
- The retrieved evidence does not determine whether short-term or selected attitude changes accumulate into durable ideological polarization or electoral effects. [S4] [S11] [S16]

## Remaining Gaps

- Whether recommendation-induced changes affect voting, turnout, donations, political participation, sharing, or offline hostility.
- Why X produces detectable issue-attitude effects while Facebook/Instagram studies produce mostly null polarization effects; possible explanations include platform design, content mix, baseline feeds, user populations, timing, and outcome measurement.
- Whether the X-induced opinion and following changes persist over substantially longer periods.
- Whether effects are concentrated among highly engaged, initially conservative, or already polarized users.
- How generalizable the findings are beyond active U.S. X users, the specific 2023 political context, and the particular recommendation systems studied.
- Whether observed conservative shifts reflect persuasion, changes in issue salience, selective following, or a combination of mechanisms.

## Conclusion

Recommendation algorithms contribute to political polarization to a meaningful but conditional extent. The evidence is strongest for changing political visibility and exposure: algorithms can systematically favor some political content and alter the composition of users’ feeds. The evidence that they change beliefs is real but mixed and outcome-specific. On X, randomized feed assignment changed selected policy opinions and political following behavior, while leaving affective polarization and self-reported partisanship unchanged. On Facebook and Instagram, large changes in exposure and engagement did not produce detectable changes in polarization-related attitudes over three months; other experiments suggest that like-minded content itself may matter more than algorithmic selection. Therefore, algorithms should not be treated as the sole or universal cause of polarization. They are better understood as mechanisms that can amplify existing preferences and social dynamics, and that can produce attitude or on-platform behavioral changes when their ranking exposes users to particularly directional or antagonistic content. The accumulated evidence does not yet establish that ordinary recommendation algorithms consistently alter consequential political behavior such as voting or turnout.
