## Comprehensiveness

### R1

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: The report correctly addresses the central distinction between nameplate capacity and demonstrated performance and presents useful aggregate and conflicting evidence. It falls short of full treatment because it does not substantively quantify sustained actual capture and storage, availability, and net emissions reductions across representative projects.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The report states that operational capture and storage capacity was “just over 50 MtCO₂ per year” in early 2025, equal to roughly “0.1–0.16% of global emissions.”
  - It distinguishes capacity from actual mitigation: “reported capture capacity does not consistently establish net, permanent climate mitigation,” and notes that inventories do not consistently document CO₂ “injected, retained, monitored, and permanently stored.”
  - It reports conflicting performance evidence: CATF identifies Sleipner, Alberta Carbon Trunk Line, and Quest as having consistently high technical performance, while IEEFA found that none of 16 reviewed projects consistently captured more than 80%.
  - It explicitly notes the lack of common definitions for capture rate, project sample, operating period, availability, and measurement methods.
- Missing:
  - The report does not provide project-level measured quantities of CO₂ actually captured and stored over specified operating periods for representative facilities.
  - It gives little quantified evidence on operating availability, downtime, planned versus actual throughput, or net lifecycle emissions reductions beyond the single maritime pilot.
  - The performance comparison is largely source-reported and does not resolve the conflicting capture-rate evidence.

### R2

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.50
- Rationale: The report covers the relevant integrity concepts and correctly warns against equating utilization or capture with permanent mitigation. However, it mainly identifies issues rather than evaluating demonstrated storage safety and permanence with measured evidence.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The report distinguishes capture, utilization, geological storage, and atmospheric removal, stating that gross capture capacity is not equivalent to “permanent geological storage or atmospheric carbon removal.”
  - It identifies leakage, permanence, embodied carbon, chain of custody, and monitoring, reporting, and verification as unresolved accounting issues.
  - It notes that enhanced oil recovery and other commercial utilization pathways do not automatically provide equivalent permanent climate mitigation.
  - It identifies the need for transport, storage, monitoring, regulation, and long-term liability arrangements.
- Missing:
  - There is little concrete geological-storage evidence, such as measured injection volumes, demonstrated retention, plume behavior, leakage rates, or monitoring results from specific storage projects.
  - Transport and injection safety are mentioned but not assessed in technical or empirical detail.
  - The report does not adequately discuss long-term institutional responsibility, remediation obligations, or regulatory treatment of leakage.
  - The climate-accounting implications of enhanced oil recovery are flagged but not critically quantified or explained.

#### F2: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED

- Claim: CCS is technically feasible, but successful projects do not establish representative sector-wide performance or net climate benefit.
- Sources: S11, S15, S13
- Rationale: S11 and S15 support the technical-feasibility portion: they describe CCS’s component technologies as available for decades and identify several large-scale projects that consistently achieved high technical performance. They also support a narrower limitation on generalization: the examined projects are a selected set, commonly cited large-scale projects represent only a fraction of commercial technologies, and many operating projects were not developed or required to maximize climate benefit. However, the sources do not directly establish the stronger claim that successful projects fail to provide representative sector-wide performance, nor do they establish a general conclusion about net climate benefit. S13 supports skepticism about efficacy and climate contribution, but its stronger claims conflict with the feasibility framing and are not independently substantiated in the saved text.
- Supporting text: S11/S15: “Several large-scale projects ... have consistently met high levels of technical performance,” while “commonly cited ‘large-scale’ CCS projects represent a fraction” of commercial technologies; most operating projects “were not developed for climate purposes” or required to maximize climate benefit. S13: “not one single CCS project has ever reached its target CO2 capture rate” and no reviewed project “has consistently captured more than 80% of carbon.”

#### F3: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Gross capture capacity should not be treated as equivalent to permanent geological storage or atmospheric carbon removal.
- Sources: S6, S7, S11, S15, S4, S22, S23
- Rationale: The cited sources distinguish capture from transport and permanent geological storage, and distinguish point-source carbon capture from carbon dioxide removal, which intentionally removes CO2 from the atmosphere and durably stores it. They also note that many operating projects were not designed or required to maximize stored CO2 or climate benefit, and that capture-facility locations may not correspond to storage sites. Together, this directly supports the claim that gross capture capacity is not equivalent to permanent storage or atmospheric removal.
- Supporting text: S11/S15 define CCS as separating CO2, transporting it, and injecting it underground for permanent storage, while noting that many operating projects are not required to maximize the CO2 they store or their climate benefit. S4 contrasts CDR—removing CO2 from the atmosphere and durably storing it—with carbon capture, which extracts CO2 before it enters the atmosphere. S6 cautions that capture-facility locations are not always synonymous with storage sites.

#### F4: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED

- Claim: CCS is costly and energy-intensive, with costs varying substantially by source, technology, transport distance, and storage site.
- Sources: S1, S12, S15, S23, S11, S29, S21

S29 directly supports variation by capture technology, source/fuel, transport options, distance-related infrastructure, and storage site; S15/S11 support higher costs for more difficult sources, distant storage, and saline aquifers; S1 gives energy-demand and cost ranges. S23 supports high capital and operational costs but does not materially add support for the full cost-variation formulation.
- Supporting text: S1: CCS is evaluated using “energy demand (2.5–8 GJ/tCO2)” and “cost ($50–600/tCO2).” S15/S11: capture costs are often much higher for cement, steel, and petrochemicals; transport becomes more costly when sources are far from storage; saline-aquifer storage adds cost. S29: costs depend on capture technology, fuel/source, transport configuration, and storage-site location/type, with storage costs varying “up to a factor of 10.”

S20 explicitly reports different savings when logistics and operational inefficiencies are included or corrected, and compares utilization with permanent storage. - Supporting text: S20: The study says OCCS benefits must be evaluated across the entire carbon value chain so upstream and downstream burdens are not missed; reported savings rise from 7.9% to 17.8% when inefficiencies are addressed, and at comparable capture rates mineralization yields 34% savings versus 21% for permanent storage. S23 identifies system boundaries, leakage, and CO2 chain of custody as key accounting challenges.
