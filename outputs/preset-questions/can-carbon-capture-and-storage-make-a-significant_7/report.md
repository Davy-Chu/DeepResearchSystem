# Research Report

## Research Question

Can carbon capture and storage make a significant contribution to reducing global CO₂ emissions? Evaluate its demonstrated effectiveness, costs, scalability, energy requirements, and major arguments for and against large-scale deployment.

## Summary

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) has demonstrated that parts of the capture, transport, and geological-storage chain can operate at large scale, and some projects have achieved high technical performance. However, its present global contribution remains very small relative to total emissions, and reported capture capacity does not consistently establish net, permanent climate mitigation. CCS is expensive, energy-intensive, infrastructure-dependent, and reliant on policy support. The evidence supports a significant potential future role in selected hard-to-abate industrial processes and genuine carbon-dioxide-removal pathways, but not universal deployment or substitution for emissions avoidance, efficiency, electrification, and renewable energy.

## Findings

### Finding 1

**Claim**

CCS currently makes only a small demonstrated contribution to global CO₂ reduction.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Multiple sources agree that current deployment is very small, although reported totals use different dates, inclusion criteria, and definitions.

**Evidence**

- The IEA reported just over 50 MtCO₂ per year of operational capture and storage capacity in the first quarter of 2025, while other inventories reported approximately 51–64 MtCO₂ per year for 2024–2025. These figures correspond to roughly 0.1–0.16% of global emissions, depending on definitions and denominators. [S24] [S6] [S7]
- A critical review states that, despite more than $40 billion in global investment, CCS captures and stores less than 0.1% of annual global CO₂ emissions. [S1]

### Finding 2

**Claim**

CCS is technically feasible, but successful projects do not establish representative sector-wide performance or net climate benefit.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The evidence demonstrates both technically strong examples and weaker aggregate assessments, but the sources do not use a common capture-rate definition, project sample, operating period, or independent audit standard.

**Evidence**

- The CATF project review identifies Sleipner, the Alberta Carbon Trunk Line, and Quest as projects that consistently achieved high technical performance. [S11] [S15]
- The same review states that many projects were developed for commercial CO₂ sales, enhanced oil recovery, or technology demonstration rather than maximizing climate benefit. [S11] [S15]
- IEEFA reports that none of 16 projects in its review consistently captured more than 80% of CO₂, despite industry claims that 95% capture is achievable. [S13]

### Finding 3

**Claim**

Gross capture capacity should not be treated as equivalent to permanent geological storage or atmospheric carbon removal.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The distinction between capture, utilization, storage, and removal is explicit across several sources and is central to interpreting capacity claims.

**Evidence**

- Operating capacity inventories report capture or storage capacity but do not consistently document CO₂ injected, retained, monitored, and permanently stored. [S6] [S7]
- Many operating projects use captured CO₂ commercially, including for enhanced oil recovery, and were not required to maximize permanent climate mitigation. [S11] [S15]
- Carbon dioxide removal is distinct from conventional point-source CCS: removal extracts CO₂ from the atmosphere and stores it durably, whereas conventional CCS prevents some source emissions from entering the atmosphere. [S4]
- Accounting guidance identifies unresolved issues involving system boundaries, leakage, permanence, embodied carbon, chain of custody, and monitoring, reporting, and verification. [S22] [S23]

### Finding 4

**Claim**

CCS is costly and energy-intensive, with costs varying substantially by source, technology, transport distance, and storage site.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The sources consistently identify high, application-dependent costs and energy penalties, but cost boundaries and comparisons with alternatives are not standardized.

**Evidence**

- Reported energy requirements range from approximately 2.5–8 GJ per tonne of CO₂, while reported cost ranges span roughly $50–600 per tonne or €50–300 per tonne; many projects are estimated at approximately $100–250 per tonne. [S1] [S12]
- Costs are generally lower for high-purity sources such as ethanol, ammonia, and natural-gas processing, and higher for more dilute or technically difficult sources such as cement and steel. [S12] [S15] [S23]
- Long-distance transport, saline-aquifer storage, and site exploration add cost compared with commercially motivated CO₂ use or favorable storage locations. [S11] [S15] [S29]
- The available evidence does not provide a standardized global estimate of the additional electricity or fuel required at billion-tonne deployment levels. [S1] [S21] [S23]

### Finding 5

**Claim**

Large-scale deployment is possible in principle but would require expansion by orders of magnitude, coordinated infrastructure, and successful delivery of projects now in the pipeline.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The large gap between current operations and modeled requirements is consistently documented. Whether the announced pipeline can be completed and perform as intended remains uncertain.

**Evidence**

- The IEA reports just over 50 MtCO₂ per year in operation, with a current pipeline that could reach around 430 MtCO₂ per year of capture capacity and 670 MtCO₂ per year of storage capacity by 2030. [S24]
- The IEA also states that current trends remain insufficient for a net-zero pathway and that recent projects entering operation were relatively small, producing only a marginal increase in operating capacity. [S24]
- Climate pathways cited by CATF require several billion tonnes of CO₂ stored annually by mid-century, many times current operations. [S11] [S15]
- The EU CCUS working group estimates a possible need for 300–640 MtCO₂ per year of EU capture, utilization, or storage by 2050, including at least 150 MtCO₂ per year of atmospheric or biogenic removals. [S5]
- Large-scale systems require coordinated capture, transport, storage, monitoring, and liability arrangements; clustered networks can provide economies of scale. [S28] [S29]

### Finding 6

**Claim**

Present deployment is concentrated in relatively favorable applications and is not yet representative of the harder sectors often used to justify CCS.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

The IEA provides the operational sector distribution, and multiple sources distinguish favorable high-purity applications from more difficult industrial sources.

**Evidence**

- More than 60% of operational capture capacity is at natural-gas-processing facilities, while projects in industry, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide removal comprise a larger share of the prospective pipeline. [S24]
- Cement, steel, and chemicals are identified as harder applications because process emissions are inherent or alternatives are limited; capture in these sectors remains costly or nascent. [S15] [S21] [S23]

### Finding 7

**Claim**

The strongest case for CCS is selective deployment in hard-to-abate industry and genuine removal pathways, not universal use across power and energy.

**Confidence:** High

**Why this confidence level**

Supportive and critical sources converge most strongly on a conditional, sector-specific role even though they disagree about its eventual breadth and urgency.

**Evidence**

- Supportive assessments identify cement, steel, chemicals, existing industrial assets, selected hydrogen production, and atmospheric or biogenic removals as potential applications where alternatives may be limited. [S2] [S5] [S8] [S11] [S15] [S21] [S23]
- Critical and technical reviews emphasize that energy efficiency, emissions avoidance, fuel switching, electrification, and renewables should be pursued first where they are available. [S1] [S21] [S23]
- The IEA and EU evidence describe renewables as capable of decarbonizing most power-sector supply, with CCS serving more specific industrial, legacy-asset, dispatchable-generation, or removal roles. [S5] [S8]

### Finding 8

**Claim**

Full-chain accounting can materially change the apparent climate benefit of capture and utilization.

**Confidence:** Medium

**Why this confidence level**

The pilot provides full-chain evidence, but it is a single maritime project and cannot be generalized to stationary CCS or all utilization pathways.

**Evidence**

- A DNV-verified maritime pilot operating at a 10.7% capture rate achieved 7.9% greenhouse-gas savings across capture, liquefaction, transport, handling, and utilization; operational inefficiencies reduced the result. [S20]
- In modeled scenarios for that specific pilot, durable mineralization produced greater savings than offshore storage when it displaced carbon-intensive products, but the result depended on the pathway, transport, capture rate, and durability assumptions. [S20]
- The CCS+ and Clean Energy Ministerial sources stress that reductions, removals, short-term utilization, durable storage, leakage, and embodied emissions must be accounted for separately. [S22] [S23]

## Conflicts and Uncertainty

- Current capacity estimates differ: the IEA reports just over 50 MtCO₂ per year in operation, while other 2025 compilations report approximately 51–64 MtCO₂ per year or larger aggregate capture-capacity figures. The differences likely reflect dates, project thresholds, and distinctions between capture, storage, and operational capacity. [S6] [S7] [S24] [S25]
- CATF identifies several projects with consistently high technical performance, whereas IEEFA reports that no project in its sample consistently exceeded 80% capture. The sources do not reconcile capture-rate definitions, project samples, availability periods, or measurement methods. [S13] [S15]
- Supportive sources describe CCS as important, critical, or potentially indispensable for net zero, while critical sources emphasize its small current contribution, high costs, underperformance, and risk of delaying alternatives. The sources agree that CCS may have specific hard-to-abate applications but disagree about aggregate feasibility and strategic breadth. [S1] [S2] [S5] [S13] [S16] [S18] [S19] [S21]
- Older cost assessments projected that CCS could become cost-competitive after demonstration and learning, whereas newer evidence reports high current costs and continuing dependence on subsidies, carbon prices, or tax incentives. The difference reflects prospective assumptions versus later market and project experience. [S1] [S12] [S21] [S29]
- Utilization can outperform storage in particular durable-mineralization applications, but enhanced-oil-recovery and other utilization pathways do not automatically provide equivalent permanent climate mitigation. [S11] [S15] [S20]
- Most available sources are institutional, advocacy-oriented, or industry-linked. They provide useful evidence about project development, technical possibilities, and arguments for deployment, but less independent standardized evidence on global net storage, lifecycle emissions, energy penalties, and representative project performance. [S11] [S15] [S16] [S18] [S19] [S28] [S29] [S30] [S31]

## Remaining Gaps

- An independently verified global inventory separating captured, transported, utilized, injected, retained, and permanently stored CO₂.
- Standardized project-level measurements of capture rates, availability, energy penalties, lifecycle emissions, leakage risk, and net climate benefit.
- Apples-to-apples comparisons between full-chain CCS and electrification, efficiency, renewable energy, alternative fuels, and process substitution in each target sector.
- A robust estimate of additional low-carbon electricity or fuel requirements at projected 2030 and billion-tonne-per-year deployment levels.
- A current assessment of geological storage that is technically, legally, politically, and environmentally usable, together with the infrastructure and long-term liability needed to access it.
- A clear separation in future scenarios between conventional point-source emissions avoidance and genuine atmospheric carbon-dioxide removal.

## Conclusion

Yes, CCS could make a significant future contribution, but the accumulated evidence supports a qualified rather than sweeping answer. Its demonstrated global contribution is currently very small, and its reported capacity is not consistently equivalent to verified permanent storage or net atmospheric benefit. Costs and energy requirements are substantial and application-dependent, while transport, storage, monitoring, regulation, finance, and public acceptance create major scalability challenges. The most defensible role is targeted deployment for unavoidable industrial process emissions—especially in cement, steel, and chemicals—and for carefully verified removal pathways such as bioenergy or direct-air capture with durable storage. CCS should therefore be treated as one conditional component of a broader mitigation portfolio, not as a universal substitute for rapid emissions avoidance, efficiency, electrification, and renewable deployment.
