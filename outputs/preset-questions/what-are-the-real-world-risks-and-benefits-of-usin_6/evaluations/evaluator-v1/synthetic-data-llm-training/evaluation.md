## Comprehensiveness

### R1

- Coverage: 0.50
- Depth: 0.50
- Rationale: The report recognizes that synthetic-data use is not a single regime and meaningfully distinguishes supplementation from recursive replacement. However, it does not fully define the requested generator types and training settings or explain their implications comparatively.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The summary distinguishes “curated supplementation or targeted augmentation” from “recursive or large-scale replacement with model-generated data.”
  - Finding 2 limits distribution-collapse claims to “recursive or substantial replacement use” and contrasts them with curated supplementation.
  - The report discusses both synthetic-data fine-tuning and synthetic data used in Phi-1 training.
- Missing:
  - It does not clearly distinguish synthetic data generated by LLMs from programmatically generated synthetic data.
  - Pretraining, continued pretraining, instruction fine-tuning, and domain adaptation are not systematically separated; the distinction appears mainly in the remaining-gaps section.
  - It does not explain in enough detail why the risks and benefits differ across these training roles or between pure-synthetic and mixed synthetic-real regimes.

### R2

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: The report covers most important benefit mechanisms—scale, coverage, rare cases, class imbalance, domain adaptation, and low-resource data—and appropriately qualifies the evidence. More direct downstream quality comparisons would be needed for full coverage.
- Candidate evidence:
  - Finding 1 says synthetic data can “expand scarce training resources,” generate domain- or task-specific examples, cover rare or edge cases, address class imbalance, and support low-resource and cross-lingual tasks.
  - The evidence cites curated synthetic textbooks and exercises in Phi-1, low-resource and cross-lingual augmentation, and targeted task-specific generation.
  - The report identifies controlled experimentation, debugging, repeatable evaluation, and golden datasets as additional practical benefits.
- Missing:
  - The report gives limited direct evidence about increased overall scale or diversity improving downstream model quality rather than enabling experimentation or augmentation.
  - It does not substantially distinguish benefits from mere availability or cost reduction from demonstrated improvements in accuracy, generalization, or robustness.
  - Benefits of programmatic generation and explicit label availability are not developed in detail.

### R3

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: The report gives a strong account of collapse, repetition, artifacts, mismatch, and quality-control conditions, with downstream consequences. It falls short of a fully comprehensive treatment of factual/semantic errors, mislabeled examples, and training contamination.
- Candidate evidence:
  - Finding 3 identifies errors, repetition, linguistic defects, artifacts, insufficient validation, distribution misalignment, and inadequate source diversity.
  - Finding 2 reports loss of distribution tails, reduced diversity, distribution collapse, and degraded outputs under recursive or substantial replacement.
  - The report connects these failures to degraded performance, linguistic diversity, robustness, and outputs, and notes mitigation through filtering, deduplication, validation, and retaining real data.
  - Finding 7 identifies contamination and distribution shift as evaluation risks.
- Missing:
  - Incorrect labels and semantic errors are not discussed explicitly or connected to downstream task performance.
  - Contamination and leakage are treated mostly as evaluation concerns rather than as data-quality failure modes affecting training.
  - Conditions affecting factuality, hallucination, and generalization are less developed than the discussion of recursive collapse and repetition.

### R4

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: It correctly distinguishes balancing the data from proving fairness and identifies targeted augmentation and filtering as conditional interventions. Full treatment would require more detail on fairness-aware and counterfactual methods and the evidence needed for each.
- Candidate evidence:
  - Finding 4 explains that synthetic data can generate examples for underrepresented classes, address class imbalance, and support multilingual or low-resource augmentation.
  - The report notes augmentation, resampling, filtering, and deliberate targeting as possible bias interventions.
  - It explicitly warns that improved representation or aggregate task performance does not establish broader demographic, linguistic, cultural, or intersectional fairness.
  - The conclusion calls for subgroup and intersectional testing and comparisons against real-data baselines.
- Missing:
  - Counterfactual generation, fairness-aware generation, and weighting are not substantially explained as distinct mechanisms.
  - The report does not specify concrete fairness outcomes or experimental designs beyond subgroup/intersectional testing and baseline comparisons.
  - The evidence for targeted interventions is mostly descriptive rather than a controlled demonstration that model behavior improved.

### R5

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: The report covers persistence, generator bias, underrepresentation, amplification, self-preference, and iterative narrowing. It lacks a fuller account of stereotype and spurious-correlation mechanisms and a more explicit dataset-versus-model measurement distinction.
- Candidate evidence:
  - Finding 5 states that foundation-model biases can persist through fine-tuning and that biases in fine-tuning material may be added or intensified.
  - The report attributes bias to the generating model and synthetic material, and discusses language bias, self-preference bias, and loss of distribution tails.
  - It warns that iterative synthetic training can amplify bias and undermine rare-group representation.
  - The report distinguishes targeted category representation from broad demographic, linguistic, cultural, and intersectional fairness.
- Missing:
  - Stereotypical associations and spurious correlations are not explicitly analyzed.
  - Subgroup or class imbalance is discussed mainly as a mitigation target, not as a detailed generation failure mode.
  - The distinction between bias measured in the synthetic dataset and bias observed in the trained model is stated implicitly but not developed with separate measures or examples.

### R6

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: The report proposes a broad and appropriate evaluation set and recognizes contamination and synthetic-test limitations. It needs a more explicit complementary measurement framework and sharper discussion of individual metric limitations.
- Candidate evidence:
  - Finding 7 recommends evaluation of generalization, factuality, robustness, safety, fairness, contamination, distribution shift, and realistic task performance.
  - The report says synthetic evaluation sets should be checked for edge-case coverage, ambiguity, multi-turn context, calibration, and robustness, and that golden datasets should be validated for objectives and breadth.
  - It warns that benchmark saturation, contamination, synthetic evaluation bias, and generator-aligned tests can make results misleading.
  - The conclusion calls for factuality checks, human-validated evaluation data, shifted or realistic task tests, and real-data baselines.
- Missing:
  - Fidelity to the intended distribution, semantic correctness, label correctness, and diversity/coverage are not organized into a clear multi-dimensional data-fitness evaluation procedure.
  - Limitations of statistical metrics, human judgments, model-based metrics, and perplexity-style metrics are not individually explained.
  - The report does not clearly state that synthetic-only or generator-aligned metrics can reward similarity to the generator while missing real-world utility, beyond general warnings about synthetic evaluation bias.

### R7

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: The report strongly supports baseline comparisons and subgroup/intersectional outcome evaluation, and rejects balance as sufficient evidence. It would be more complete with explicit held-out-real-data and metric-level recommendations.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The conclusion recommends “role-specific comparisons against real-data baselines” and “subgroup and intersectional testing.”
  - Finding 7 cites intersectional gender and racial disparities in resume screening and says aggregate capability scores are insufficient.
  - The report says bias evaluation should examine data, models, outputs, demographic attributes, and application contexts.
  - It states that targeted representation and aggregate performance do not establish broad fairness.
- Missing:
  - Held-out, preferably real, subgroup-sensitive evaluation data is not stated as a clear requirement in the evaluation recommendation.
  - Specific metrics such as subgroup performance, error rates, calibration, representation rates, or disparity changes are not specified in a concrete protocol.
  - The report does not explicitly recommend a no-synthetic baseline alongside real-data-only and mixed-data comparisons.

### R8

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: The report reaches a nuanced, evidence-qualified conclusion and supplies meaningful safeguards. It does not fully cover all requested conditioning variables, especially model capability, prompting, and mixture ratios.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The conclusion gives a conditional rather than categorical judgment: supplementation or targeted, curated, validated, distribution-matched use is more defensible than uncontrolled replacement.
  - The report conditions outcomes on recursion, curation, filtering, validation, source-model diversity, target-distribution alignment, and synthetic-to-real use patterns.
  - It acknowledges heterogeneous evidence and unresolved comparisons across pretraining, fine-tuning, domain adaptation, privacy, and evaluation methodology.
  - Practical safeguards include real-data baselines, subgroup testing, factuality and robustness checks, contamination and leakage controls, human validation, and realistic shifted-task tests.
- Missing:
  - Generator capability and target-model capability are not explicitly analyzed as interacting conditions.
  - Synthetic-to-real ratio, prompting strategy, and detailed training-regime choices are mentioned little or not at all.
  - Deployment decision criteria are sensible but remain a high-level checklist rather than a thresholded or decision-oriented framework.

### Novel Value

- The report synthesizes a useful conditional distinction between curated supplementation and recursive replacement rather than treating synthetic data as uniformly beneficial or harmful.
- It identifies evaluation failure as a central risk, including contamination, generator-aligned synthetic tests, distribution narrowing, self-preference, and intersectional disparities.
- It explicitly records unresolved evidence gaps, including the lack of controlled cross-regime comparisons and validated end-to-end evaluation methodology.

#### F5: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED

- Claim: Synthetic-data fine-tuning does not automatically remove foundation-model bias. Pre-existing biases may persist, while biases in fine-tuning material can be added or intensified; deliberate configuration of demographic perspectives is not evidence of improved fairness without outcome-level evaluation.
- Sources: S9, S12, S13
- Rationale: S9 directly supports the core claim that foundation-model biases persist after fine-tuning and may be extended by biases in fine-tuning materials. It also describes deliberately configuring models to reflect demographic characteristics, but this is presented as producing diverse or deliberately biased outputs—not as evidence of improved fairness—and the source notes that benchmarking does not address bias in training data or model responses. S12 supports that quality control, language bias, and distributional bias remain challenges in LLM-generated synthetic data and that fair representation requires careful control and filtering. S13 supports that synthetic fine-tuning can produce undesirable effects and that outcomes vary by data source, but its supplied text concerns distributional, adversarial-robustness, and self-preference biases rather than demographic fairness or intensified demographic bias. The specific claim that biases are “added or intensified” is therefore well supported by S9, while the stronger outcome-level-evaluation formulation is only indirectly supported.
- Supporting text: S9: “Biases built into a foundation model like an LLM or MM-LLM will still persist and may be extended by biases in the materials used for fine-tuning.” S9 also says customised models can be configured to reflect gender, age, and race and that benchmarking systems “do not address any of the issues caused by bias in the training data or in the responses.” S12 identifies persistent “quality control,” “language bias,” and “distributional bias” challenges and says fair representation requires careful control and post-generation filtering.

#### F6: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED

- Claim: Synthetic-data diversity is a material condition for safer LLM fine-tuning: multiple source models can mitigate distribution collapse and preserve broader output and linguistic diversity relative to single-source synthetic data, although downstream safety risks remain.
- Sources: S13
- Rationale: S13 directly supports that greater synthetic-data source diversity mitigates distribution collapse and preserves broader output and textual/linguistic diversity, and that synthetic fine-tuning can weaken safeguards or reduce adversarial robustness. However, it does not establish that diversity is a necessary or material condition for “safer” fine-tuning overall; the reported safety effects are mixed, and the supplied excerpt does not specifically state that multiple source models preserve linguistic diversity relative to single-source data in every respect.
- Supporting text: The abstract reports that synthetic data from diverse sources can mitigate distribution collapse, preserving the breadth of the output distribution and diversity of output text. The paper’s main findings also say higher source diversity mitigates collapse, while synthetic fine-tuning decreases adversarial robustness and can preserve output quality, potentially making outputs more usable and dangerous.

#### F7: PARTIALLY_SUPPORTED

- Claim: Benchmark gains from synthetic-data training are insufficient by themselves to establish real-world improvement. Evaluation should test generalization, factuality, robustness, safety, fairness, contamination, distribution shift, and realistic task performance.
- Sources: S5, S4, S1, S7, S9, S6, S13, S15
- Rationale: The sources strongly support the central point that benchmark gains alone do not establish real-world improvement: S5 says benchmarks can be saturated or contaminated and that models may perform well on standard benchmarks while behaving unreliably on complex real-world tasks; S13 likewise says performance benchmarks may miss output-distribution changes and possible robustness effects. The sources also support evaluating realistic task performance, safety, fairness or bias, contamination, robustness, and distributional effects. However, the full prescribed list is not uniformly supported in the supplied excerpts: factuality, generalization, and distribution shift are not all explicitly established as evaluation requirements across the cited material, and S4/S15 mainly discuss synthetic-data use and evaluation design rather than proving the entire claim.
- Supporting text: S5: “Determining whether an LLM is truly stronger is not as simple as looking at a few benchmark scores”; a model may score well on standard benchmarks yet behave unreliably in complex real-world tasks. S5 calls for evaluations closer to real applications while addressing contamination, fairness, reproducibility, and safety. S13 reports that benchmark performance may not capture output-distribution narrowing or adversarial robustness, and specifically studies susceptibility to jailbreaks and self-preference bias.

#### F8: SUPPORTED

- Claim: Synthetic test collections can introduce systematic evaluation bias: LLM-generated queries and relevance judgments may differ from human data and judgments, potentially overestimating absolute system performance even when relative comparisons are less affected.
- Sources: S11
- Rationale: The saved source directly supports all important elements of the claim. It reports systematic differences between synthetic and human queries, positive bias in LLM relevance judgments, overestimation of system performance, and a weaker effect when comparing relative system performance than when computing absolute performance.
- Supporting text: The source states that synthetic queries “tend to be longer” and have different vocabulary patterns, while GPT-4 relevance judgments score approximately 0.28 points higher than human assessments. It further reports “systematic overestimation of system performance” and says bias may be significant for absolute performance but less significant for comparing relative performance.
