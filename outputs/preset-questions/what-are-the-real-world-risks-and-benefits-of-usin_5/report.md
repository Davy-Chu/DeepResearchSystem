# Synthetic Data for Training and Fine-Tuning Large Language Models  
## Real-World benefits and risks, with emphasis on data quality, bias, and evaluation

## Executive summary

Synthetic data—text generated by humans or by language models and then used to train or fine-tune another model—can be highly useful, but its value depends on how it is generated, filtered, mixed with human data, and evaluated.

### Main benefits

1. **Scalable data creation:** It can produce large quantities of instructions, explanations, domain examples, and edge cases at substantially lower cost than manual annotation.
2. **Improved task coverage:** Synthetic generation can target underrepresented intents, languages, formats, safety scenarios, or rare failure cases.
3. **Privacy and security advantages:** Properly designed synthetic data can reduce exposure of personal or confidential information, especially for prototyping and supervised fine-tuning.
4. **Better supervision:** Model-generated rationales, critiques, preference comparisons, and tool-use traces can support instruction tuning and alignment.
5. **Controlled experimentation:** Researchers can systematically vary style, difficulty, demographics, or scenarios.

### Main risks

1. **Quality degradation:** Generated examples can contain factual errors, subtle logical mistakes, invalid code, unnatural language, or mislabeled outputs. These errors may be amplified during training.
2. **Bias amplification and homogenization:** A generator reflects its own training data, safety policies, and demographic or linguistic biases. Synthetic datasets can narrow diversity and overrepresent the generator’s worldview.
3. **Recursive-training degradation (“model collapse”):** Repeatedly training on model-generated outputs can cause loss of rare events, linguistic diversity, and calibration, particularly when original human data are progressively displaced.
4. **Evaluation contamination:** If synthetic training data are generated from benchmark questions or resemble benchmark distributions, reported performance can become inflated without corresponding real-world capability.
5. **False confidence:** Large synthetic datasets may create an appearance of breadth while containing repeated templates, correlated errors, and limited independent information.

### Bottom line

Synthetic data is generally safest and most effective as a **targeted supplement** to high-quality human or naturally occurring data—not as an unverified replacement for it. The strongest systems use:

- explicit provenance and generator tracking;
- human or trusted-model validation;
- deduplication and diversity controls;
- filtering for factuality, toxicity, privacy, and distributional imbalance;
- a mixture of human, synthetic, and real-world data;
- evaluation on fresh, held-out, human-authored, adversarial, and deployment-like datasets.

# 1. What counts as synthetic data?

“Synthetic data” covers several materially different practices:

| Type | Example | Principal value | Principal risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Synthetic instructions** | A model generates user questions and ideal answers | Inexpensive instruction tuning | Errors and repetitive templates |
| **Synthetic demonstrations** | Generated examples of classification, reasoning, or tool use | Expands task coverage | Demonstrations may encode incorrect procedures |
| **Synthetic preference data** | A model ranks two answers or writes critiques | Scales preference optimization | Generator’s preferences become the target |
| **Synthetic reasoning traces** | Generated chains of thought or explanations | May improve intermediate supervision | Plausible but invalid reasoning |
| **Synthetic conversations** | Simulated user-assistant dialogues | Supports dialogue and safety tuning | Unrealistic user behavior and conversational shortcuts |
| **Synthetic domain data** | Generated medical, legal, financial, or technical examples | Helps with low-resource domains | Hallucinated or legally/medically unsafe content |
| **Augmented natural data** | Human examples paraphrased or transformed by a model | Preserves an original task anchor | Semantic drift and distribution narrowing |
| **Generated test data** | Model-created adversarial prompts or edge cases | Finds vulnerabilities | May fail to represent real users and can leak test patterns |

Risk is therefore determined not merely by the percentage of synthetic examples, but by **what the examples are used for**, **whether their labels are independently checked**, and **how much independent human data remain in the training mixture**.

# 2. Real-world benefits

## 2.1 Lower cost and faster iteration

Human annotation is expensive, slow, and difficult to scale. Synthetic generation can produce:

- instruction-response pairs;
- domain-specific question-answer examples;
- negative examples;
- safety refusals;
- formatting transformations;
- multilingual variants;
- tool-use traces;
- preference comparisons.

This is particularly valuable when the target task has a clear specification and verification is feasible. For example, generated examples for SQL, code formatting, structured extraction, or arithmetic can often be automatically checked.

The major practical benefit is not simply “more tokens.” It is the ability to create **targeted supervision** for a specific capability or failure mode.

## 2.2 Data generation for low-resource domains and languages

Synthetic data can help where naturally occurring data are scarce, including:

- minority languages;
- specialized scientific or technical fields;
- rare safety incidents;
- unusual software libraries;
- new products or APIs;
- narrowly defined enterprise workflows.

However, this benefit is strongest when synthetic examples are anchored to reliable sources—such as verified documents, databases, APIs, or human-authored seed examples. Purely free-form generation is much less reliable.

## 2.3 Privacy and data minimization

Synthetic data may reduce the need to distribute raw personal or confidential records. It can be used to:

- prototype systems before access to production data;
- create test fixtures;
- share approximate statistical patterns;
- train models on transformed or de-identified examples;
- generate rare combinations that are difficult to collect safely.

But synthetic data is not automatically private. A model can memorize and reproduce training examples, particularly when prompted with unusual or identifying information. Synthetic data can also preserve sensitive attributes or enable re-identification when combined with external information.

Privacy claims therefore require empirical testing, such as:

- membership-inference testing;
- extraction testing;
- nearest-neighbor and memorization analysis;
- attack simulations;
- formal privacy guarantees where appropriate, such as differential privacy.

## 2.4 More balanced and controllable datasets

Synthetic generation can deliberately produce examples for:

- underrepresented demographic groups;
- rare intents;
- difficult levels of complexity;
- specific dialects or writing styles;
- safety-critical edge cases;
- counterfactual scenarios.

This provides a way to address gaps that occur naturally in data. For example, a classifier trained mostly on common requests may benefit from generated examples of uncommon but operationally important requests.

The danger is that “balancing” based on assumed categories may impose simplistic or inaccurate representations. Synthetic demographic variation can become stereotyped rather than genuinely diverse.

## 2.5 Improved alignment and instruction following

Several influential alignment methods use generated data in some form:

- **Self-Instruct** generated instruction-following examples using an existing language model.
- **Constitutional AI** used model-generated critiques and revisions guided by stated principles.
- **Distillation methods** use a stronger model to produce demonstrations for a smaller model.
- **Preference optimization** can use model-generated rankings or critiques when human preference labels are expensive.

These methods show that synthetic supervision can improve helpfulness, refusal behavior, formatting, and adherence to instructions. Yet the resulting model typically inherits the teacher’s omissions and preferences.

## 2.6 More systematic safety testing

Synthetic prompts can be generated to search for:

- jailbreaks;
- prompt-injection patterns;
- self-harm or violence scenarios;
- privacy attacks;
- discriminatory outputs;
- hallucination triggers;
- failures under long context;
- tool-use errors.

This is valuable because naturally occurring safety incidents are sparse and difficult to collect. Synthetic red-teaming is best treated as a **search and coverage tool**, not as a complete substitute for real-world incident data.

# 3. Data-quality risks

## 3.1 Plausibility is not correctness

Language models are optimized to produce likely continuations, not verified statements. Synthetic examples can therefore be fluent but wrong. Common errors include:

- fabricated facts and citations;
- incorrect medical, legal, or financial advice;
- invalid code;
- subtly false premises;
- inconsistent labels;
- contradictions across turns;
- incorrect reasoning with a correct final answer;
- unsafe or incomplete instructions.

Because these errors are often stylistically polished, automated quality checks based on fluency are weak.

## 3.2 Error amplification

If generated data are used as training targets, the model may learn the generator’s mistakes as authoritative. This is especially problematic when:

1. the generator is stronger than the student in language fluency but weaker in factual reliability;
2. generated labels are accepted without independent verification;
3. the same error appears repeatedly;
4. the training objective rewards imitation rather than truth;
5. the synthetic data dominate the mixture.

A mistaken answer in one example is not necessarily serious. A systematic pattern of mistaken answers can shift the model’s behavior.

## 3.3 Correlated errors and lack of independent information

Thousands of generated examples may not represent thousands of independent observations. They may share:

- the same model priors;
- the same prompt template;
- the same factual misconception;
- the same refusal policy;
- the same preferred writing style;
- the same blind spots.

This creates a form of **effective sample-size inflation**: the dataset looks large, but the independent information content is much smaller.

## 3.4 Template and style homogenization

Synthetic datasets often contain repetitive structures:

- similar openings and conclusions;
- standardized politeness;
- formulaic reasoning;
- overuse of bullet points;
- consistent verbosity;
- narrow cultural assumptions.

A model trained heavily on such data may become less adaptable to natural variation. This can affect:

- robustness to informal language;
- dialects and code-switching;
- user-generated text;
- domain-specific conventions;
- multilingual performance;
- creative or open-ended tasks.

## 3.5 Distribution mismatch

Synthetic examples may not resemble actual deployment inputs. Simulated users tend to be:

- more articulate;
- more cooperative;
- more explicit about their goals;
- less emotionally variable;
- less likely to make typos;
- less likely to use slang, code-switching, or incomplete context.

A model can perform well on synthetic test conversations and still fail in production.

## 3.6 Recursive training and model collapse

The most serious long-term concern is the repeated use of model-generated data to train later models. Research on model collapse suggests that recursive training can cause models to:

- lose low-frequency or “tail” events;
- overproduce common patterns;
- reduce diversity;
- amplify distributional distortions;
- forget details present in the original human data.

The mechanism is intuitive: each generation samples from a model’s already-smoothed approximation of the data distribution. Rare examples become less likely to be regenerated, so they disappear over successive cycles.

The severity depends on:

- whether original data are retained;
- the proportion of synthetic data;
- whether generation uses sampling or deterministic decoding;
- filtering and selection procedures;
- the diversity of generators;
- the task and model architecture.

Model collapse is not an argument against all synthetic data. It is an argument against **untracked, recursive replacement of human data with generated data**.

## 3.7 Data contamination and leakage

Synthetic data can accidentally include:

- benchmark questions;
- answers copied from public evaluation sets;
- proprietary documents;
- personal information;
- confidential prompts;
- generated text from prior model releases.

This can make training appear more effective than it is and can create legal, privacy, or intellectual-property problems.

# 4. Bias risks

## 4.1 Synthetic data inherits generator bias

A generator reflects:

- its pretraining corpus;
- its language and geographic distribution;
- its moderation policy;
- its commercial or institutional objectives;
- its representation of social groups;
- its assumptions about what constitutes a “good” answer.

When its outputs become training data, these biases can be transferred to another model, sometimes more strongly because the outputs are presented as curated examples.

## 4.2 Bias amplification through selection

Bias is affected not only by generation but also by filtering. For example, a quality filter may preferentially retain:

- standard written language;
- dominant dialects;
- culturally familiar examples;
- majority-group names or contexts;
- responses that conform to one institution’s notion of politeness.

A dataset can therefore become more biased even if the generator itself is not overtly discriminatory.

## 4.3 Stereotype reinforcement

Prompts asking for “diverse examples” can elicit shallow or stereotyped variation. Common failures include:

- associating occupations with gender or ethnicity;
- depicting certain groups primarily through risk, poverty, or crime;
- treating minority dialects as errors;
- representing cultures as homogeneous;
- reducing disability or illness to a narrow set of narratives.

Synthetic augmentation can increase the number of biased examples while making the dataset appear balanced numerically.

## 4.4 Majority-language and cultural dominance

A model trained on synthetic translations or multilingual generations may produce text that is grammatically acceptable but culturally unnatural. It may also erase:

- regional vocabulary;
- dialect distinctions;
- indigenous concepts;
- local legal or medical practices;
- culturally specific conversational norms.

This is especially consequential when synthetic data are used for languages with limited original training data. The resulting model may be “fluent” but not representative.

## 4.5 Bias in preference and alignment data

If a model generates preference labels, it defines what is considered:

- helpful;
- harmless;
- respectful;
- persuasive;
- politically acceptable;
- appropriately cautious.

These judgments may diverge from users’ preferences or from the requirements of a specific community. Synthetic preference data can create over-refusal, paternalism, excessive hedging, or systematic suppression of legitimate viewpoints.

## 4.6 Feedback loops in deployed systems

A particularly concerning pattern is:

1. a model generates content;
2. that content enters search results, social media, customer-service logs, or educational materials;
3. future datasets collect it;
4. a later model is trained on it;
5. the model’s original biases become more prevalent.

This is a real-world version of recursive data contamination. Provenance and source weighting become important as AI-generated content becomes widespread.

# 5. Evaluation challenges

## 5.1 Standard benchmark scores may overstate gains

Synthetic data can improve benchmark performance through:

- direct or near-direct contamination;
- generation of examples similar to the benchmark format;
- optimization toward known benchmark styles;
- teacher knowledge of common test distributions;
- evaluation on synthetic data that resembles the training data.

A reported score increase is meaningful only if evaluated on data that are:

- held out from generation and training;
- independently authored;
- contamination-checked;
- representative of deployment;
- sufficiently difficult and diverse.

## 5.2 Evaluator models share biases with generator models

Using another language model as the sole judge creates several problems:

- generator and evaluator may share factual errors;
- both may reward verbosity and polished style;
- evaluator preferences may reflect the same cultural or political assumptions;
- models may be poor at detecting subtle logical or factual errors;
- “judge hacking” can optimize outputs for the evaluator rather than users.

LLM-as-judge methods are useful for scalable screening, but high-stakes evaluation should include human experts, executable checks, and task-specific measurements.

## 5.3 Synthetic tests may be too easy

A model can perform well on generated questions because they contain:

- explicit instructions;
- clean formatting;
- obvious expected answers;
- limited ambiguity;
- familiar phrasing;
- no adversarial or emotional context.

Real users generate underspecified, contradictory, noisy, and context-dependent requests. Evaluation should therefore include real interaction logs where legally and ethically usable, as well as human-authored adversarial examples.

## 5.4 Capability versus imitation

Fine-tuning on synthetic demonstrations may improve imitation of a particular answer style without improving underlying capability. This can manifest as:

- better formatting but no better factuality;
- longer explanations but no better reasoning;
- more confident answers without improved calibration;
- benchmark gains without transfer to novel tasks;
- improved refusal language without improved safety judgment.

Evaluation must distinguish **surface compliance** from actual task performance.

## 5.5 Hidden distributional regressions

Aggregate accuracy can improve while performance worsens for:

- minority dialects;
- low-frequency intents;
- long-tail entities;
- non-English languages;
- users with disabilities;
- informal or adversarial inputs;
- high-stakes domains.

Evaluation should report disaggregated results, not just overall averages.

# 6. What the research suggests

Several strands of research inform current understanding:

### Synthetic instruction tuning

**Self-Instruct** demonstrated that a model can generate instruction-following data and use them to improve another model’s instruction following. This supports the scalability argument, but the method also relies on filtering and seed-task quality.

### Distillation and model-generated supervision

Work such as **Alpaca**, **Vicuna**, **Orca**, and related distillation approaches showed that relatively small amounts of model-generated instruction data can produce substantial behavioral improvements. These results also illustrate the danger of conflating imitation of a strong teacher with independent reasoning or factual reliability.

### Constitutional and critique-based methods

**Constitutional AI** showed that models can generate critiques and revisions under explicit principles, reducing the need for some human preference labels. The approach transfers the chosen constitution—and its ambiguities—into the training process.

### Model collapse

**Shumailov et al.** and subsequent work analyzed how recursive training on generated distributions can eliminate low-probability modes and degrade diversity. Later studies have found that preserving original data and controlling synthetic-data mixtures can mitigate, though not necessarily eliminate, the problem.

### Data quality and scaling

Research on data curation, deduplication, and “data cascades” indicates that dataset quality and provenance can matter as much as raw quantity. Synthetic data can be beneficial when it increases task-relevant information, but redundant or noisy data can produce diminishing returns or harm.

### Bias and fairness

Fairness research consistently finds that representation, labeling, and evaluation choices affect model behavior. Synthetic examples can improve subgroup coverage, but only when the target groups and scenarios are defined carefully and outputs are checked by people with relevant cultural or domain knowledge.

# 7. A practical risk-benefit framework

## 7.1 When synthetic data is relatively safe

Synthetic data is lower risk when:

- outputs have objectively verifiable answers;
- examples are generated from authoritative sources;
- the generator is used for augmentation, not replacement;
- independent validators can check correctness;
- the task is low stakes;
- data provenance is recorded;
- performance is tested on fresh human-authored data.

Examples include:

- code transformations with unit tests;
- schema-constrained extraction;
- format conversion;
- synthetic API calls validated against a sandbox;
- arithmetic and symbolic tasks with exact checking.

## 7.2 When caution is required

Risk increases when data concern:

- medicine, law, finance, or safety;
- identity, reputation, or employment;
- social or political judgments;
- mental health;
- multilingual or culturally specific communication;
- open-ended factual knowledge;
- model-generated preference labels;
- rare or vulnerable populations.

In these settings, synthetic examples should generally require expert review, robust provenance, and explicit uncertainty handling.

## 7.3 When synthetic data should not be the primary source

It is particularly risky to rely mainly on synthetic data for:

- factual pretraining corpora;
- representations of real human experience;
- socially sensitive classification;
- high-stakes advice;
- estimating real-world prevalence;
- evaluating fairness or safety;
- replacing naturally occurring long-tail data.

Synthetic data cannot reliably tell an organization how people actually behave unless it has been calibrated against real observations.

# 8. Recommended production practices

## 8.1 Maintain provenance

For every example, record:

- generator model and version;
- prompt and system instructions;
- decoding parameters;
- generation date;
- source documents or seed examples;
- transformations and filters;
- human or automated validation status;
- whether the example was used for training, validation, or testing.

Do not mix synthetic and human data invisibly.

## 8.2 Use source mixtures

Preserve a meaningful amount of:

- human-authored data;
- naturally occurring user data, subject to privacy and consent requirements;
- expert-authored examples;
- independently collected edge cases.

Track the synthetic proportion by task, language, demographic category, and training stage.

## 8.3 Validate at multiple levels

### Example-level checks

- factuality;
- label correctness;
- toxicity and safety;
- privacy leakage;
- instruction validity;
- internal consistency;
- citation or source verification.

### Dataset-level checks

- deduplication;
- template diversity;
- lexical and semantic diversity;
- subgroup balance;
- language and dialect coverage;
- source concentration;
- generator concentration;
- distribution relative to deployment traffic.

### Model-level checks

- capability;
- calibration;
- hallucination rate;
- refusal accuracy;
- subgroup performance;
- robustness to paraphrase;
- out-of-distribution generalization;
- memorization and extraction risk.

## 8.4 Use independent evaluation data

A strong evaluation set should be:

- created independently of the synthetic generator;
- held out before training;
- partly human-authored;
- refreshed over time;
- difficult to infer from public benchmark distributions;
- representative of actual users and failure modes.

For high-stakes systems, combine:

- automatic metrics;
- exact-match or executable tests;
- expert review;
- adversarial testing;
- user studies;
- incident monitoring after deployment.

## 8.5 Test for bias rather than assuming balance

Measure performance and error types across relevant groups and contexts. Include:

- dialect and language variation;
- names and demographic descriptors;
- culturally specific scenarios;
- intersectional groups;
- ambiguous cases;
- counterfactual tests;
- false-positive and false-negative disparities;
- differences in refusal and helpfulness rates.

Numerical balance in the synthetic dataset is not evidence of fair representation.

## 8.6 Prevent recursive contamination

Organizations should:

- label AI-generated content in internal repositories;
- avoid feeding unverified model outputs back into training;
- retain original human data;
- weight synthetic examples by confidence and validation quality;
- use multiple independent generators where appropriate;
- periodically compare against pre-synthetic baselines;
- monitor rare-event and tail-performance retention.

## 8.7 Evaluate the generator, not just the student

Before using a teacher model, test it for:

- factual accuracy;
- demographic bias;
- calibration;
- refusal behavior;
- multilingual quality;
- contamination;
- privacy leakage;
- consistency across prompts and sampling settings.

A weak or biased generator can produce a large, polished, and systematically defective dataset.

# 9. Suggested evaluation protocol

A practical evaluation program can follow these stages:

### Stage 1: Baseline

Train or fine-tune using only the human/natural dataset. Record:

- overall task performance;
- subgroup metrics;
- calibration;
- factuality;
- robustness;
- safety;
- compute and annotation cost.

### Stage 2: Controlled synthetic addition

Add synthetic data in several proportions, such as:

- 10%;
- 25%;
- 50%;
- higher proportions only if justified.

Compare both average performance and tail performance.

### Stage 3: Ablation

Separate the effects of:

- synthetic instructions;
- synthetic answers;
- synthetic critiques;
- synthetic preference labels;
- paraphrased human data;
- model-generated edge cases.

This identifies which synthetic component creates benefit or harm.

### Stage 4: Independent evaluation

Use a fresh evaluation set containing:

- human-authored examples;
- real or realistically sampled user inputs;
- adversarial cases;
- low-frequency cases;
- multilingual and dialectal examples;
- expert-verified high-stakes examples.

### Stage 5: Contamination and memorization testing

Check:

- n-gram and semantic overlap;
- benchmark exposure;
- extraction under prompting;
- membership inference;
- repeated-template memorization.

### Stage 6: Deployment monitoring

Track:

- user-reported errors;
- subgroup-specific incidents;
- changes in refusal behavior;
- hallucination rates;
- unusual confidence;
- drift toward synthetic writing styles;
- performance on newly collected human examples.

---

# 10. Overall assessment

Synthetic data offers a powerful way to expand supervision, reduce annotation costs, target rare cases, and improve alignment. Its benefits are most reliable when generation is constrained by authoritative sources and outputs can be independently verified.

The greatest risks arise when synthetic data are treated as interchangeable with human data. Generated examples are not independent observations, and their fluency can conceal factual errors, stereotypes, and narrow assumptions. Repeated training on generated content can also erase low-frequency information and make models increasingly resemble their own prior outputs.

The central operational principle is:

> **Use synthetic data to fill known gaps, not to obscure unknown ones.**

In practice, this means preserving human and naturally occurring data, recording provenance, validating generated content, testing subgroup and long-tail performance, and evaluating on fresh human-authored and deployment-like data. Synthetic data should be viewed as a controllable instrument for data improvement—not as a universally safe substitute for real-world evidence.
