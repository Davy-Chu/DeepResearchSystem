## Comprehensiveness

### R1

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: The report substantially addresses arrangements and measurement heterogeneity, but the productivity construct and its distinction from related outcomes could be more explicit and systematic.
- Candidate evidence:
  - The report distinguishes structured hybrid work from fully remote work and office-only comparisons: the Trip.com trial compared two work-from-home days with office-only work, while the Ctrip study compared fully remote and office workers.
  - It distinguishes measures including calls, hours worked, code output, performance reviews, promotions, innovation, self-reported productivity, and industry-level total-factor productivity.
  - It notes that remote-work studies can conflate fully remote, hybrid, and office-based arrangements and that productivity is not a single metric.
- Missing:
  - The distinction between productivity and related outcomes such as satisfaction, retention, and career progression is not developed systematically; retention is reported as a benefit but not clearly separated from productivity throughout.
  - The report does not clearly explain how output per hour differs from output per employee or how quality-adjusted output affects interpretation.

### R2

- Coverage: 0.75
- Depth: 0.75
- Rationale: The report provides meaningful causal evidence, treatment comparisons, populations, outcomes, directions, and some magnitudes and limits. It falls short of a fully developed evaluation because precision and study-design details are incomplete.
- Candidate evidence:
  - It describes the randomized six-month Trip.com trial of 1,612 employees, including the two-days-remote treatment and office-only comparison.
  - It reports no measurable difference in performance reviews, promotions, innovation, development, leadership, code output, and other business measures, while noting approximately one-third lower quit rates.
  - It describes the reported Ctrip randomized call-center experiment with 249 employees and a 13.5% increase in calls for remote workers.
  - It explicitly limits generalization beyond the firms, schedules, occupations, countries, and contexts studied and notes that some older fully remote results rely on secondary summaries.
- Missing:
  - The experimental evidence is not presented with enough detail on statistical precision or uncertainty, such as confidence intervals or whether reported differences were statistically distinguishable from zero.
  - The Ctrip experiment's treatment duration, exact comparison design, and limitations are less fully described than the Trip.com trial.
  - The report does not fully distinguish measured calls from broader productivity or quality outcomes in the Ctrip case.

- Depth: 0.50

### Novel Value

- The report offers a useful conditional synthesis separating structured hybrid work from fully remote work instead of treating remote work as one treatment.
- It appropriately emphasizes that the strongest causal evidence concerns a specific hybrid schedule and does not generalize that result directly to fully remote work.
- Its main limitation is that much of the fully remote and observational evidence is drawn from secondary summaries rather than detailed primary-study evaluation.
